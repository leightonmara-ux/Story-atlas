## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*Prometheus* (2012) was directed by Ridley Scott from a screenplay by Jon Spaihts and Damon Lindelof, and set in the same universe as Scott's *Alien* (1979). In 2089 two archaeologists, Elizabeth Shaw (Noomi Rapace) and Charlie Holloway (Logan Marshall-Green), find the same star map painted in ancient sites around the world, and read it as an invitation from humanity's makers. The dying industrialist Peter Weyland (Guy Pearce) funds an expedition, and the ship *Prometheus* reaches a distant moon in 2093, carrying a crew, a corporate overseer, Meredith Vickers (Charlize Theron), and an android, David (Michael Fassbender). What they find is not a welcome but a weapons installation. This case study uses the film honestly, for what it gets right and for what it gets wrong.

## Why it works

### Big questions, built into the design

The film's ambitions are enormous, and they're stated in its images before anyone speaks. The opening shows a pale, giant humanoid alone beside a waterfall on a barren planet. He drinks a dark liquid, his body breaks apart, and his DNA falls into the water: life seeded by a sacrifice. The film never fully explains it, and doesn't need to.

*Light, colour and setting* teaches that a good setting embodies the conflict, and the production design does exactly that. The alien structure on the moon is vast, dark and organic, its walls ribbed like a body; it echoes H. R. Giger's designs for *Alien*, so the audience senses the danger before the characters do. Against it, the human ship is clean, bright and full of glass. The film's question, what do creators owe the things they make, and what do the made owe their makers, is in the contrast between the two.

### David: an introduction that tells us everything

The best writing in the film is David's introduction, near the start. While the crew lies in hypersleep for the two-year voyage, the android has the ship to himself. We watch him shoot baskets while riding a bicycle, study languages, look in on Shaw's dreams, and watch *Lawrence of Arabia* (1962) over and over, copying Peter O'Toole's hair and lines.

*Introducing a character* says that the first action is a thesis, and here the thesis is precise. David is alone, curious, imitative and slightly unsettling: a made thing studying how to be a person, and longing for something he can't name. Without a word of exposition we know he will be the film's most interesting mind.

The film's best dialogue builds on it. When Holloway, drunk and disappointed, tells David that humans made him simply because they could, David asks how Holloway would feel to hear the same answer from his own creator. That exchange is the theme in two lines. *Whose side are we on?* explains why this works: we're aligned with David more than anyone, and Fassbender makes him the character we watch even as we stop trusting him.

### The medpod: a set piece built on a want

About two-thirds of the way in, Shaw, who has been told she cannot have children, discovers she is carrying something not human, conceived only hours earlier with Holloway, whom David had secretly infected. David wants to keep it frozen for study. She escapes to Vickers's automated surgical pod, only to find it calibrated for male patients. She improvises, orders surgery for a foreign body in the abdomen, and the machine cuts her open while she is awake.

It works because everything in it is a want under pressure. Shaw wants to live; the machine's limitation is an obstacle; she finds a way around it; and the stakes are her body and her faith. Spaihts has said a version of the sequence went back to his early drafts. It's also the film's best use of *Genre as a promise*: horror, in Noël Carroll's sense, is disgust at something that breaks our categories, and here that something is inside the heroine.

### Where it fails: decisions without motivation

The film's most famous problem is that its characters, supposedly the best scientists on Earth, behave in ways that make little sense. Holloway takes off his helmet in an alien building because the air reads as breathable. A geologist who has been mapping the structure with laser drones gets lost in it. A biologist stranded overnight in the structure coos at a snake-like creature and reaches out to it. After the medpod scene, Shaw walks, stapled, into a room full of people who barely react. Late on, Vickers and Shaw flee a giant ship rolling towards them by running in a straight line in front of it.

*Want, need and wound* explains the underlying fault. A character's choices have to come from what they want, under pressure, and what wounds them. The film supplies wounds: Shaw's faith, inherited from a father she lost as a child, and her infertility; Weyland's terror of death; Vickers's resentment of her father. But too many decisions come from what the plot needs to happen next rather than from those wants. When a choice has no motivation inside the character, the audience stops believing the scene and starts noticing the writers.

### Too many people to care about

*Making us care* says that identification has to be built early. *Prometheus* carries well over a dozen people, and most of them get little more than a job title before the danger starts. Idris Elba's captain, Janek, makes a strong late impression, with a dry wit and a final sacrifice, but most of the crew die before we know who they are, so the deaths register as spectacle rather than loss. Compare *Alien*, with seven crew who are given time to bicker over pay before anything goes wrong. Even Holloway, whose fate should be the emotional centre of the middle act, is drawn so thinly that his death matters more for what it does to Shaw than for himself.

## The shape

The film follows the horror pattern Noël Carroll calls the complex discovery plot (see *Genre as a promise*): onset, discovery, confirmation, confrontation. Positions are approximate.

| Stage | What happens | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| Prologue | The Engineer at the waterfall; the cave painting in 2089 | Opening minutes |
| Setup | David alone on the ship; the crew wakes; Weyland's hologram briefing | To about a sixth |
| Discovery | The first trip into the structure; the dead Engineers; the black liquid | About a sixth to a third |
| Onset | David infects Holloway; two crew stranded in the structure overnight | About a third to half |
| Confirmation | Holloway sickens and dies; Shaw's pregnancy; the medpod | About half to two-thirds |
| Confrontation | Weyland revealed on board; the living Engineer wakes and attacks | About two-thirds to five-sixths |
| Resolution | Janek rams the Engineer's ship; Shaw leaves with David's head | Final sixth |

The confirmation stage is where the plot frays. Carroll's model depends on characters who must be persuaded the impossible is real. Here they accept it, or ignore it, almost at once, so the middle of the film has nobody to persuade and little suspense.

## What people said

**The makers.** Jon Spaihts's original draft, titled *Alien: Engineers*, was a much more direct prequel to *Alien*, with familiar creatures. Damon Lindelof was brought in to rewrite it, and he has said he kept much of Spaihts's script, including David and the opening, while pulling the story away from the earlier film toward its own mythology. After release, Lindelof answered criticism in several interviews. In a 2012 conversation with the *Wall Street Journal*, he argued that some of the supposed mistakes were deliberate or were lines he hadn't written, and said that criticism of an open-ended story was what he had signed up for. In a later email Q&A with MTV, he insisted there were answers to the film's central mysteries, even if the film didn't give them.

**The critics.** Roger Ebert gave it four stars in 2012, praising it as a magnificent science-fiction film that raises questions about where life comes from and is wise enough not to answer them. Fassbender's performance was the most consistently praised element across reviews. Others were harder. *Time Out*'s review called the dialogue lazy and said the plot, crammed with striking concepts, failed to come together, and many reviewers and viewers singled out the scientists' choices as the film's weak point. The two verdicts are not really opposed: the ideas and the design are first-rate, and the people carrying them are not.

## Watch for

- **The waterfall** (opening minutes): a whole theme stated without a word.
- **David alone** (first tenth or so): every activity is a clue to who he is.
- **The holographic Weyland** (about a sixth in): what he says about David, and what he leaves out.
- **The helmet** (about a quarter in): Holloway's decision, and whether anything in him motivates it.
- **"Because we could"** (about a third in): the exchange between Holloway and David.
- **The medpod** (about two-thirds in): count the obstacles, and how each is overcome.
- **The aftermath** (just after): how the other characters react to Shaw, and what that does to belief.
- **The last scene** (final minutes): Shaw's choice to keep searching, and the creature that hatches after she leaves.

## Try it

Audit your characters' decisions for motivation. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Pick the five biggest decisions in your work-in-progress, the moments where a character chooses something that moves the plot.
2. For each, write one sentence: what the character wants at that moment, and what in their past makes this choice feel right to them.
3. If you can't write the sentence, or the honest answer is "because the plot needs it", rewrite the decision, or add a beat earlier that makes it make sense.

- **Stage:** Give each key decision a visible moment of hesitation. What does the actor need to have seen or heard just before?
- **Screen:** For one decision, write a short earlier scene that plants the want or the wound, as David's introduction plants everything we need to understand his later choices.
- **Journalism:** When a subject made a surprising choice, find out what they believed or feared at the time, and put it before the choice in your piece, not after.

**Check:** Could a sceptical reader explain each decision from the character's point of view, without mentioning the plot?
