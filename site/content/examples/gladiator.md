## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*Gladiator* (2000) was directed by Ridley Scott from a screenplay credited to David Franzoni, John Logan and William Nicholson, from a story by Franzoni, with a score by Hans Zimmer and Lisa Gerrard. In 180 AD the Roman general Maximus (Russell Crowe) wants only to go home to his farm in Spain, but the dying emperor Marcus Aurelius (Richard Harris) asks him to take power and return it to the Senate. The emperor's son Commodus (Joaquin Phoenix) murders his father, seizes the throne and has Maximus's wife and son killed; Maximus is sold into slavery, becomes a gladiator, and fights his way to the Colosseum and a final duel with the emperor. It won five Academy Awards, including Best Picture and Best Actor.

## Why it works

### A want and a duty, pulling apart

The film opens on a hand drifting over ripe wheat, in warm light. Then it cuts to a grey, frozen German forest, where Maximus waits for the last battle of a long war. Before a word is spoken, the film has set up two worlds: the one he wants and the one he serves.

*Want, need and wound* describes the classic design in which the want and the need pull in opposite directions. *Gladiator* builds its version early, about a sixth of the way in. Maximus's want is concrete: home, his wife, his son, the harvest. He describes his farm to Marcus Aurelius in loving detail. (Crowe has said he improvised part of this, describing his own home in Australia.) Marcus answers with a duty: he asks Maximus to become protector of Rome after his death and hand power back to the Senate. Maximus asks for time. It's *Joseph Campbell & Christopher Vogler*'s Refusal of the Call, and it's a sympathetic one, because what he wants is so modest.

Then the film destroys the want. Commodus kills his father, Maximus refuses him loyalty and escapes execution, and he rides home to find his wife and son murdered. He buries them himself. From here, about a quarter in, his want is vengeance, and the duty he refused comes back to him in the arena.

### The fall, then the rise

The middle of the film is a classic **Man in a Hole**, the shape *Kurt Vonnegut* drew as a fall followed by a rise. Maximus begins at the top: a general, the emperor's favourite. In the space of one sequence he loses rank, family and freedom, and wakes up as property in a North African slave market.

The rise is built in clear, countable steps, each one in a bigger arena. In a provincial arena in Zucchabar, about two-fifths in, he kills his opponents with contemptuous speed, hurls his sword into the stands and shouts at the silent crowd, "Are you not entertained?" It's the film's statement of theme: Maximus despises the crowd, and the crowd is exactly what he will have to win. Proximo, his owner (Oliver Reed), tells him so: Proximo was once a gladiator himself, freed by Marcus Aurelius, and his advice is to win the crowd.

In the Colosseum, about halfway, Maximus leads a doomed band of gladiators in a staged re-enactment of a Roman victory and turns it into a win. Commodus comes down to congratulate the masked gladiator, and Maximus takes off his helmet and names himself, his rank, his murdered family and his promise of vengeance. The crowd won't let Commodus kill him. A fall built in one sequence; a rise built in three arenas, each with a larger audience.

### Commodus: the hero's dark twin

*The cast as a system* says the strongest antagonist wants the same thing as the hero. Commodus wants what Maximus has without trying: his father's love, the army's loyalty and, later, the people's devotion. Marcus has chosen Maximus over his own son, and the scene in which Commodus learns it, weeping that he has never been good enough before embracing and smothering his father, makes him a wounded son as well as a villain.

That's why he's frightening rather than cartoonish. Each of his scenes shows a need for love curdling into control: his possessiveness towards his sister Lucilla (Connie Nielsen), his threats against her young son, his attempts to win the crowd with games. In the arena, he is the emperor who has to give the thumbs-up to the man he hates because the people demand it. Phoenix ad-libbed one of his most famous lines, "Am I not merciful?", according to accounts of the shoot, and the line sums him up: he wants credit for virtues he can't feel.

The two men are a pair of sons in a story about fathers. Maximus is the son Marcus wished he had; Commodus is the son he got. *Whose side are we on?* explains how films adjust our sympathy, and here the alignment is mostly with Maximus, but Phoenix keeps tipping us towards pity.

### The supporting cast as a ladder

The secondary characters give Maximus a series of different relationships to the arena. Proximo is the cynic who once had what Maximus had (freedom granted by Marcus) and sold his skill for money; by the end he gives his life to help Maximus escape. Juba (Djimon Hounsou) is the friend who shares Maximus's belief in an afterlife where families wait. Lucilla is the lost love who connects him back to politics. Gracchus (Derek Jacobi), the senator, represents the Rome Marcus wanted to restore. *Memorable side characters* calls this the side character who changes the hero: Proximo, especially, turns from trader to protector.

### The wheat field and the afterlife

The film's spine is a motif, not a plot point. Maximus carries two small carved figures of his wife and son, and prays to them. His speech to his cavalry before the opening battle tells them that if they find themselves riding alone through green fields in the sun, they shouldn't be troubled, because they'll be in Elysium and already dead. The image of fields, and of a path home, keeps returning, in flashes and visions, desaturated and dreamlike.

William Nicholson, brought in to rewrite the script, has said he developed the afterlife thread and the friendship with Juba, because he didn't want to see a film about a man who only wanted to kill somebody. It matters structurally: revenge alone ends when Commodus dies, but the afterlife motif gives Maximus somewhere to go.

In the final duel, about the last tenth, Commodus stabs him secretly before they enter the arena. Maximus kills him anyway, asks that the gladiators be freed and Gracchus restored, and dies. As he does, we see the wheat field again, his hand passing over the heads of grain, and his wife and son waiting on the path. That night Juba buries the two small figures in the sand where Maximus died. The opening image has become the ending: home, reached at last, beyond the end of the story. *Music and silence* explains how a recurring musical idea can carry this kind of meaning, and here Lisa Gerrard's wordless vocal, heard over the visions, does the work. The film tells us how to feel about death before it shows us one.

## The shape

Vonnegut's Man in a Hole fits the film's fortunes, with one change: the hero dies at the top of the rise. Vogler's stages map onto the same line.

| Stage | What happens | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary World | The wheat field; the German battle; the hero at the top | Opening tenth |
| Call and Refusal | Marcus asks Maximus to protect Rome; Maximus asks for time | About a sixth |
| The fall | Marcus murdered; Maximus's family killed; slavery | A fifth to a third |
| Tests, Allies, Enemies | Zucchabar: Proximo, Juba, "Are you not entertained?" | A third to two-fifths |
| Ordeal and Reward | The Colosseum; the helmet removed; the crowd saves him | About half |
| Road Back | Tigris of Gaul; the plot with Lucilla and Gracchus; Proximo's death | Half to five-sixths |
| Resurrection | The duel; Commodus killed; Maximus dies and goes home | Final sixth |

## What people said

**The makers** describe a hard shoot. Franzoni wrote the first draft, inspired by Daniel P. Mannix's 1958 novel *Those About to Die*. Scott then brought in John Logan, who rewrote much of the first act and made the decision to kill Maximus's family, giving him a sharper reason for vengeance. Nicholson's rewrite followed. Crowe told *Inside the Actors Studio* that filming began with only a few dozen usable pages, and the script kept changing through the shoot, with Scott taking input from writers, producers and actors; a DreamWorks executive later said Crowe tried to rewrite much of it himself, though a producer has since called those stories exaggerated.

**Oliver Reed** died of a heart attack in Malta on 2 May 1999, during a break in filming, before his scenes were complete. The script was revised so that Proximo dies in the story, and his remaining scenes were finished with a body double and Reed's face composited digitally from other footage. The film is dedicated to him.

**The critics** were split between the spectacle and the script. Kenneth Turan in the *Los Angeles Times* (2000) praised the physical and psychological reality Crowe gave the role, and many reviewers admired the opening battle and Zimmer and Gerrard's score. Ian Nathan in *Empire* found the dialogue pompous and overwritten. Roger Ebert, in the *Chicago Sun-Times* (2000), was sharper about the script:

> employs depression as a substitute for personality
> — Roger Ebert, *Chicago Sun-Times* (2000)

**The historians** pointed out that Marcus Aurelius died of disease, not murder; that he had shared power with Commodus for years and had no plan to restore the Republic; and that Commodus ruled for twelve years and was strangled in his bath by a wrestler, not killed in the arena. Scott hired historical advisers, but at least one resigned over the changes. Others noted that the plot owes a lot to Anthony Mann's *The Fall of the Roman Empire* (1964), which tells the same succession story.

## Watch for

- **The hand in the wheat** (first minute): the shot before the story starts, and the colour of it.
- **The cavalry speech** (opening battle): the line about the green fields and Elysium, which sets up the ending.
- **Marcus and Maximus** (about a sixth in): Maximus describing his home. Listen to how specific it is.
- **Commodus and his father** (about a fifth in): the embrace that becomes a murder.
- **Zucchabar** (about two-fifths in): the thrown sword, the silent crowd, and then the roar.
- **The helmet** (about halfway): Maximus naming himself, and Commodus's face as he does.
- **Proximo's death** (about five-sixths in): a death written into the story after Reed died during the shoot.
- **The ending** (final minutes): the wheat returns, and Juba buries the figures in the sand.

## Try it

Write a story's first and last images so that they are the same image, changed. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Choose a character who wants something modest and concrete: a place, a person, a routine. Write a one-paragraph opening image of that want, with no dialogue.
2. List three ways the story could take it away. Pick the one the character least deserves.
3. Write the closing image: the same place or object, but now its meaning has changed because of what the character went through. Keep it wordless.

- **Stage:** Use one prop for both images (a figure, a bowl, a handful of earth), handled differently in the last scene.
- **Screen:** Script both as single shots with the same framing. Note one detail that differs.
- **Journalism:** Open and close a feature at the same place (a farm, a kitchen, a street corner). Revisit it at the end and report honestly what has changed and what hasn't.

**Check:** Could someone who saw only your first and last images tell what the story was about?
