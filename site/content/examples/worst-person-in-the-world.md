## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*The Worst Person in the World* (*Verdens verste menneske*, 2021) was directed by Joachim Trier and written by Trier and his long-time collaborator Eskil Vogt. It completes Trier's "Oslo trilogy", after *Reprise* (2006) and *Oslo, August 31st* (2011), and like them it stars Anders Danielsen Lie. Julie (Renate Reinsve) is a gifted young woman approaching thirty who keeps changing course: from medicine to psychology to photography, and from one man to another. Over roughly four years, told in a prologue, twelve titled chapters and an epilogue, she lives with Aksel (Lie), an older, successful comic-book artist, falls for Eivind (Herbert Nordrum), a café worker her own age, and finally has to face what she has chosen and what she hasn't.

## Why it works

### Chapters: the telling chooses its own speed

The film announces its shape. After a brisk prologue, each section opens on a title card, and the titles are wry, sometimes comic, like chapter headings in a novel that is slightly amused by its heroine (one is "Oral Sex in the Age of #MeToo"). Between chapters, time simply jumps: weeks or months vanish in a cut, and we catch up from clues.

This is the gap between events and their telling that *Story vs. telling* is about. The fabula is several unremarkable years in a young woman's life. The syuzhet picks a dozen moments and drops the rest. The prologue runs at the speed of **summary**, galloping through Julie's changes of subject, hair and boyfriend in a few minutes; the chapters slow down to **scene**, sometimes for a single evening; and the cuts between them are **ellipses**. The chapter cards make the ellipses honest. The film admits it is choosing, and that admission becomes its tone: episodic, provisional, like Julie herself.

### A narrator who knows more than Julie does

Much of the film comes with a woman's voice-over: third person, calm, a little ironic. It tells us what Julie is thinking, sometimes more crisply than she could put it, and it summarises what she would rather skip. *Point of view and voice* gives the tools for this. The narrator is close to Julie but not identical with her, so the film can be fond of her and see through her at the same moment. When the voice-over explains a decision, we often hear the self-justification inside the explanation.

The narration also widens the frame beyond anything Julie could know. At her thirtieth birthday, the voice-over steps back through the women in her family line, noting what each had already lived through by the same age: marriages, children, early deaths. It turns one woman's dithering into a question about history: what is thirty for, now that the old answers have gone? Only a narrator with a wider view than Julie's could do that.

### Stopping the clock

The film's most famous sequence comes in the kitchen one morning with Aksel. Julie flicks a light switch and the world stops: Aksel freezes mid-gesture, and outside, all of Oslo is still, people caught in mid-stride, cars halted. Only Julie moves. She runs through the stopped city to the café where Eivind works, and the two of them spend a stolen day together while everyone else waits. Then she goes home, and time starts again.

*Story vs. telling* lists **pause** among the speeds of narrative: the events stop while the telling continues. Here pause is made literal and visible, and it shows exactly what Julie wants: not to choose, not to hurt anyone, to have the new love without paying for the old one. Once the clock restarts she has to act on it, and soon after she tells Aksel she is leaving. Eskil Vogt has said that this image was one of the first ideas he and Trier had, before they had a story at all: the film grew around a picture of wanting time to wait.

### Meeting Eivind: an introduction by rules

*Introducing a character* says that a first action is a thesis. Eivind's introduction is a whole sequence. Julie walks out of a launch party for Aksel's book, wanders into a wedding party she wasn't invited to, and meets him. Both are attached to someone else, so they make a game of it: they will not cheat. Then they test how far that rule can bend, sharing secrets, smelling each other's sweat, watching each other go to the toilet, passing cigarette smoke from mouth to mouth. Nothing that counts as cheating happens, and everything does.

The sequence introduces Eivind as the man who plays, unlike Aksel, who explains, and it introduces the relationship's central contradiction: intimacy and avoidance at once. And Julie is honest about the rules and wholly dishonest about what she is doing.

### A bildungsroman that won't promise an ending

*The bildungsroman* says the form promises formation, education and an answer to the question "who will she be?", classically either accommodation (she takes her place, often by marrying) or rupture (she breaks away). Trier's film keeps both doors open, then walks through neither.

Aksel represents one kind of accommodation: he is established, articulate, and he wants children; Julie isn't sure she does. Eivind represents another, the life of an ordinary job and no grand plan. She tries both. The film's late movement, after Aksel's brother tells her that Aksel has incurable cancer, brings a different kind of education: Julie sits with someone who is running out of the time she has been treating as unlimited. In their last conversations, at the hospital, Aksel talks about the world he grew up in, of books, records and comics he could hold, and his sense that it is disappearing; he is gentler with her than she expects, and she listens in a way she rarely has. Nothing is resolved, but she is changed.

The epilogue answers the form's question quietly. Julie is working as a stills photographer on a film set, alone, competent, apparently at peace. Through a window she sees Eivind with a partner and a baby. It is neither triumph nor loss, and closer to the künstlerroman's ending than the marriage plot's: she has, at last, done something steadily.

### A want without a tidy wound

*Want, need and wound* teaches the classic chain: a past injury, a false belief, a want that drives the plot. Julie has the outline of a wound: a father who is distracted and unreliable, who fails to come to her birthday and later seems more interested in his new family than in her. The film shows us that, and then declines to make it the key to everything.

Her want keeps moving: a new subject, a new man, a new way of seeing herself. The mushroom trip, at a party with Eivind and friends, is where the film comes closest to opening her up. In a hallucinatory sequence her anxieties come out as images: her father, and her own body seen as ageing. It's raw, funny and embarrassing, but it isn't a revelation that fixes her: Trier heeds the lesson's warning about over-tidy backstory. Julie's need, if she has one, is to stop measuring her life against all the lives she might be living. The film lets her get there partly, and late.

## The shape

The film doesn't fit a three-act or five-part model comfortably. Its chapters behave more like the episodes of a picaresque, each a self-contained adventure, held together by a bildungsroman arc and one clear hinge, the frozen-time sequence, a little before the middle.

| Stage | What happens | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| Prologue | Julie switches studies and lovers; meets and moves in with Aksel | Opening tenth |
| Testing the first life | Aksel's friends and the question of children; the wedding party and Eivind | About a tenth to a third |
| The hinge | Frozen time; Julie leaves Aksel for Eivind | About two-fifths |
| The second life | Life with Eivind; the mushroom trip; Julie's restlessness returns | About half to two-thirds |
| Mortality | Aksel's illness; their last conversations; Julie's pregnancy and its loss | About two-thirds to the epilogue |
| Epilogue | Julie as a photographer on set; Eivind seen through a window | Final minutes |

What none of the standard frameworks captures is that the climax isn't a decision. The last third is driven by something outside Julie's control, another person's death, and the change it brings is in how she holds time rather than in anything she does.

## What people said

**Joachim Trier** has explained that the title is a Norwegian figure of speech: what you say, half-jokingly, when you've let someone down in some small way. He liked the irony of putting it on a film about love and passion (he said so to *The Hollywood Reporter* around the film's US release). In interviews he has tied the frozen-time sequence to the film's larger subject, time itself.

**Eskil Vogt**, interviewed by *Seventh Row* in 2022, said that the frozen-time scene was among the earliest ideas the writers had, before there was a plot around it.

**Renate Reinsve** has said in several interviews that she had been close to giving up acting before the film. Trier had given her a tiny part in *Oslo, August 31st* years earlier and wrote Julie with her in mind. She won Best Actress at Cannes in 2021, from a jury led by Spike Lee, and was nominated for a BAFTA. The script was nominated for the Academy Award for Best Original Screenplay, and the film for Best International Feature.

**The critics** were overwhelmingly warm. *The Guardian* headlined Peter Bradshaw's Cannes review (2021) by calling the film an instant classic, and Mark Kermode in *The Observer* (2022) hailed Julie as a heroine for our times. A. O. Scott in *The New York Times* (2022) suggested that a film about Julie's indecision was also about Trier's own ambivalence, a way of reading the narrator's irony. The dissent was real, though. Deborah Ross in *The Spectator* (2022) said she couldn't see what the fuss was about. Even admirers called the late turn to illness the most conventional part of the film, and the Rotten Tomatoes critics' summary conceded that its set-up is a little contrived and its plot turns melodramatic, disguised by Trier's lightness of touch.

## Watch for

- **The prologue** (first ten minutes): how much life the narrator gets through, and how fast. Count the identities Julie tries on.
- **The weekend with Aksel's friends** (early on): their children everywhere, Aksel's wish for his own, and Julie's evasions.
- **The wedding party** (about a fifth in): the rules of not cheating, and how each test is more intimate than the last.
- **The thirtieth birthday** (in the first third): the narrator's step back through Julie's female ancestors, and her father's absence.
- **The light switch** (a little before halfway): what is frozen, what isn't, and how the film brings time back. Watch the scene that follows it.
- **The mushroom trip** (a little past halfway): how the images give away fears Julie never says aloud.
- **The hospital conversations** (last third): how much Aksel talks, and how little Julie does. Notice who is listening for once.
- **The epilogue** (final minutes): Julie at work, the window, Eivind and the baby. Ask whether this is loss, peace or both.

## Try it

Write a scene where the clock stops for your character, and then write the moment it starts again. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Pick a character who wants two incompatible things and is avoiding the choice.
2. Write a short passage in which time pauses (literally, or through a dream, a lie, a held breath) and they get to have the forbidden thing without consequences.
3. Write the moment time restarts, and the first action they take afterwards. It must cost them something.

- **Stage:** Freeze the other actors mid-gesture while your character moves among them. Decide what the audience sees in the frozen faces.
- **Screen:** Script the stopped world in a few precise images, then cut back to the exact frame where it stopped. Note what has changed in the character's face.
- **Journalism:** In a profile, slow one decisive moment right down, a pause in the telling, before returning to the subject's ordinary pace of life. Use it to show what they were weighing.

**Check:** If you cut the pause, would the reader still understand what your character wants, and why it's impossible?
