## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*Uptown Girls* (2003) was directed by Boaz Yakin, who had made *Fresh* (1994) and *Remember the Titans* (2000). Molly Gunn (Brittany Murphy) is the grown-up daughter of a dead rock star, living on her inheritance as a Manhattan party girl, until her money manager disappears with it. Broke, she takes a job as nanny to Lorraine "Ray" Schleine (Dakota Fanning), an eight-year-old who is anxious, rigid, obsessed with germs and far more adult than Molly, and whose father lies in a coma while her mother, a music executive, is rarely home. Reviews were poor, but the film found a devoted audience on cable and video, and its reputation has grown, partly because of how good both leads are and partly, since Murphy's death in 2009, because of how much warmth she brings to it.

## Why it works

### Two halves of one person

*Blake Snyder* sorts films into ten story types, and one of them, **Buddy Love**, isn't just romance. It covers any story where two people, each incomplete, are pushed together, clash, separate and come back, each made whole by the other. *Uptown Girls* is a Buddy Love with no romance in it at all.

The fit is exact. Molly is all play and no responsibility: impulsive, generous, unable to pay a bill or keep a schedule. Ray is all responsibility and no play: she has a routine, a list of rules, opinions on nutrition and no friends her own age. Each has exactly what the other lacks. The film's comedy comes from the clash, and its feeling comes from the gradual exchange. Molly teaches Ray to play, make a mess and be a child; Ray teaches Molly to look after herself, and it's Ray who suggests that Molly auction her father's guitar collection to get on her feet. Like any good Buddy Love, it separates them in the last act, at the moment they need each other most.

### The child as parent

Bergson's list of comic devices in *Comedy structure* includes **inversion**: roles flip, and the child lectures the parent. *Uptown Girls* is built on it. Ray gives orders, checks Molly's behaviour and keeps the house running in her absent mother's place. Molly behaves like a child let out of school.

What lifts this above a gag is that the inversion is also the wound, the subject of *Want, need and wound*. Both are children who lost their parents. Molly's famous parents died when she was young, and she has stayed young ever since, surrounded by their things, unable to move on. Ray's father is dying in front of her and her mother is never there, so she has become her own parent. One stopped growing up; the other grew up far too soon. The rigidity that makes Ray funny (Bergson's mechanical habit in a living child) is a defence against a grief she won't feel. The film's job is to let both of them become the right age.

### Foils who are alike

*The cast as a system* says a foil works through likeness: two characters in the same situation, making different choices. The film quietly lines Molly and Ray up as versions of each other at different stages of the same loss. Both are, in their way, rich girls with no one looking after them. Both cling to a father: Molly to his songs, guitars and fame; Ray to a man who can no longer speak. The difference is only time.

Heather Locklear's Roma, Ray's mother, completes the triangle. She's not a villain, just a distracted, busy woman whom both girls, in different ways, have to forgive or work around. The supporting world (Molly's friends Ingrid and Huey, played by Marley Shelton and Donald Faison; and Neal, a musician played by Jesse Spencer) mostly exists to show us Molly's life before she has to grow up.

### The teacup ride

The scene that people remember is set on the teacup ride at Coney Island, and the film sets it up twice. On an outing earlier, Molly tells Ray that after her parents died, she ran away to Coney Island and rode the teacups, and she takes Ray there. It's the first time Ray loosens up.

Then Ray's father dies. She blames Molly for having let her hope, has her fired, and runs away. Her mother asks Molly to find her, and Molly knows exactly where she'll be: spinning in a teacup at Coney Island, alone. Ray lashes out at her at first, then finally breaks down and cries in Molly's arms.

It works because the place carries both girls' grief at once. The ride Molly used to escape feeling becomes the place where Ray finally feels it. The setting does the emotional work the dialogue doesn't need to (compare *Light, colour and setting* on place as meaning), and the scene is a payoff of a plant from half an hour before. The child who refused to be a child is finally held like one, and the adult who refused to be an adult finally acts like one.

### Making us care about two difficult people

On paper, neither lead is easy to like. Molly is spoiled and careless; Ray is bossy and rude. *Making us care* explains how the film gets round this with Michael Hauge's ways in. Molly gets undeserved misfortune (robbed by the person she trusted) and humour, a whole repertoire of Murphy's physical comedy. Ray gets undeserved misfortune too, which we learn gradually, through the father in his coma. Both get vulnerability. The film never asks us to approve of either; it asks us to understand them, and then to watch them help each other.

### A song as the final image

The ending pays off the music thread. Molly's father wrote a song for her when she was a child, "Molly Smiles". At Ray's ballet recital, Molly arrives late to find Ray wearing the tutu Molly made for her, and dancing not the strict routine but freely, to Neal singing that song. The child who once couldn't bear mess dances improvised steps to the song that was Molly's childhood. It's a strong use of what *Music and silence* calls a leitmotif: a piece of music that carries memory and returns changed.

## The shape

Snyder's beat sheet (see *Save the Cat in practice*) maps cleanly onto the film's ninety-odd minutes.

| Beat | What happens | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| Opening Image / Set-Up | Molly's carefree, chaotic life among her parents' things | Opening to about a tenth |
| Catalyst | Her money is gone | About a tenth in |
| Debate / Break into Two | Out of options, she takes the nanny job with Ray | About a fifth in |
| Fun and Games | The odd couple clash; Molly drags Ray into the city | About a quarter to half |
| Midpoint | They grow close; Ray begins to loosen | About half |
| Bad Guys Close In | Molly's life and Ray's family pressures mount; Coney Island outing | About half to three-quarters |
| All Is Lost | Ray's father dies; Molly is fired | About three-quarters in |
| Finale | The teacups; reconciliation | About the last fifth |
| Final Image | Ray dances freely to "Molly Smiles" | Final minutes |

The Opening and Final Images work as Snyder wants: a woman frozen in her childhood at the start, and a child freed into hers at the end, watched by a woman who has finally grown up.

## What people said

**The critics** were mostly unkind. Stephen Holden in *The New York Times* found it shallow, and the film's aggregate scores were low; the common complaint was that the plot was contrived and the tone lurched between broad comedy and grief. Many reviewers who disliked the film still praised Fanning's dry, unsentimental performance. The best-known champion was Roger Ebert, who gave it three stars out of four and compared Murphy's comic skill to Lucille Ball's; he argued that objections to the plot's plausibility missed the point, because the film was about its two personalities.

**Later writing** has been warmer. Retrospectives, including a 2023 piece in *Paste* marking the film's twentieth anniversary, have argued that it's about reconnecting with your inner child, and revival screenings have presented it as a cable-era favourite for a generation of young women. Murphy's death in 2009, at 32, has made her performance poignant to watch. The honest weakness is real: the money plot and the romance with Neal are thin, and the film leans on coincidence to get its pieces in place. Its strength is that none of that is what audiences remember.

## Watch for

- **Molly's apartment** (opening minutes): how much of it belongs to her parents.
- **Ray's introduction** (about a fifth in): her routine and rules, and how quickly she takes charge of Molly.
- **The first outing** (about a third in): the moment Ray resists, and then doesn't.
- **The father** (around the middle): how little screen time he has, and how much he shapes Ray.
- **The guitars** (around two-thirds in): Ray's practical advice to sell them, and what Molly has to let go of.
- **Coney Island, first visit** (about two-thirds in): Molly's story about the teacups. The plant.
- **The teacups, second visit** (about four-fifths in): the payoff. Watch how the spinning stops.
- **The recital** (final minutes): the tutu, the song, and the freestyle dance.

## Try it

Write an odd-couple scene in which a child acts as the parent. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Choose two characters, one older and one younger, and give each half of a whole: one has play, the other responsibility.
2. Write a short scene in which the younger one takes charge of the older one over a practical problem (a bill, a meal, getting to work on time).
3. Give both the same wound, but let them have reacted to it in opposite ways. Don't name the wound in the scene; let one object or place hint at it.

- **Stage:** Put the shared object on stage from the start (a photograph, an instrument) and let both characters touch it differently.
- **Screen:** Plant a place in this scene that you could return to at the climax, as the teacups are.
- **Journalism:** In a profile, look for the role reversal: the child who manages a parent's illness, the junior colleague who runs the boss. Report one moment that shows it, without stating it.

**Check:** If you swapped your two characters' ages, would the scene stop working, and does each one have what the other lacks?
