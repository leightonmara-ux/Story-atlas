## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*World War Z* (2013) was directed by Marc Forster and produced by and starring Brad Pitt, with a screenplay credited to Matthew Michael Carnahan, Drew Goddard and Damon Lindelof. It takes its title, and not much else, from Max Brooks's 2006 novel *World War Z: An Oral History of the Zombie War*. Gerry Lane (Pitt), a former United Nations investigator, is pulled back into service when a fast-spreading plague turns people into sprinting, swarming attackers; to keep his wife and daughters on a navy ship, he must travel the world to find where the outbreak began and how to fight it. The film is famous almost as much for its troubled production as for itself: its original third act, a battle in Russia, was scrapped after filming and replaced by a quiet, claustrophobic finale in a World Health Organization lab in Cardiff.

## Why it works

### An investigation, not a siege

Most zombie films are sieges: a group holes up in a house, a mall or a farm, and the walls slowly fail. *World War Z* turns the genre into an investigation. Gerry is a reporter's kind of hero. He asks questions, follows leads, weighs sources, and his weapon is noticing things.

That's why the film keeps moving. Each location is a lead that pays off with one piece of information and a new place to go. In South Korea, at a US base, a former CIA operative held in a cell tells him that Israel walled itself off before the outbreak, so it must have known something. In Jerusalem, a Mossad official, Jurgen Warmbrunn, explains why: a rumour in an intercepted message, taken seriously because of a "tenth man" rule, under which if nine officials agree, the tenth is duty-bound to argue the opposite. Each answer is a clue, not a resolution. *Genre as a promise* describes horror as a plot of proof: the monster appears, is discovered, is confirmed to the sceptics, and is confronted. Here the world already believes in the monster, so the proof the plot chases is how to beat it.

### Each stop is a little film

*Frank Daniel & Paul Gulino* teaches that a feature breaks into sequences of ten to fifteen minutes, each with its own goal, tension and resolution. *World War Z* is almost a textbook. Philadelphia: get the family out of the traffic jam. Newark: survive the night in a housing block and reach the roof before the helicopter leaves. Korea: find patient zero, and get back to the plane. Jerusalem: learn what Israel knew, then escape when the wall is breached. The plane: survive an outbreak in a sealed cabin. Cardiff: reach the lab's sealed wing.

Every sequence ends by launching the next, and each has a small dramatic question (*will they make the roof?*) inside the big one (*can the world be saved?*). This is also what *The quest and the picaresque* calls the problem of momentum: a road story needs a destination pulling it forward, not just a string of sights. Gerry's destination keeps moving, but there is always one.

### Scale, then intimacy

The film's spectacle is enormous: the swarm pouring over Jerusalem's wall in a living pyramid, drawn by the sound of people singing over loudspeakers inside, is the image the trailers sold. But the film keeps tying scale to one family. The opening is pancakes at breakfast. The crisis begins in a traffic jam with the girls in the back seat. On the ship, the navy makes it clear that Gerry's family has a place only while he's useful; when he's presumed dead after the plane crash, they are moved off it.

The cleverest touch connects the two scales directly. In Korea, Gerry's wife Karin calls his satellite phone at the worst possible moment, and the ringing draws the infected to his team. Love becomes a danger. *Making us care* lists jeopardy and undeserved misfortune as the fastest ways to make an audience attach to someone; here the film uses a family on a ship as the jeopardy that a whole planet can't provide. A billion deaths are a statistic. Two daughters on a carrier deck are a story.

### The rewrite: trading a battle for a corridor

The finale that exists was not the finale that was shot. In the original version, as Forster and others later described it, Gerry's plane from Jerusalem landed in Moscow, he was conscripted by the Russian military, and the film ended in a large battle in which the cold slowed the infected. After a rough cut, everyone involved agreed it didn't work. Damon Lindelof was brought in; by his account he offered two routes, trying to repair what had been shot or throwing out everything after Gerry leaves Israel and writing something new. They chose the second, and Drew Goddard finished the rewrite when Lindelof's schedule ran out. Weeks of reshoots followed, and the budget swelled.

What they built is a lesson in contrast. After an hour and a half of crowds and crashes, the last act is nearly silent: three people creeping through the corridors of a WHO research building, past rooms where infected scientists stand motionless until a sound wakes them. One, behind glass, clacks his teeth. The menace is a vending machine's rattle or a dropped object. *Music and silence* notes that removing sound makes the audience lean in; this whole act is built on that drop-out. In Snyder's terms (see *Blake Snyder*), a globe-trotting film ends as a Monster in the House.

It also gives the hero the right kind of climax. Gerry doesn't win with a gun. He wins with a deduction he has been assembling all along (in Jerusalem, he noticed the swarm run past a frail, sick-looking boy), and a gamble on his own body: he injects himself with a deadly pathogen to test whether disease makes him invisible to them. An investigator's climax is a test of the hypothesis.

### From oral history to one man's road

Brooks's novel is a fake piece of journalism. Its narrator is an agent of a United Nations commission who, years after the war, interviews survivors across the world; the book presents itself as the human testimony left out of his official report. Brooks has cited Studs Terkel's oral histories as a model, and *Journalism's story types* explains what that form promises: the voices themselves, with the editor's art in choosing and sequencing them so they answer and contradict each other. There is no single protagonist, and the war is already over.

Film resists that form. A feature needs a person in danger now, so the adaptation turns the interviewer into the hero and the past into the present: the questions are still asked, but in the middle of the outbreak, by a man with something to lose. Some pieces survive the move, notably Israel's wall and the idea of tracing the plague back to its source. What's lost is the book's chorus, its satire of governments and the sense of a whole world's experience. It's a useful case for *Same skeleton, three media*: the same premise, rebuilt from an oral history into a quest.

## What people said

**The makers.** Forster said publicly that the Russia ending, once cut together, felt too big and exhausting and that a simpler route was better; its visual effects were never finished. Lindelof described his two-option pitch in interviews in 2013, and a much-cited *Vanity Fair* account that year laid out the reshoots and spiralling costs. Pitt and Forster have maintained that the released version is the film they wanted. **Max Brooks** has been gracious but clear that the book and film share little beyond the title; he said he wasn't involved in the script.

**The critics** were more positive than the bad pre-release press had led anyone to expect. Justin Chang, in *Variety*, found it surprisingly smart and gripping, and liked Pitt's understated, appealing hero. Kenneth Turan (*Los Angeles Times*) thought familiar material was handled with intelligence and skill. The sharpest dissent came from Matt Zoller Seitz at RogerEbert.com, who argued that Forster had made a zombie film for people who don't like zombie films, keeping the body count bloodless and the meaning thin, which he didn't consider an achievement. Many fans of the novel agreed that the film threw away the book's best ideas. Audiences disagreed with the doubters: it earned more than half a billion dollars worldwide.

## Watch for

- **The opening montage** (first few minutes): news fragments and nature footage of swarming creatures, which set up the film's idea of the infected as a swarm, not individuals.
- **The traffic jam** (about a twentieth in): how long the film stays inside the car with the family before showing what's happening ahead.
- **The count** (Philadelphia and the Newark roof): Gerry timing how long infection takes, and later counting while he waits to see whether he's been infected. The investigator's habit, made physical.
- **The satellite phone** (about a third in): Karin's call in Korea, and what it costs.
- **The tenth man** (about halfway): Warmbrunn's explanation in Jerusalem. A structural idea disguised as exposition.
- **The boy in the crowd** (Jerusalem): watch the swarm swerve around him. The film's ending is planted here.
- **The corridor** (final third): how much of the Cardiff sequence is played without score, and what makes noise.
- **The injection** (near the end): Gerry walking down the corridor past an infected man, unseen.

## Try it

Rewrite a sprawling story as a chamber piece. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Take a project of yours, or a story you know, whose climax is big: a battle, a crowd, a public confrontation.
2. Ask what single question the climax has to answer. Write it in one sentence.
3. Now write a new climax that answers the same question in one confined space, with two or three people, and as little noise as possible. Let the hero win by testing an idea, not by force.

- **Stage:** Turn the confined climax into a single scene in one room, with one sound effect that carries the danger.
- **Screen:** Script the scene with no score. Mark every sound that matters, and what it threatens.
- **Journalism:** Take a large story (a disaster, an election) and find one room where its central question was decided. Write that scene, reported, as the climax of the piece.

**Check:** Does your smaller climax answer the same question as the big one, and is it the hero's thinking, not their strength, that answers it?
