## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*Braveheart* (1995) was directed by Mel Gibson, who also stars as William Wallace, from an original screenplay by Randall Wallace, with a score by James Horner. In thirteenth-century Scotland under the English king Edward I, "Longshanks" (Patrick McGoohan), a farmer who only wants a quiet life loses his secretly married wife, Murron (Catherine McCormack), to an English magistrate, and his revenge becomes a national rising. He wins at Stirling, is betrayed at Falkirk, and is finally captured and executed in London, while the nobleman Robert the Bruce (Angus Macfadyen) slowly learns to deserve the crown he wants. It won five Academy Awards, including Best Picture and Best Director.

## Why it works

### A private want, broken on purpose

The first act gives the hero the smallest possible want. As a boy, Wallace sees the bodies of hanged Scottish nobles and then loses his father and brother; at the funeral a little girl, Murron, gives him a thistle. When he comes home as a man, he says he wants to farm, raise a family and live in peace. He marries Murron at night, in secret, so that no English lord can claim the wedding-night rights the film's Longshanks has granted his barons.

Then the film takes the want away. A soldier assaults Murron, Wallace fights him off, she flees but is caught, and the local magistrate cuts her throat in front of the village as an example. It's about a quarter in, and it's the film's hinge. This is the classic shape *Want, need and wound* describes, with a twist: the wound is inflicted on screen, after we've watched the want form, so we feel it as Wallace does. His first act of rebellion, storming the garrison and killing the magistrate, is pure grief. The rest of the film slowly turns that revenge into a cause.

It's also why we care so fast: *Making us care* puts undeserved misfortune first among the routes to identification, and Wallace loses his family twice before he has done anything wrong.

### The speech as a turning point, not a summary

Before the battle of Stirling, about two-fifths in, the Scottish nobles are haggling with the English and the ordinary soldiers are drifting away. Wallace rides in with his face painted blue and makes the film's most famous speech. It works because of *Dialogue reveals character*'s rule that a speech in a crisis is an action: it doesn't describe the stakes, it changes the situation. The men are leaving; by the end they aren't. Run and live a while, it argues, or fight for the chance to tell the enemy

> they may take our lives, but they'll never take our freedom!
> — William Wallace (Mel Gibson), *Braveheart*

It never mentions Murron. When Wallace then rides out to "parley" and insults the English commander instead, the nobles lose their deal and the battle becomes his.

### The cast as a set of answers

*The cast as a system* says each character should be a different answer to the hero's moral problem. Here the problem is loyalty: to what, and at what price? Wallace answers with his life. Longshanks answers with power, witty and utterly cold. His son, the future Edward II, is presented as weak, and the film's treatment of him drew accusations of homophobia. The Scottish nobles, Mornay and Lochlan among them, answer with land and titles.

The most important answer is Robert the Bruce's, because he is the character who changes. Wallace is close to what K. M. Weiland, quoted in *Want, need and wound*, calls a **flat arc**: he already knows his truth, and the story tests it and changes others. Bruce carries the **positive change arc**. He admires Wallace, but his leprous father insists a crown requires compromise. At Falkirk, about two-thirds in, the nobles' cavalry rides away, bribed; Wallace chases Longshanks, unhorses an armoured knight, and the knight lifts his visor. It's Bruce. The reveal works because we've watched him torn. He helps Wallace escape, and is left sitting among the dead.

Later, when Wallace is betrayed at a meeting in Edinburgh, Bruce learns his father arranged it and turns on him. The film's last movement belongs to Bruce.

### The narrator who turns out to be the hero

The film opens with a voice-over telling us that English historians will call the speaker a liar. Only at the end do we learn whose voice it is: Robert the Bruce. That makes the whole film his testimony, and it reframes the structure. Wallace's story is a martyrdom; Bruce's, a conversion. *Introducing a character* points out that others' talk can introduce someone before we see them; here, the character talking is introduced by his own voice, and we don't know who he is until he has earned his crown.

It's also a sly defence against the film's critics: a narrator who admits he'll be called a liar has claimed the right to tell a legend.

### The ending: one word, and music that knows the way

The execution, about five-sixths in, is built by *Editing*'s cross-cutting. In London, Wallace is hanged, stretched and disembowelled while the magistrate offers him a quick death if he will beg for mercy. The crowd, which came to jeer, begins to call for mercy too. Intercut, Isabelle goes to the dying Longshanks, who can no longer speak, and tells him his line will not last. Wallace gathers himself and shouts one word, "Freedom!", and the film cuts to Longshanks's last moments, as if the shout killed him. Before the axe falls, he sees Murron in the crowd, and a cloth that was hers drops from his hand: the wound and the cause, joined.

James Horner's score prepares this all film long. Its Celtic melodies, carried on uilleann pipes and whistle, are tied first to Wallace's childhood and to Murron, and later swell under the battles. By the end, music first tied to love is carrying the cause: *Music and silence*'s leitmotif, telling us the two were always one story.

### The epilogue as payoff

The final minutes jump to 1314. Bruce, now king, faces the English at Bannockburn. He turns to his army and invokes Wallace, someone throws Wallace's sword to land point down in the ground, and Bruce leads the charge while the narration tells us that the Scots won their freedom. *Joseph Campbell & Christopher Vogler* would call this the Return with the Elixir, with a twist: the hero who returns isn't Wallace but Bruce, and the elixir is Wallace's example.

## The shape

The film fits Vogler's journey (*Joseph Campbell & Christopher Vogler*) almost too neatly, provided you let Bruce finish what Wallace starts. Read alone, Wallace's story ends as tragedy does in *Tragedy and comedy*, in death and isolation, but the film frames that death as a victory.

## What people said

**Randall Wallace** took his story largely from Blind Harry, the fifteenth-century poet whose long poem about Wallace made him a legend. Asked whether Blind Harry was true, he said he didn't know, only that it spoke to his heart. **Mel Gibson**, on the DVD commentary, acknowledged the inaccuracies and argued that his versions of events were more cinematically compelling than the record. He initially passed on the script, and at first wanted only to direct, agreeing reluctantly to play Wallace as well.

**The critics** on release were mostly impressed by the spectacle. Caryn James in *The New York Times* (1995) called it one of the most spectacular entertainments in years, and Roger Ebert, in the *Chicago Sun-Times*, gave it three and a half stars and compared it to the old Hollywood swordplay pictures. Richard Schickel in *Time* mocked the star-director's self-indulgence, and Peter Travers in *Rolling Stone* thought the love scenes dragged.

**The historians** were harsher. Elizabeth Ewan wrote that it almost totally sacrifices accuracy for epic adventure. Sharon Krossa listed the problems: belted plaids and kilts centuries too early, wrong dates, wrong names, wrong characters. Its Stirling Bridge has no bridge. Isabella of France was a child in France at the time of Falkirk, so her affair with Wallace, and the hint that he fathered Edward III, is pure invention. The medieval right of the lord to the bride's wedding night is generally regarded as a myth, Bruce probably didn't fight for the English at Falkirk, and Scotland had not been under English rule for a century; it had been invaded the year before Wallace's rising. The historian Colin McArthur argued that the film turns Wallace into a modern nationalist guerrilla, leaving out his own use of conscription, and he called it xenophobic; the medieval historian Robert Bartlett agreed that its English characters support the charge of Anglophobia. The film also had real effects: it drew tourists to Stirling, and Lin Anderson, in *Braveheart: From Hollywood to Holyrood*, credits it with a part in Scottish politics in the years around the 1997 devolution vote.

**The director's later conduct** has shaped how the film is now discussed. In 2006 Gibson was arrested for drunk driving and made antisemitic remarks to the arresting officer, for which he later apologised, and in 2010 recordings of abusive phone calls to a former partner became public. Neither changes what is on screen, but later discussion of the film rarely separates it from him.

## Watch for

- **The thistle** (first ten minutes): the girl at the funeral, and where the flower turns up again.
- **Murron's death** (about a quarter in): the film keeps the killing brief and lingers on the aftermath. Watch Wallace's return to the village.
- **The Stirling speech** (about two-fifths in): count the men leaving at the start and watch the crowd's faces at the end.
- **Bruce at Falkirk** (about two-thirds in): the helmet coming off, then Bruce alone among the dead.
- **The execution** (about five-sixths in): the cross-cutting with Longshanks, the crowd's change, Murron in the crowd, and the falling cloth.
- **The narrator's identity** (final minutes): listen for who has been talking all along.

## Try it

Write a sequence where a private wound turns into a public cause. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Give a character one small, concrete want (a farm, a shop, a marriage) and write a short scene where they nearly have it.
2. Take it away through someone else's deliberate act. Write the character's first response as purely personal: revenge, rage or flight.
3. Write a later scene where the same character speaks to a group, and the original loss is not mentioned once. Let us feel it underneath.

- **Stage:** Put the loss offstage and the speech onstage. Have the speaker address the audience as the crowd.
- **Screen:** Plan one sound or image from the first scene (a tune, an object) that returns in the speech scene without comment.
- **Journalism:** In a profile of an activist, find the private event that started it, then show how they talk about the cause now. Report the gap honestly.

**Check:** Could a reader trace a single line from the loss in step 2 to the speech in step 3, even though the speech never names it?
