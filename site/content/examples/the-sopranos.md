## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole series, including the ending.*

*The Sopranos* was created by David Chase and ran on HBO for six seasons and 86 episodes, from January 1999 to June 2007. Tony Soprano (James Gandolfini) is a New Jersey mob boss with a wife, Carmela (Edie Falco), two teenage children, a domineering mother and a crew of killers. In the first episode he collapses with a panic attack and, in secret, starts seeing a psychiatrist, Dr. Jennifer Melfi (Lorraine Bracco). The premise is a gangster film turned inside out: a family drama about a man whose business is violence, told largely from his own unreliable point of view.

## Why it works

### The therapy frame

The pilot is built around Tony's first sessions with Melfi. He sits in her waiting room, then tells her, evasively, about his life, and the episode flashes back to show what he's leaving out. The trigger he eventually describes is a family of wild ducks that had settled in his swimming pool. When they flew away, he passed out.

Therapy gives the series what *Whose side are we on?* calls subjective access: a reason for a criminal to explain himself, week after week, out loud. It also gives every scene a second audience. We watch Tony act, then watch him narrate it to Melfi, and the gap between the two is the show's richest source of irony. *Point of view and voice* would call him an unreliable narrator in his own story: he lies to his therapist, and the series lets us see the lie.

Melfi isn't just a device. The series gives her a life and a moral problem: should she treat a man like this at all? Its sharpest test comes in the third season's "Employee of the Month". Melfi is raped in a parking garage stairwell, and her attacker goes free on a procedural error. She knows that one word to Tony would bring a revenge no court will. In a session, Tony senses something is wrong and asks if she wants to say something. She says "No," and the episode ends. A whole season's tension about whether therapy can civilize Tony turns into whether Tony can corrupt therapy, and she refuses.

### Allegiance under pressure: "College"

*Whose side are we on?* explains how stories keep us with bad people: alignment, a worse world around them, the slow slide. The fifth episode, "College" (1999), is where *The Sopranos* tested how far that could go, very early.

Tony drives his daughter Meadow (Jamie-Lynn Sigler) around Maine to visit colleges. It's tender: they talk honestly, and she asks him straight out whether he's in the Mafia. At a gas station he spots Fabian Petrulio, a former associate who informed on the mob and vanished into witness protection. Between campus visits, Tony tracks him down and strangles him with a wire. Then he goes back to Meadow, and the episode cuts between the murder and the family trip until they're one story.

Chase has said HBO's then president Chris Albrecht objected strongly, fearing viewers would never forgive Tony. Chase's answer was that a mob boss who let an informer walk would be a fraud, and the audience would know it. HBO accepted the killing, and by most accounts the episode made the informant as unpleasant as possible to soften it. "College" won an Emmy for its writing. It set the terms of the whole series: we would stay aligned with Tony, and the show would keep testing whether we should.

### Dreams that know more than Tony

The series uses dreams the way *Ingmar Bergman* did: as images that state what the waking story can't. The best example is the second-season finale, "Funhouse". Tony has food poisoning and a night of fever dreams. In one, he's on a pier talking to a fish laid out on a stall, and the fish speaks in the voice of his old friend Big Pussy, admitting what Tony has long suspected and refused to say: Pussy is wearing a wire for the FBI. When Tony wakes, he acts on it. Before the episode ends, Pussy has been taken out on a boat and killed.

The dream isn't decoration. It's the scene where Tony stops lying to himself, and it can only happen asleep. Later seasons push further: whole episodes of dream ("The Test Dream", season 5) and, after Tony is shot in season 6, a long coma sequence in which he lives as a mild salesman called Kevin Finnerty. They divide viewers, and the show didn't care. *Want, need and wound* talks about the truth a character won't face; *The Sopranos* gives that truth its own setting.

### "Pine Barrens": the comedy of getting lost

"Pine Barrens" (season 3, 2001), written by Terence Winter from a story by Winter and Tim Van Patten and directed by Steve Buscemi, is the series at its funniest. Paulie (Tony Sirico) and Christopher (Michael Imperioli) go to collect money from a Russian, Valery, fight with him, and believe they've killed him. They drive him into the snowbound Pine Barrens of southern New Jersey to bury him. He isn't dead. He escapes into the woods, and the two hunters become the hunted, lost overnight in a freezing forest, sheltering in an abandoned van, eating ketchup packets, and turning on each other. Over a crackling phone, Tony tells them Valery was a commando who worked for the Interior Ministry, and by the time Paulie passes it on, the commando has become an interior decorator.

The episode shows how comedy works in drama (see *Comedy structure*): put two proud, vain men in a situation that strips away every status marker they have, and let them blame each other. It also refuses a payoff. Valery's fate is never revealed. Winter has said it's the question he is asked most. *Television structure* talks about A, B and C stories; here a whole A story is left unresolved, and the series trusts the audience to live with it.

### The cut to black

The final episode, "Made in America" (written and directed by Chase, 10 June 2007), ends in a diner, Holsten's. Tony arrives first and chooses Journey's "Don't Stop Believin'" on the table jukebox. Carmela comes in, then their son AJ. Each time the door's bell rings, Tony looks up. A man in a Members Only jacket at the counter glances over and walks to the restroom. Outside, Meadow is struggling to parallel park. The bell rings again; Tony looks up; and the screen goes black, silent, for several seconds before the credits roll. Many viewers thought their cable had failed.

*Frank Kermode & Peter Brooks* explains why endings matter so much: we read for the end, which gives the rest its meaning. Chase withheld it. In the final season, Tony's brother-in-law Bobby had reflected that when you're killed, you probably don't even hear it happen, and many viewers read the black screen as exactly that, Tony's death from his own point of view. Others read it as a portrait of the life he has left: forever looking up at the door.

## The shape

The series follows the season-arc model in *Television structure*, with a mostly self-contained episode inside each season's longer story.

| Season | Main arc | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (1999) | Uncle Junior and Tony's mother, Livia, plot against Tony; therapy begins | First 13 episodes |
| 2 (2000) | Richie Aprile's challenge; Big Pussy unmasked as an informant | Episodes 14–26 |
| 3 (2001) | Ralph Cifaretto; Melfi's assault; Tony and Gloria Trillo | Episodes 27–39 |
| 4 (2002) | The marriage collapses; Tony and Carmela separate | Episodes 40–52 |
| 5 (2004) | Tony's cousin Tony Blundetto; Adriana's betrayal discovered | Episodes 53–65 |
| 6 (2006–07) | Tony is shot; Christopher's death; war with Phil Leotardo; the diner | Episodes 66–86 |

What no arc model predicts is how little Tony changes. Therapy ends in the last season ("The Blue Comet") when Melfi, persuaded by a study suggesting that talk therapy can help sociopaths sharpen their manipulation, turns him away. The series is an argument against the transformation arc.

## What people said

**David Chase** refused to explain the ending. Days after it aired, he told Alan Sepinwall of *The Star-Ledger* (2007):

> I have no interest in explaining, defending, reinterpreting, or adding to what is there.
— David Chase, *The Star-Ledger* (2007)

He kept talking about it all the same. In *DGA Quarterly* (spring 2015) he went through the diner scene shot by shot, describing how he built tension out of nothing but ordinary customers and the bell, and why the Journey song suited Tony's past, without saying whether Tony dies. In 2014, an article by Martha P. Nochimson for *Vox* reported that Chase had told her Tony wasn't dead, and Chase's representative said his words had been misread. In the interview that closes Matt Zoller Seitz and Alan Sepinwall's *The Sopranos Sessions* (2019), Chase referred to the ending as a "death scene", then said he'd had a different scene in mind at the time and hadn't yet thought of cutting to black.

**The critics** treated the show as the start of a new era. Brett Martin's *Difficult Men* (2013) and Sepinwall's *The Revolution Was Televised* (2012) both put Tony Soprano first in the line of cable antiheroes that ran through *Breaking Bad*. The dissent took two forms. Many viewers and some critics called the finale a cheat or a provocation; Chase denied, in later interviews, that he had been trying to blow anyone's mind. And Italian-American organizations argued from the start that the show traded in ethnic stereotypes; in 2002, cast members were excluded from New York's Columbus Day parade.

## Watch for

- **The ducks** (pilot, about halfway): Tony feeding them, and his face when they fly off.
- **"College"** (season 1, episode 5): cut between the strangling and the campus tour. Notice what Meadow asks him in the car.
- **Melfi's answer** ("Employee of the Month", season 3, final minutes): the session, Tony's question, and her "No."
- **The fish** ("Funhouse", season 2 finale): the dream conversation, then the boat.
- **The van** ("Pine Barrens", season 3): Paulie and Christopher's status war over a packet of ketchup.
- **The kitchen fight** ("Whitecaps", season 4 finale): Tony and Carmela's marriage ending in one long argument.
- **Melfi's last session** ("The Blue Comet", season 6, penultimate episode): who ends it, and how.
- **The diner** ("Made in America", final five minutes): count how often the bell rings and Tony looks up.

## Try it

Write a pair of scenes: one in which your character does something, and one in which they describe it to someone they want to impress. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Pick a character who has done something they're ashamed of. Write the act itself, plainly, in under a page.
2. Write a scene in which they tell it to a confidant (a therapist, a priest, a lawyer, a friend). Let them leave out, soften or reframe one crucial thing.
3. Give the listener one moment where they notice the gap, and decide whether to say so.

- **Stage:** Play both scenes on the same set, so the audience sees the place where the act happened while the character lies about it.
- **Screen:** Cross-cut the telling with the act, as "College" does, so the cuts expose each lie.
- **Journalism:** Compare a source's account of an event with the documents or other witnesses. Write the passage so the reader sees both versions side by side.

**Check:** Can a reader point to the exact line where your character's version departs from what happened?
