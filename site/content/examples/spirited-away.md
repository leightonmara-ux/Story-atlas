## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*Spirited Away* (*Sen to Chihiro no Kamikakushi*, 2001) was written and directed by Hayao Miyazaki at Studio Ghibli, with music by Joe Hisaishi. Ten-year-old Chihiro, sulking in the back of the car as her family moves house, follows her parents through a tunnel into what her father takes to be an abandoned theme park. When her parents gorge themselves on food at an empty stall and turn into pigs, Chihiro has to survive alone in a bathhouse for spirits run by the witch Yubaba, and find a way to get them, and herself, home. It shared the Golden Bear at the Berlin Film Festival in 2002, won the Academy Award for Best Animated Feature, and was for nearly two decades the highest-grossing film ever released in Japan.

## Why it works

### A world built around a workplace

Most fantasy worlds are built around a journey or a war. Miyazaki builds his around a job. The bathhouse is a vertical machine: Yubaba in her ornate rooms at the top, the staff dormitories and bathing halls in the middle, and at the bottom the boiler room, where the six-armed Kamaji stokes the furnaces and the little soot sprites carry coal.

*Light, colour and setting* teaches that a good setting embodies the conflict, and here it does. To stay in this world without being turned into an animal, Chihiro must work. Haku, the boy who helps her, tells her so on her first evening, and she spends the next long stretch climbing down to the boiler room and then up to Yubaba to ask for a job. Yubaba, bound by an oath to give work to anyone who asks, gives her one, takes most of her name in the contract and calls her Sen. The setting's rules are the plot's rules: work, the contract and the name.

### Growth through work and kindness, not fighting

Chihiro never defeats anyone. There is no final battle with Yubaba. She grows the way the heroes of a *bildungsroman* grow, through a curriculum of tasks, mentors and mistakes, and *The bildungsroman* lesson's question, "who will they be?", is answered by how she does her work.

The key scene comes a little under halfway in. A foul-smelling "stink spirit" arrives, and Chihiro is given the job nobody wants. Scrubbing the spirit in the great tub, she finds something lodged in its side, which turns out to be a bicycle handlebar, and with the whole staff hauling on a rope she pulls out a torrent of rubbish. The spirit is a polluted river god, and he leaves her a gift: a herbal dumpling.

From there, her power is kindness and attention. She gives half the dumpling to Haku, wounded and in dragon form, and the other half to No-Face, the masked spirit she let in out of the rain, who has swollen into a monster by swallowing staff and handing out gold. She doesn't fight either of them; she feeds them, and they are purged. As *Ursula K. Le Guin* argues in its section on conflict, a story's engine doesn't have to be a weapon.

### Making us care about a sulky child

Miyazaki's heroine starts unappealing on purpose. In the opening minutes she is whiny, limp and clingy: she complains about the move, flops in the back seat, and hangs on her mother's arm. *Making us care* explains why this still works. We are attached first by Michael Hauge's undeserved misfortune and jeopardy: her parents are transformed through their own greed, not hers, and she is alone among things that want to eat her.

Then the film switches to the most reliable tool of all, Emma Coats's rule that we admire a character more for trying than for succeeding. Watch the scene on the steep wooden outside staircase, where Chihiro, terrified, edges down sideways, then breaks into a run she can't control. She isn't brave yet. She is trying, and that is what makes us lean in. By the end, the girl who couldn't let go of her mother's arm walks out of the tunnel on her own.

### Ma: the train ride

In a 2002 interview with Roger Ebert, Miyazaki explained the moments in his films where nothing much happens. He clapped his hands several times and said that the time between the claps is what the Japanese call *ma*, a kind of emptiness, and that it is there intentionally. Without it, he said in effect, nonstop action is just busyness.

The great example is the train ride, about three-quarters of the way in. Chihiro, No-Face, the shrunken baby Boh and Yubaba's bird ride a train that runs across a shallow sea. Passengers are translucent shadows; one gets off at a station in the water; the light goes from afternoon to dusk. Almost nothing is said. The music is quiet and the sound of the train carries much of the scene.

*Music and silence* explains how removing the expected bed of action makes us lean in. Here it does something more: it lets Chihiro, and us, absorb everything that has happened, just before the story's resolution. The train is also a one-way journey: Kamaji warns that it used to run back as well, but no longer does. The quiet carries that weight.

### Side characters with their own wants

The bathhouse is crowded with characters drawn in one or two strokes, the method of *Memorable side characters*. Lin, the brusque older worker who calls Chihiro slow and then looks after her. Kamaji, who grumbles and then gives her the train tickets he has saved for forty years. Yubaba, greedy and doting on her giant baby; her twin sister Zeniba, who turns out to be gentle.

No-Face is the most interesting. He has a want of his own: to be noticed and accepted, above all by Chihiro. He offers gold, and the bathhouse staff, who want gold, give him whatever he asks. Chihiro is the only one who refuses it. A side character becomes the test of what the heroine values.

## The shape

Critics have not settled on one framework for the film. The closest fit is Campbell's three movements (see *Joseph Campbell & Christopher Vogler*), though with a heroine who wins by service rather than combat:

| Stage | What happens | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| Separation | The car, the tunnel, the empty town; the parents become pigs | Opening to about an eighth |
| Threshold and contract | Haku; Kamaji's boiler room; Yubaba gives her work and takes her name | About an eighth to a third |
| Initiation: tasks | The stink spirit; the gold rush; No-Face swells | About two-fifths to two-thirds |
| Crisis | Haku wounded; Chihiro feeds him the dumpling and decides to go to Zeniba | About two-thirds |
| The journey out | The train ride; Zeniba's cottage | About three-quarters |
| Return | Flight on Haku; remembering his name; the pig test; the tunnel | Last sixth |

The shape is less a rising fight than a growing list of debts and kindnesses that pay out at the end. Some critics have read Miyazaki's films through kishōtenketsu (see *Kishōtenketsu*): Francesca Arnavas and Mattia Bellini, in the journal *Narrative Works* (2023), discuss *Spirited Away* as a hybrid of Western tropes and the four-part pattern, built more around a riddle to solve than a conflict to win. It's a suggestive reading, not one Miyazaki himself has proposed, and the film's late, quiet turn on the train is the part that fits it best.

## What people said

**Miyazaki** has described his working method many times. He doesn't write a finished screenplay first; the story develops as he draws the storyboards, and production begins while those boards are still being made, so that he doesn't know at the start exactly how the story will end. He has called this a dangerous way to make an animated film. He has also said that he made *Spirited Away* with the ten-year-old daughters of friends in mind, wanting a heroine who was an ordinary girl rather than a special one.

**Roger Ebert** reviewed it on release in 2002 and later added it to his Great Movies series, calling it one of the finest animated films ever made. His interview with Miyazaki, quoted above on *ma*, is one of the most cited conversations with the director in English.

**The dissent** was mild, but there was some. Total Film's review complained that the plot sprawls too randomly before it moves forward, and the *Orlando Weekly*, in 2002, thought its two hours and five minutes too long for the medium. Both point at the same thing the film's admirers praise: its willingness to wander and to pause.

## Watch for

- **The car** (opening minutes): Chihiro's posture in the back seat, and the bouquet with the farewell card. Compare her body language with the last scene.
- **The parents eating** (about a tenth in): what greed looks like, and how calm the film is about it.
- **The outside staircase** (about a fifth in): terror, effort and a run she can't stop.
- **The contract** (about a third in): Yubaba lifting the characters of Chihiro's name off the page, leaving "Sen".
- **The stink spirit** (a little under halfway): the whole bathhouse working together on a rope.
- **No-Face and the gold** (about three-fifths): who takes the gold, and who doesn't.
- **The train** (about three-quarters): watch with no distractions and time how long nothing is said.
- **The pig test** (final minutes): how she knows, and that she doesn't look back.

## Try it

Write a scene of *ma*: a pause that lets your character, and the audience, absorb what has happened. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Find the point in your work-in-progress just before the climax or the final turn.
2. Write a scene in which the character travels, waits or sits, and nothing new happens. No new information, no conflict.
3. Fill it with three or four precise sensory details from the world of the story, and one small sign that the character is different from who they were at the start.

- **Stage:** Write a passage of stage directions with no lines: a character alone, a light change, a sound. How long can you hold it before the audience grows restless?
- **Screen:** Write a sequence of three or four shots with no dialogue, and note the sound for each one.
- **Journalism:** In a feature, find the moment your subject was waiting (a bus, a hospital corridor, a queue). Write it in a short, slow paragraph without quotes, before the piece's last turn.

**Check:** If you cut the pause, would the ending land as hard? If the answer is yes, the pause isn't doing its job.
