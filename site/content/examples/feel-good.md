## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole series, including the ending.*

*Feel Good* is a romantic comedy-drama created and written by Mae Martin and Joe Hampson, starring Martin as a version of themself. It ran for two series of six half-hour episodes: the first on Channel 4 and Netflix in March 2020, the second, its last, on Netflix in June 2021. Mae, a Canadian stand-up comic in London and a recovering drug addict, falls for George (Charlotte Ritchie), an English woman who has only ever dated men and who doesn't tell her friends about Mae. Series 1 is the love affair and Mae's relapse; series 2 turns to what Mae has been running from, a past in Toronto that began when they were a teenager on the comedy circuit, and to questions about gender that the first series only touched. Lisa Kudrow plays Mae's mother, Linda.

## Why it works

### Love as the new drug

The show's central idea is in its premise. Mae is clean from drugs when the story starts, but not from addiction. Channel 4 approached Martin after a stand-up show about addictive behaviour, and the series treats falling in love the same way that show did: as the next thing to get hooked on. Early in series 1, Mae moves fast, needs constant reassurance, and fixes on George with an intensity the show plays as both romantic and alarming.

*Want, need and wound* separates what a character wants from what they need. Mae wants George, completely and immediately. What they need is to stop using people and substances to avoid something older. The romance looks like the cure and is actually the symptom, and the series lets us enjoy the romance while slowly understanding that. When George pulls back, late in series 1, Mae relapses. The relapse isn't a plot twist; it's what the first episodes have been quietly setting up.

### Two people hiding different things

George's secret is Mae. She keeps the relationship from her friends and family, and the early episodes get comedy from the gap between her private warmth and her public evasions. Mae's secret is everything before London. Each of them wants the other to come out, in a different sense: George to say who she's with, Mae to say what happened to them.

*The cast as a system* describes the foil: a character who makes the protagonist's traits visible by contrast. George is Mae's foil, and Mae is George's. Mae can say anything about sex and nothing about their past; George can talk easily about anything except who she loves. Each scene between them tests which secret will give first. It's why the romance holds up over two short series: the barrier is inside both of them, not imposed from outside (see *Genre as a promise* on romance and its barrier).

### The stage as the place where pain becomes a story

Mae and George meet at the comedy club where Mae performs; Mae's set goes over badly, but one person in the audience laughs, and it's George. The club becomes home base. On stage, Mae tells funny versions of things the series shows us unfunny.

*Point of view and voice* asks who tells a story and how far we can trust them. *Feel Good* has two narrators: the Mae on stage, controlling the material and the laughs, and the series around them, which shows what the stage version leaves out. Compare *Phoebe Waller-Bridge*: Fleabag's asides to camera are a performance of being fine, and *Feel Good* uses stand-up the same way. The difference is that Mae's performance is literally their job, so the question of whether to turn pain into material is one they face professionally, every night. *Comedy structure* is useful here too: Bergson's observation that laughter needs a little detachment explains why Mae's jokes work best about things they've stopped feeling, and why the show's least funny scenes are the ones they can't joke about yet.

### Withholding the wound across a whole series

From the start of series 2, the show makes it clear that something went wrong in Mae's teenage years with an older man called Scott. The series doesn't explain at once. As Mae, in recovery again, tries to make amends to friends and to understand their own reactions, the past arrives in pieces. Mae, like Martin in real life, started on the Toronto comedy circuit as a young teenager. Scott, it emerges, was a much older comedian who housed Mae at fifteen and took advantage of them.

*Story vs. telling* explains why this order matters. Told chronologically, Mae's story would begin with the harm and follow the damage. Told this way, we meet the damage first (the addiction, the fierce need for George) and only later learn where it came from. When the truth arrives, everything in series 1 reads differently. In the penultimate episode, Mae, George and Phil (George's housemate, an unlikely friend) fly to Toronto so that Mae can face it. In the confrontation, Scott tries to make Mae feel the abuse was their fault, and the scene doesn't hand Mae a clean victory. As in *Want, need and wound*'s warning about backstory, the wound isn't a twist; it's an explanation that complicates rather than excuses.

### Gender as a question, not an announcement

Series 1 lets Mae's uncertainty about gender sit in the background. Series 2 brings it into the open, in conversations where Mae tries to say what they don't yet have words for. The show doesn't stage a single coming-out scene or hand Mae a label at the end. Martin has said in interviews since that they use they/them pronouns, and has described the show as very close to the truth of their life.

*Who gets to be the subject* asks whose experiences become stories, and on whose terms. *Feel Good* answers by keeping the subject in control: the person whose gender is being explored is also the writer and the actor. The effect for an audience is unusual intimacy. We're not watching a character be explained; we're watching them work it out.

### A planned ending in twelve episodes

Martin and Hampson planned the series as two runs from the start. When Netflix announced the second series as the last, Martin said they were excited to take Mae and George to the conclusion they'd always planned, and Charlotte Ritchie said she'd known about the two-series trajectory. Martin has cited the British habit of short runs (*The Office* stopped after two series), and Hampson's insistence on it.

*Television structure* explains how most series need an engine that keeps generating stories. *Feel Good* doesn't want one. Its engine is one relationship and one wound, and it closes both. The final episode brings Mae and George to Mae's parents' house in Toronto and ends with the couple together and steadier, without pretending everything is solved.

## The shape

The two series make a two-part movement: the first about getting hooked, the second about getting clean, in every sense.

| Part | Episodes | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Falling | Series 1, episodes 1–2 | The comedy club; Mae and George get together fast; George keeps it secret |
| Strain | Series 1, episodes 3–4 | George's secrecy and Mae's need pull against each other; Mae's parents and past start to surface |
| Relapse | Series 1, episodes 5–6 | George pulls back; Mae uses again; George asks Mae back |
| Apart | Series 2, episodes 1–2 | Mae in recovery, making amends; the two apart |
| Facing the past | Series 2, episodes 3–5 | Scott comes into focus; the trip to Toronto; the confrontation |
| Ending | Series 2, episode 6 | Mae's family home; Mae and George together, on firmer ground |

This is close to Booker's "rebirth" (see *Christopher Booker*): a character under a dark spell, a crisis, release. The difference is that *Feel Good* gives the spell two names, drugs and love, and shows that breaking one doesn't break the other.

## What people said

**Mae Martin** has explained in interviews that Channel 4 approached them after a stand-up show about addictive behaviour, and that the project got moving only once they brought in Joe Hampson, with whom they found a shared sense of humour. Martin has said the characters include composites of people both writers had dated, and that the show stays very close to the truth of Martin's life without being a documentary of it.

**Critics** loved the second series especially. Rebecca Nicholson in *The Guardian* (2021) called it a deadpan delight and praised its balance of comedy and darkness. *Rolling Stone*'s review (2021) said the series ended with more of an ellipsis than a full stop, because there was so much to cover in so few episodes: a compliment and a reservation at once.

**Dissent** came mostly with series 1. *The Arts Desk* (2020) found it fresh and bingeable but mild for something that digs so deep, and questioned how funny it really was; *Metro*'s reviewer found the romance underpowered. The most common complaint was the same in both cases: a show that sets itself up as a comedy and repeatedly chooses the drama.

## Watch for

- **The first gig** (series 1, episode 1, early): a set that goes badly, and the one person laughing.
- **George with her friends** (series 1, early episodes): how she introduces Mae, and what Mae does with their face when she does.
- **Maggie** (series 1): Sophie Thompson as the woman from Narcotics Anonymous who declares herself Mae's sponsor. Comic, and a warning about what recovery can look like.
- **The relapse** (series 1, episodes 5–6): how the show stops being funny, and how quickly.
- **Linda** (both series): Lisa Kudrow's deadpan mother. Notice how much of her love comes out as criticism, and what that taught Mae.
- **Making amends** (series 2, early): Mae laying down rules for themself in a bar.
- **The confrontation with Scott** (series 2, near the end): watch how he turns the conversation around, and how Mae resists.
- **The last scenes** (series 2, episode 6): an ending that stops rather than resolves.

## Try it

Write the stand-up version and the real version. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Take a painful event from your work-in-progress (or, carefully, from your own life).
2. Write two minutes of stand-up about it: the version where the teller controls the laughs.
3. Then write the scene the stand-up is covering for, with no jokes. Let it be awkward.
4. Mark the one detail the stand-up version left out. That's your scene's centre.

- **Stage:** Put both versions in the same play: the character addresses the audience as a comic, then the scene happens behind them or after them.
- **Screen:** Cross-cut the set and the scene. Decide whether the audience laughter continues over the scene, and what that does.
- **Journalism:** In a personal essay, write the version you tell at parties, then the version you've never told. Use the second; keep one line from the first to show the gap.

**Check:** Does the joke version make the real version hurt more, not less?
