## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*Nights of Cabiria* (*Le notti di Cabiria*, 1957) was directed by Federico Fellini, who wrote it with Ennio Flaiano and Tullio Pinelli, with Pier Paolo Pasolini helping on the Roman street dialogue; Nino Rota wrote the music. Giulietta Masina, Fellini's wife, plays Cabiria, a small, fierce, hopeful prostitute who works the edges of Rome and owns a tiny house of her own on the outskirts. The film follows her through a string of encounters, each of which ends with her hope betrayed, until the last betrayal nearly kills her. Masina won Best Actress at Cannes, and the film won the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film; it later became the Broadway musical *Sweet Charity* (1966).

## Why it works

### An introduction that is the whole film in miniature

The film opens with Cabiria and her boyfriend Giorgio running and laughing across open ground to a riverbank. At the water's edge he snatches her handbag and shoves her in. She can't swim. Some boys pull her out, half-drowned. Her first reaction when she comes round is not grief but fury, and her second is to refuse to believe Giorgio meant it.

*Introducing a character* says the first action should be a thesis the rest of the story will test. Cabiria's first scene is exactly that: a moment of joy, a betrayal for money, a near-death, and a recovery driven by stubborn hope. Every later episode repeats this pattern, and the ending repeats it almost exactly, on a cliff above a lake instead of a riverbank. By the time the end comes, we've been told the shape twice before we know we've been told it.

### Episodes, held together by a person

Structurally the film is a picaresque, in the sense of *The quest and the picaresque*: a low-born heroine moving through a series of separate encounters that tour her society from bottom to top. A film star, Alberto Lazzari (Amedeo Nazzari), picks her up on the Via Veneto after a row with his girlfriend, takes her to his villa, and then hides her in the bathroom all night when the girlfriend comes back. A religious procession to a shrine of the Madonna, where Cabiria prays for her life to change, ends in a drunken picnic where she rages that nothing has changed. At a variety theatre a stage hypnotist puts her under, and she acts out a girlish romance in front of a jeering audience. Outside, a quiet man named Oscar (François Périer) introduces himself, and says he was moved.

*Aristotle* calls the worst plots episodic: one thing after another, with no necessity linking them. The lesson on the picaresque raises the obvious problem, which is momentum: why keep watching after the third episode? Fellini's answer is the one the lesson calls a moral journey inside the physical one. The episodes don't cause each other, but each one tests the same thing, her ability to hope, and each test is harder. The order matters even where the causes don't.

### Repetition as rising action

There is a real chain under the episodes, and it's built from repeated betrayals. Giorgio steals her bag. Lazzari uses her and puts her out at dawn. The Madonna doesn't answer. The audience laughs at her. Then Oscar, unlike all the others, courts her slowly and respectfully, and the film lets us hope with her. She sells her house and withdraws all her savings to marry him.

On a walk at sunset, on a wooded cliff over a lake, Oscar grows silent and strange. She realizes, slowly, that he means to take her money and push her over. She throws the money at his feet and begs him to kill her. He grabs the bag and runs.

*Kurt Vonnegut*'s story graphs are the clearest way to see this. Each episode is a small rise and fall. The Oscar episode is the biggest rise in the film, so the fall from it is the deepest. The repetitions do the work that escalation does in a conventional plot: we know the pattern so well by now that Oscar's goodness becomes the suspense.

### A face that carries the film

Masina's face, with its big eyes, mobile mouth and sudden swings between bravado and hurt, is the film's real special effect. Critics have often compared her to Chaplin's tramp: a small figure in a too-big world, who keeps getting knocked down and keeps getting up. In the hypnotist scene her expression moves from suspicion to innocence to something like rapture, while the theatre audience laughs. *The face and the cut* explains why this works: the meaning of the scene lives in the reaction, and here Fellini cuts between her face and the jeering crowd, so we see both her dignity and how she looks to them.

*Tragedy and comedy* is the other lesson here. The film is a tragicomedy in John Fletcher's sense: no one dies, but Cabiria comes near death twice, at the beginning and at the end. Her swagger, her quarrels with the other women, her funny little walk, all make us laugh, and the laughter makes the cruelty land harder.

### The look at the camera

After Oscar runs, Cabiria lies sobbing in the leaves. Then she gets up and walks out onto a road at dusk, and a group of young people comes along it, playing music, singing and dancing as they walk. They swirl around her. One girl greets her with a cheerful good evening. Cabiria, her face smeared with tears and make-up, begins to smile. And for a moment she looks straight into the lens, at us, before the film ends.

Two things happen at once. The music, which we see the young people playing, is *diegetic*, in the language of *Sound inside and outside the story*: it belongs to her world, not to a score telling us how to feel. Life has simply walked up the road and offered her a song. And the look at the camera breaks the fourth wall, the device that *Mood on stage* calls direct address and that *Phoebe Waller-Bridge* turned into a whole series. In *Fleabag* the glance is an invitation into a joke. Here it is closer to a question. She has survived again; what about us?

## The shape

The film is a picaresque in episodes, with a hidden rising line under them. *Gustav Freytag*'s pyramid doesn't fit the episodes, but it fits the repeated betrayals once you see them as a single pattern escalating.

| Part | What happens | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| Thesis | Giorgio pushes her into the river and takes her bag | First few minutes |
| Episodes | Her house and her friend Wanda; the Via Veneto and the film star's villa | About a tenth to a third |
| Faith tested | The procession to the shrine; the drunken picnic | About a third to a half |
| Humiliation | The hypnotist; Oscar appears outside | About half |
| False rise | Oscar's courtship; she sells the house and draws out her savings | About half to five-sixths |
| Reversal | The cliff at sunset; Oscar takes the money and runs | About five-sixths |
| Recovery | The road, the musicians, the look at the camera | Final minutes |

The opening and the reversal are the same event staged twice, the move *Jane Alison* calls a spiral: a return to the same centre at a higher level, so the second time means more.

## What people said

**Federico Fellini** built the character from Masina's brief appearance as a prostitute called Cabiria in his earlier film *The White Sheik* (1952). He brought in Pasolini, then a young writer known for his novels of Rome's slums, to help with the street dialogue and the world of the periphery. One episode, in which Cabiria meets a man who quietly carries food at night to homeless people living in caves outside the city, was cut before release; it was restored when the film was re-released in 1998.

**The critics** made Masina the centre of almost every review. Her Cannes prize came on the film's premiere, and her performance is still widely ranked among the great screen performances. Roger Ebert, in his Great Movies essay, gave the final shot special attention, describing her face in it as indomitably resolved. A Criterion essay links that final look to the end of Chaplin's *City Lights* (1931), where the tramp, recognized at last, looks out with the same mix of hurt and hope.

**The dissent** came mainly on release. Bosley Crowther's review in the *New York Times* was mixed: he admired the film's small, observed details but was put off by its setting, and some American reviewers were uneasy with a heroine who was a prostitute.

## Watch for

- **The river** (first few minutes): how quickly joy turns to betrayal, and how stubbornly she refuses to accept it.
- **The film star's villa** (about a fifth in): Cabiria shut in the bathroom, watching a life she'll never have through the door.
- **The procession** (about a third in): her prayer, and then her anger afterward that nothing changed.
- **The hypnotist** (about half in): her face under hypnosis, intercut with the audience laughing.
- **Selling the house** (about four-fifths in): what she gives up, and her goodbye to Wanda.
- **The cliff** (about five-sixths in): the light going, Oscar's silence, the moment she understands.
- **The road** (final minutes): the young musicians, the girl's greeting, the smile, the look at us.

## Try it

Write a character's introduction that already contains their ending. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Choose a character with a habit of hope, or trust, or some other virtue that keeps getting them hurt.
2. Write their first scene in under a page: a small joy, a betrayal, and how they get up.
3. Now sketch the final scene as a near-repeat of the first: the same pattern, higher stakes, a different place. Change only one thing: how they get up.

- **Stage:** Stage the first and last scenes in the same spot on the set, so the audience's memory does the comparing.
- **Screen:** End on a held close-up. Decide whether the character looks into the lens or just past it, and what each choice asks of the audience.
- **Journalism:** In a profile, find the small early incident in your subject's life that matches the big one you're writing about. Open with it, briefly, and let the reader recognize it later without your pointing it out.

**Check:** If a reader saw only your first and last scenes, would they see both the pattern and the change?
