## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*Sorry, Baby* (2025) was written and directed by Eva Victor, who also plays the lead. It premiered at the Sundance Film Festival in January 2025, where it won the Waldo Salt Screenwriting Award in the U.S. Dramatic competition, closed the Directors' Fortnight at Cannes that May, and was released by A24 that summer. Agnes (Victor) is a literature academic who has stayed on in the small college town where she did her graduate degree Her best friend from those years, Lydie (Naomi Ackie), has moved away. The film follows several years of her life after she was sexually assaulted by her thesis adviser, an event the film mostly calls "the bad thing". Because it's recent, this case study is shorter than most and sticks to what reviews and interviews confirm.

## Why it works

### Chapters out of order

The film is divided into chapters, each introduced by a title card that begins "The Year with…" and ends with something as vague as "the bad thing" or as small as a good sandwich. They don't run in chronological order. The film opens with "The Year with the Baby": Lydie visits Agnes years after their graduate school days, the two are instantly easy and funny together, and before she leaves Lydie reveals she's pregnant. Only then does the film go back to the year of the assault.

*Story vs. telling* gives the terms: the fabula runs from the assault to the baby, but the syuzhet starts near the end. That choice matters. We meet Agnes first as a person (dry, quick, slightly stuck) rather than as a victim, and we sense something is wrong before we know what. The chapter cards also do what Trier's do in *The Worst Person in the World*: they make the jumps in time honest, and their deadpan wording sets the tone. Victor told interviewers the story was always meant to be non-linear; reviewers have read the scrambled order as a picture of healing, which doesn't go in a straight line.

### Not showing the assault

The film doesn't show the assault. Agnes, then a graduate student, goes to her adviser's house, and the film doesn't follow her inside. We learn what happened when she tells Lydie afterwards, and much of the time it's simply "the bad thing".

*Want, need and wound* warns about the trauma plot, the critic Parul Sehgal's name for stories that withhold a past injury and then reveal it as the key to a character. *Sorry, Baby* refuses the reveal and refuses the spectacle. The wound isn't a secret to be unlocked; it's a fact Agnes has to live around, at work, in bed, at a doctor's office, in the town. The *New Statesman* (2025) went further, framing its essay on the film as a sign that the trauma plot's moment may be ending.

### Comedy as a way of telling the truth

The register is the film's boldest choice. It's a comedy-drama: many scenes are funny, some very funny, and the jokes sit right next to fear. Victor told NPR (2025) that they don't really know how to write a straight drama.

*Tragedy and comedy* and *Comedy structure* explain how comedy works through incongruity and status. Victor uses both. Agnes deals with institutions (a university administration, a jury selection, a doctor) that respond to what happened with forms and procedure, and the absurdity of that is played straight, which makes it both funnier and more damning. One of the film's most moving scenes is with a stranger: when Agnes has a panic attack while driving, a sandwich-shop owner, Pete (John Carroll Lynch), helps her calm down. Kindness arrives from the side, without speeches. *Sight & Sound*'s review summed up the method in its headline: the film wears its jokes like armour.

### A friendship as the spine

Lydie is the film's other lead. Her visits frame the story, and the friendship's changes (Lydie moving on and starting a family while Agnes stays put) are the closest thing the film has to a plot. *The cast as a system* says each character should answer the story's question differently. Lydie is the life Agnes might have had; Gavin (Lucas Hedges), a neighbour Agnes starts sleeping with, is a gentler, more awkward version of intimacy; Pete is the kindness of strangers. None of them fixes her.

### The title, at the end

The title doesn't make sense until the last scene. Agnes holds Lydie's baby and talks to her, almost as if to her own younger self, and apologizes for the bad things that are going to happen to her in life. Victor has said the title was something they rewarded themselves with once the script was written (*Little White Lies*, 2025). *Frank Kermode & Peter Brooks* explain how an ending reorganizes everything before it; here three words, finally spoken, turn a private injury into something passed between generations, and a comedy into a promise of protection.

## The shape

No conventional arc fits, and the film doesn't try. Its shape is closer to the episodic structures in *The quest and the picaresque*, each chapter a self-contained stretch of life, held together by the friendship.

| Section | What happens | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| "The Year with the Baby" | Lydie visits; Agnes's present-day life; the pregnancy | Opening quarter |
| The year of "the bad thing" | Graduate school; the adviser; the assault, told afterwards | About a quarter to half |
| The years after | Institutions, the panic attack and Pete, Gavin, jury duty | About half to the last fifth |
| Return to the present | Lydie's baby; Agnes speaks to her | Final minutes |

## What people said

**Eva Victor** discussed the film widely in 2025. NPR headlined its interview with a line from them:

> I tried to create a film that I feel like I needed.
— Eva Victor, interview with NPR (2025)

Victor, who had been known for online comedy writing and for acting in the series *Billions*, has said the events of the film are close to their own experience.

**The critics** were overwhelmingly warm. *Time Out* gave it five stars; NPR's review praised how Agnes sidesteps the clichés of trauma at every turn; *Sight & Sound* admired how it balances dark humour with real melancholy. The reservations were small but real: Wendy Ide in *The Observer* (2025) admired it as a debut but found parts of it too heavy-handed.

## Watch for

- **The title cards** (throughout): how each one sets the tone before the chapter begins.
- **Lydie's visit** (opening chapter): how the two friends move and talk before we know anything about the past.
- **The jump back** (about a quarter in): what we now understand about the Agnes we just met.
- **The house** (the assault chapter): where the film chooses to leave us, and what it does instead of showing.
- **Pete** (middle of the film): the sandwich-shop scene, and how little he needs to say.
- **The ending** (final minutes): Agnes and the baby, and the line that names the film.

## Try it

Write a short piece about something painful in a character's past, and tell it without showing it. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Choose the event, and give it a plain nickname the character would use instead of its real name.
2. Write three short sections, out of order, each titled "The Year with…" plus something small or odd. Make the first one the latest in time.
3. In the middle section, stop at the door: don't show the event. Show the next morning, or the conversation in which the character tells a friend.

- **Stage:** Have the character tell the event to a friend downstage while the place where it happened stays dark behind them.
- **Screen:** Write the title cards first and read them in order. Do they alone tell the shape of a life?
- **Journalism:** In a piece about a survivor, organize the story around the years after, in the survivor's own words, rather than reconstructing the event.

**Check:** Does the reader understand what happened without ever seeing it, and is the character more than what happened to them?
