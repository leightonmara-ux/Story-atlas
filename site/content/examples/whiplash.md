## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*Whiplash* (2014) was written and directed by Damien Chazelle, who drew on his own years as a drummer in a fiercely competitive high-school jazz band. Andrew Neiman (Miles Teller), a nineteen-year-old first-year drummer at a prestigious New York music conservatory, is recruited into the studio band run by Terence Fletcher (J. K. Simmons), a conductor whose method is humiliation, fear and physical intimidation. Andrew wants to be one of the greats, and Fletcher claims he wants the same for him. The film, shot quickly on a small budget, premiered at Sundance in 2014, where it won both the Grand Jury Prize and the Audience Award for drama, and went on to win three Oscars.

## Why it works

### A two-hander locked to one goal

The film is essentially two people. Andrew's father (Paul Reiser), his girlfriend Nicole (Melissa Benoist) and the other drummers are there to be pushed aside. *The cast as a system* explains why the pairing is so tight: the best opponent wants the same thing as the hero. Fletcher and Andrew both say they want greatness, and both believe it requires suffering. That's the bond that stops either walking away, Egri's unity of opposites. Fletcher can't simply be quit, because quitting him means giving up the goal.

The opening shot sets the pairing up. A long, dark corridor; at the end, Andrew alone at a drum kit, practising. The camera pushes slowly toward him. Then Fletcher is in the doorway. He asks him to play, criticizes, leaves, comes back for his jacket. The relationship's whole pattern, attention given and withdrawn, is there before either character has a name.

Fletcher also fits McKee's principle of antagonism: the story is only as strong as the forces against the hero. Simmons plays him with a terrifying precision that is also funny, which makes him harder to dismiss. In the first rehearsal he chats warmly with Andrew in a break, asks about his family, then minutes later throws a chair at his head and slaps him in front of the band to teach him whether he's rushing or dragging the tempo.

### Escalation: every scene costs more

The structure is a ladder. Each time Andrew climbs a rung, the price rises, and *Want, need and wound* helps name what is being paid. His want is to be great. What he gives up for it is everything else.

The ladder, in order: he gets into the studio band, as the alternate; he takes the core seat after a rival's sheet music goes missing at a competition and Andrew, who has memorized the piece, plays it; he breaks up with Nicole, in a pizza restaurant, explaining that she'd hold him back; at a family dinner, he sneers at his cousins' sporting achievements and says he'd rather die broke and drunk at 34 and be talked about than live a long life no one remembers; he practises until his hands bleed into the drum skin and he plunges them into iced water; he wins back his seat in an hours-long tryout against two other drummers, the tempo pushed faster and faster until there is blood on the cymbals. Each step is plausible from inside, and each sacrifices something real. By the car crash (Andrew, late for a competition, is hit by a truck, crawls out of the wreck and runs to the venue bleeding to play), the escalation has become literal violence.

### Cut like a fight

Tom Cross won the Oscar for Best Film Editing, and *Editing* is the lesson to watch the film with. Cross has said that Chazelle wanted the playing and practice scenes to feel brutal, like the boxing in *Raging Bull*, and the two planned the editing closely in advance (they had worked out much of it on the short version Chazelle made first).

The musical scenes are cut on the beat, often in very short shots: valve, spit, sweat, cymbal, Fletcher's hand, Andrew's eyes. The cut itself becomes percussion. But *Editing* also says pace is variation, not speed. The film knows when to stop cutting: when Fletcher stands over Andrew counting the tempo, the shots lengthen, the music stops, and the silence becomes the threat. Murch's rule of six ranks emotion first, rhythm third; *Whiplash* is a rare film where rhythm and emotion are the same thing, because the emotion is about rhythm.

### Sound you can feel

*Sound inside and outside the story* and *Music and silence* matter here because almost all the music is diegetic: the band is playing it, in the room. When Fletcher stops the band with a raised fist, the cut to silence is as much an event as a slap. Drums are close-miked so that sticks and cymbals feel physical, and the film's sound mixing also won an Oscar.

### The final performance as climax

After the crash, Andrew attacks Fletcher on stage and is expelled. He gives anonymous testimony about a former student, Sean Casey, whose suicide Fletcher had told the band was a car accident, and Fletcher is dismissed. Months later Andrew finds Fletcher playing piano in a jazz club. They talk almost civilly. Fletcher tells the story of Jo Jones throwing a cymbal at the young Charlie Parker, and says that no two words are more harmful than "good job". Then he invites Andrew to drum with his new band at a festival.

On stage, Fletcher reveals that he knows who testified, and counts in a piece Andrew has never been given the music for. Andrew fumbles, is humiliated, walks off, and is hugged by his father in the wings. Then he turns around, sits back down, and starts playing "Caravan" before Fletcher can announce anything else, cueing the band himself. The film ends on an extended solo: Fletcher's fury turns into attention, then into a conductor's collaboration, and finally a nod and a small smile between them. Cut to black.

*Frank Kermode & Peter Brooks* talk about the ending that gives meaning to everything before it. This one is designed to split audiences. As a performance, it's triumph: the want is achieved, on stage, in front of witnesses. As a story, the abuser's method has just been proved right in the one way Fletcher cares about.

### What does the film endorse?

*Whose side are we on?* separates alignment from allegiance. We are aligned with Andrew throughout, and Simmons's charisma gives Fletcher a pull of his own. The film never shows us anyone succeeding by another route, which is why critics argued over whether it endorses Fletcher's method. Chazelle's own position, given in interviews after release, is that the ending is not a happy one: he has said he imagines Andrew as a hollowed-out person who dies of an overdose in his thirties, an echo of the boast at the family dinner. The film gives you the thrill and leaves the bill unstated.

## The shape

*Syd Field*'s paradigm fits closely, which is part of why the film moves so fast at 106 minutes.

| Point | What happens | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| Setup | The corridor; Andrew's life (father, cinema, Nicole) | Opening to about an eighth |
| Plot Point I | Fletcher brings Andrew into the studio band | About an eighth in |
| Confrontation | The first rehearsal; the core seat; the breakup; the dinner; bleeding hands | About an eighth to two-thirds |
| Midpoint | Andrew takes the core seat at the competition | About two-fifths in |
| Plot Point II | The car crash, the failed performance, the attack on Fletcher | About two-thirds to three-quarters |
| Resolution | Expulsion; testimony; the jazz club; the festival performance | The last quarter |

Notice how much of Act III is still conflict. Field's "resolution as solution" is half-delivered: the musical problem is solved; the human one is not.

## What people said

**Damien Chazelle** has said the film came from his time as a teenage drummer under a demanding band director, and from the fear he felt then. Unable to finance the feature, he shot one scene, with Simmons, as a short film that played at Sundance in 2013, which helped raise the money. In interviews after release he described the ending as, in effect, the saddest happy ending he could imagine.

**Tom Cross** has discussed the editing in craft interviews, including the *Raging Bull* comparison and the way the short worked as a test for the feature. **J. K. Simmons** won the Academy Award for Best Supporting Actor.

**The critics** were mostly enthusiastic, but the sharpest dissent came from people who knew jazz. **Richard Brody**, in *The New Yorker* (October 2014), argued that the film gets jazz wrong: its idea of music is competition and speed, with no sense of the music's history or its joys; Andrew's idol is Buddy Rich, a technical showman rather than a major jazz inspiration; and the Charlie Parker story Fletcher tells is distorted (in accounts of the incident, Jones threw the cymbal toward Parker's feet, not at his head). Musicians have made similar points since, arguing that it's a film about obsession that happens to use jazz. Others replied that the film is about the myths musicians tell, and Fletcher's version of the Parker story is one of them.

## Watch for

- **The corridor** (first minute): the slow push-in, and Fletcher appearing in the doorway.
- **The first rehearsal** (about a sixth in): the friendly break, then the chair and the slaps. Count the shots during the tempo test.
- **"Not quite my tempo"** (same scene): how the silences between Fletcher's counts are edited.
- **The family dinner** (around halfway): Andrew's boast about dying young. Hold on to the age.
- **The tryout** (about three-fifths in): three drummers, one seat, the tempo climbing. Watch the cuts get shorter.
- **The car crash** (about two-thirds in): how abruptly it arrives, mid-phone-call, with no warning in the cutting.
- **The jazz club** (about four-fifths in): Fletcher's speech about "good job". Listen to how reasonable he sounds.
- **The final solo** (last ten minutes): the cutting between hands, sticks and faces, and the moment Fletcher's expression changes.

## Try it

Write a two-hander escalation: two people chasing the same goal, five scenes, each costing more than the last. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Pick two characters who want the same thing (a title, a role, a story, a child's success), and decide which of them believes suffering is the price.
2. Write one line for each of five scenes. In each, the protagonist gains something and loses something. Make each loss bigger than the last.
3. Write the last scene in full, so that the protagonist wins in the opponent's terms, and the reader isn't sure whether to cheer.

- **Stage:** Keep it to one room, such as a rehearsal room or office, and let the opponent control the room's rhythm: when people speak, sit, start and stop.
- **Screen:** Mark in the script where the cutting should speed up and where it should stop dead. Put your biggest threat in a silence.
- **Journalism:** In a profile of a demanding mentor (coach, editor, chef), structure the piece as a ladder of escalating demands, and give the last word to someone who paid the price rather than someone who succeeded.

**Check:** Does your final scene work as both a triumph and an indictment, depending on what the reader values?
