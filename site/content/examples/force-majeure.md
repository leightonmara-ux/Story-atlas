## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*Force Majeure* (*Turist*, 2014) was written and directed by the Swedish filmmaker Ruben Östlund, photographed by Fredrik Wenzel and shot at the ski resort of Les Arcs in the French Alps. A Swedish family, Tomas (Johannes Kuhnke), Ebba (Lisa Loven Kongsli) and their two children, Vera and Harry, are on a five-day skiing holiday. At lunch on a restaurant terrace a controlled avalanche seems to come straight at them; Ebba grabs the children, and Tomas grabs his phone and runs. The snow turns out to be harmless powder, nobody is hurt, and the rest of the film is about what that half-second has done to a marriage. It won the Jury Prize in the Un Certain Regard section at Cannes in 2014.

## Why it works

### An inciting incident that lasts a few seconds

Most inciting incidents are events: a body is found, a letter arrives. This one is a reflex. The avalanche comes about a tenth of the way in. Tomas is filming it on his phone, reassuring everyone that it's controlled; the cloud keeps coming; the terrace panics. When the white-out clears, Ebba is shielding the children and Tomas's chair is empty. A moment later he strolls back and sits down as if nothing happened.

Nothing outward has changed. Nobody is injured, the holiday continues, and the family goes skiing. What has changed is a value, in exactly the sense *Robert McKee* teaches: Tomas has gone from protector to coward in his wife's eyes, and possibly in his own, and every later scene is a negotiation over which of those he is. The cleverness is that the incident is unarguable on film (Ebba has the footage on his phone) and yet arguable in words, which is where the story lives.

### The comedy of the rigid male role

The film is very funny, and painfully so. Its comedy is the kind Henri Bergson describes in *Comedy structure*: we laugh when a living person behaves like a machine. Tomas cannot adapt. When Ebba tells the story at dinner to another guest and his date, he contradicts her, calmly insisting he sees it differently. When she raises it again with their friends, he holds the same line. He keeps reaching for the script of the reasonable husband, and it keeps failing to fit what everyone saw.

Östlund pushes this until it breaks. Late on, about two-thirds of the way through, Tomas collapses outside their room, sobbing so loudly and for so long that the children come out and cling to their parents. The scene is both genuinely distressing and ridiculous, and Östlund holds it until the laugh and the wince arrive together. *Tragedy and comedy* calls this a modern blend: the film keeps both contracts open and refuses to tell us which one we're watching.

### A second couple as a mirror

A little later the family dines with Mats (Kristofer Hivju), an old friend of Tomas's, and his much younger girlfriend Fanni (Fanni Metelius). Ebba tells the avalanche story again, then plays the phone video. Mats tries gallantly to defend Tomas: in a crisis, he argues, people don't act as they would like to, and perhaps Tomas was running to come back for them. It's a decent man's attempt to restore a friend's dignity, and it fails.

Then the infection spreads. Back in their own room, Fanni wonders aloud whether Mats would have run too, and the couple who came to comfort are soon arguing. This is what *The cast as a system* teaches: secondary characters are most useful as variations on the central question. Mats and Fanni show that the issue isn't Tomas alone. It's a shared, half-believed assumption about what men owe in a crisis, and once said aloud it can't be unsaid in any couple. A third character, a woman Ebba meets at the resort who is holidaying apart from her husband and children and is relaxed about an open marriage, gives Ebba another mirror: a mother who is frank about wanting freedom, which Ebba finds disturbing.

### Long, static takes that make us the witnesses

Östlund and Wenzel mostly hold the camera still, at a distance, for a long time. Arguments play out in wide shots and two-shots, often without cutting to close-ups or reaction shots. The hotel's corridors, bathrooms and the family's small room become boxes the characters can't escape, and we can't either. A recurring image shows the four of them in a row in the bathroom, brushing their teeth with electric toothbrushes, a picture of order that grows more absurd each time.

*The camera's eye* explains that a long take is worth its cost when continuity of time and space is itself the meaning. Here it is. Embarrassment depends on duration: the longer a scene refuses to cut away, the more we feel stuck in the room with people who can't escape each other. The static frame also turns us into an audience of bystanders, like the hotel worker who, in one scene, watches Tomas and Ebba argue from along the corridor. We are invited to judge, and then made uncomfortable about how much we enjoy it.

### Vivaldi as an alarm bell

At intervals, usually at transitions between scenes or days, the soundtrack bursts in with the stormy final movement of Vivaldi's "Summer" from *The Four Seasons*, in a hard, driving arrangement. The music doesn't feel with the characters, as most scores do; it sounds like an alarm, or a joke about how much drama a middle-class holiday can contain.

*Music and silence* calls a returning cue tied to an idea a leitmotif, and music that carries on in its own mood regardless of the scene anempathetic. This cue is both. Each time it returns it reminds us of the avalanche and its aftershocks, while its baroque energy pokes fun at the family's tidy, ordered life. Between bursts, the film relies heavily on ambient sound: the thud of the resort's avalanche-control explosions, the hum of ski lifts, wind. That contrast, between a near-silent world and a sudden storm of strings, makes the music land like a slap.

### Who gets our sympathy, and when

*Whose side are we on?* separates alignment (whom the film follows) from allegiance (whom we side with). *Force Majeure* is aligned with both spouses, and keeps shifting our allegiance. Early on, Ebba is plainly right, and Tomas's denial is maddening. But the longer she presses, the more she seems to be prosecuting rather than seeking repair, and Tomas's collapse makes him pitiable.

## The shape

The film is divided into five days, one chapter per day of the holiday, which gives it a natural five-part shape. It maps well onto *Gustav Freytag*'s pyramid, with the inciting incident much earlier than Freytag's model usually places it.

| Stage | What happens | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| Exposition | The family arrives; a resort photographer poses them for a picture; skiing | Opening to about a tenth |
| Inciting incident | The avalanche on the terrace; Tomas runs | About a tenth |
| Rising action | Ebba's two retellings at dinner; the phone video; Mats and Fanni's argument; Tomas and Mats ski together | About a fifth to three-fifths |
| Climax | Tomas's breakdown outside the room, with the children | About two-thirds |
| Falling action and resolution | The last day: Ebba lost in the fog and carried back by Tomas; the bus down the mountain | About the last fifth |

The resolution is double. On the last morning of skiing, in thick fog, Ebba goes missing and Tomas leaves the children to go and find her, coming back carrying her in his arms. The family seems restored. But the scene is so neat that many viewers suspect Ebba staged it to give him his role back. Then, on the shuttle bus down the mountain, the driver takes the hairpin bends so badly that Ebba panics and demands to be let off, and soon most of the passengers are walking down the road. This time it's the mother who flees. The film ends with the group trudging downhill together, and Tomas, as cigarettes are passed around, accepting one. Nothing is resolved; everyone has been shown to be fallible.

## What people said

**Ruben Östlund** said in interviews around the film's release that a YouTube video of tourists watching an avalanche come toward them was one starting point, and that his years making ski films meant he knew resorts well. He often said, half joking, that one of the film's aims was to raise the divorce rate; another was to stage the most spectacular avalanche in film history. He has also said the bus scene was inspired by a viral video of a reckless bus driver.

**The critics** were mostly enthusiastic. Variety's Cannes review called the avalanche the most talked-about shot of that year's festival. Peter Bradshaw, reviewing it for *The Guardian* on its UK release, found it compelling, intelligent and grimly entertaining despite some overstretching. Time Out treated it as a study of deflated masculinity. Several reviewers compared Östlund's coolness and long static shots to Michael Haneke's.

The dissent mostly targets the second half. Ryan Gilbey, in the *New Statesman* (2015), felt that the film's moral set-up came to resemble a dinner-party game the audience is manipulated into playing, and that its grip loosened as the story grew more outlandish. Other reviewers found the bus finale aimless or the ending deliberately evasive. An American remake, *Downhill* (2020), was far less admired.

## Watch for

- **The family photograph** (first few minutes): a resort photographer arranges them into the perfect family image. Hold it in mind.
- **The avalanche** (about a tenth in): watch Tomas's hands. Notice what he takes with him, and how he sits back down.
- **The first retelling** (about a fifth in): Ebba tells the story to strangers at dinner, and Tomas offers his version. Watch the strangers' faces.
- **The phone video** (about a third in): the moment the argument turns from interpretation to evidence.
- **Mats and Fanni's room** (shortly after): how the question jumps from one couple to another.
- **The toothbrushes** (recurring): the four of them in a row. Notice how the image changes in meaning.
- **The breakdown** (about two-thirds in): how long the shot is held, and when you start laughing, if you do.
- **The fog and the bus** (final fifth): ask whether Ebba's disappearance is real, and why the film gives her a panic of her own.

## Try it

Write a scene in which a character's three-second reflex becomes a question nobody can settle. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Pick a moment where someone reacts before they think: they flinch, laugh, look away, step back, or save themselves. Write it in as few lines as possible.
2. Write a second scene, days later, where someone else retells that moment to a third party, in front of the person who did it.
3. Give the person who acted one line of defence, and make it reasonable. Let the scene end without a verdict.

- **Stage:** Put the retelling at a dinner table with a guest who doesn't know them well. The guest's awkwardness is your comic engine; give them something to do with their hands.
- **Screen:** Shoot the retelling in one wide, static take. Write in the script where you would be tempted to cut to a close-up, and don't.
- **Journalism:** In a profile or feature, find a moment someone has been judged for (a decision in a crisis). Report two people's accounts of it, and show the subject's own account last, without ruling on it.

**Check:** Could a reader argue for both sides of the moment after your scene? If they can't, the reflex isn't ambiguous enough to carry a story.
