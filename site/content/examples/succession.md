## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole series, including the ending.*

*Succession* is a drama created by Jesse Armstrong for HBO, running four seasons and thirty-nine episodes from 2018 to 2023. Logan Roy (Brian Cox), the ageing founder of a media and entertainment conglomerate, won't name an heir, and his children, Kendall (Jeremy Strong), Roman (Kieran Culkin), Shiv (Sarah Snook) and the older half-brother Connor (Alan Ruck), spend four seasons scheming for the company and his approval, alongside Shiv's husband Tom (Matthew Macfadyen) and cousin Greg (Nicholas Braun). The course's lesson *Jesse Armstrong* covers the show's big ideas (an ensemble with no hero, comedy played straight, dialogue as combat, the writers' "alts", seasons that converge); this case study looks closely at two episodes, season 2's "Hunting" and season 4's "Connor's Wedding", and at how the final season is built around them.

## Why it works

### "Boar on the Floor": a game that escalates

Season 2, episode 3, "Hunting", takes Logan's senior executives to a hunting lodge in Hungary for a corporate retreat. Logan wants to buy a rival news organisation, Pierce, and someone inside the company has been leaking. Late in the episode, at dinner, he decides to find out who talked. He makes several of his executives, Tom and Greg among them, get down on the floor and crawl, calling out "boar on the floor", while he throws sausages for them to fight over.

*Comedy structure* teaches the "game of the scene": find the unusual thing, then heighten it. Watch how Armstrong's script does this. It starts as a joke at the table, the kind a powerful man makes and everyone laughs at. Then it becomes an instruction. Then the instruction gets worse, and no one can find the moment to refuse, because refusing would mark you as the leaker. Each step is small; the end point is grotesque. The comedy and the horror are the same mechanism, which is the point of *Tragedy and comedy*: whether we laugh depends on how far away we stand.

It's also a lesson in status (see *Dialogue reveals character*). Logan never raises his voice. The person with power sets the rules of the game, and everyone else has to decide in public how much dignity to give up. Tom, who is usually the bully in his scenes with Greg, is down on the carpet with him.

### A death heard, not seen

Season 4, episode 3, "Connor's Wedding", written by Armstrong and directed by Mark Mylod, opens on the day of Connor's wedding to Willa, which his father has decided to skip. Logan calls Roman from the airport with business, telling him to fire Gerri, the general counsel, and boards a plane to meet Lukas Matsson, the tech billionaire buying the company. Roman, Kendall and Shiv go to the wedding, on a boat in New York.

About a quarter of the way in, Roman gets a call from Tom, who is on the plane: Logan has collapsed. The rest of the sequence happens over phones. Tom holds a phone to Logan's ear so his children can speak to him, and they do, one after another, not knowing whether he can hear, or whether he's already gone. We never see Logan's body on the plane. We barely see the plane.

*Sound inside and outside the story* explains how offscreen sound works: a voice or event we hear but can't see makes us imagine it, and the imagined thing is often more powerful than any image. Here Logan, who dominated every room for three and a half seasons, has no voice at all; the father the children spent their lives trying to reach is unreachable even with a phone held to his ear. Their goodbyes are one-sided conversations with a silence, which is what their relationship with him always was.

### One long take

Mylod has explained in interviews that the sequence on the boat, from the first call through the siblings' reactions, was shot as a single continuous take of more than twenty-five minutes, with the crew reloading film cameras on the fly, and that much of what's in the episode comes from that take. The actors couldn't stop and reset; the camera, handheld, had to find each of them as the news spread.

*The camera's eye* teaches how camera movement and framing control the audience's experience. A long handheld take puts us in the room, a step behind the action, catching people mid-reaction. It also removes the editor's protection: no cutaway to give us a break, no reaction shot chosen for maximum effect. The result feels unrehearsed in the way that real bad news is unrehearsed. Sarah Snook arriving at the phone after her brothers is one of the most praised moments in the series, and Mylod has said he was moved to tears while shooting it.

### The wedding goes on

While the Roys fall apart on deck, Connor's wedding carries on: Connor and Willa marry after all, in front of hardly anyone. Then the siblings go to meet the plane, and Kendall stays to watch his father's body being taken away.

*Tragedy and comedy* notes how the two registers can coexist in one scene. "Connor's Wedding" is the series' saddest episode and still has jokes in it, many of them Connor's, because Connor, who has always been the least important child, has his own grievance: even his father's death has happened on his day. Armstrong never makes the wedding a gag. It's a second, smaller tragedy running alongside the big one, about a son who is upstaged for the last time.

### Killing the centre a third of the way in

Logan dies in the third of ten episodes. Armstrong had the idea of killing him early as far back as the planning of season 3. *Television structure* describes the season arc: a series of episodes building to a climax at the end. Season 4 puts its biggest event near the start and spends the rest asking what each child does with the vacuum. The children have been defined by one man for three seasons (see *The cast as a system*); with him gone, each of them has to show who they are.

The answers come in the next six episodes: an election night at the family's news channel (episode 8), where Roman pushes the network into calling the presidential race early for a far-right candidate and Kendall lets it happen; Logan's funeral (episode 9), where Roman breaks down and can't deliver his eulogy and Kendall gives one instead; and the board vote in the finale.

### The vote: whose side are we on?

In the finale, "With Open Eyes", the siblings spend a night at their mother's house in Barbados, where Roman and Shiv crown Kendall in a mock ritual, mixing a revolting drink as "a meal fit for a king". It's the warmest the three have ever been together. The next day, in the boardroom, Shiv refuses to vote for Kendall. The sale goes through, and Tom, the in-law, is chosen to run the company.

*Whose side are we on?* explains how allegiance can be set against alignment. For four seasons we've been aligned with the children, and the series has trained us to root for whichever one is losing. In the last episode, we finally get the scene we've wanted, the three of them on the same side, and then the show takes it away. Shiv's vote is a betrayal and also, arguably, the most clear-sighted thing she does: she's not sure Kendall should run anything. Whether we side with her depends on what we've been rooting for all along: the company, or the family.

## The shape

Season 4 is a ten-episode tragedy with its catastrophe moved forward. Freytag's pyramid (see *Gustav Freytag*) puts the reversal at the centre; here it comes about a third of the way in, and the rest is the long falling action.

| Stage | Episodes | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Exposition | 1–2 | Logan's birthday party; the siblings plan their own deal; the sale to Matsson's GoJo looms |
| The catastrophe | 3 ("Connor's Wedding") | Logan dies on the plane; the children hear it on the phone |
| Scramble | 4–7 | The wake; the deal; the children try to take Logan's place, each in their own way |
| Moral low | 8 ("America Decides") | Election night; the network calls the race |
| Grief | 9 ("Church and State") | The funeral, and the eulogies |
| Resolution | 10 ("With Open Eyes") | Barbados, the crowning, the vote, Tom as CEO |

## What people said

**Jesse Armstrong** has said in interviews around the final season that he had considered Logan's early death since planning season 3, and HBO's own behind-the-scenes material quotes him explaining that the episode was meant to be about the reactions, not the death. **Mark Mylod** described the long take in several interviews after the episode aired in April 2023, including how much of it made the final cut and the practical problems of keeping film cameras running.

**Critics** were nearly unanimous about "Connor's Wedding". Justin Sayles in *The Ringer* (2023) called it one of the best and most consequential episodes of the run. Philip Martin, in the *Arkansas Democrat-Gazette* (2023), called it one of the greatest hours of television he'd seen, while still questioning whether *Succession* was a great series or merely an entertaining one.

**The dissent** started early and never quite went away. On the show's debut in 2018, the *Washington Post*'s critic said they couldn't work out who the show was for. In 2023, a piece in *National Review* called the series overrated and argued that it flatters its audience's cynicism. Others, including a widely shared Yahoo Finance column, admitted getting bored midway through the second season.

## Watch for

- **The joke turns into an order** ("Hunting", near the end): the exact line where "boar on the floor" stops being a game.
- **Tom on the floor** ("Hunting"): watch the bully become the bullied, and Greg's face as he realises.
- **Logan's call to Roman** ("Connor's Wedding", opening minutes): the last time we hear him, talking business.
- **Tom's call** ("Connor's Wedding", about a quarter in): who answers, and who's told second and third.
- **The phone held to Logan's ear**: listen to what each child says, and what none of them can say.
- **Connor** (throughout "Connor's Wedding"): the son whose day it was meant to be.
- **The eulogies** ("Church and State"): who can speak, and who can't.
- **The boardroom glass** ("With Open Eyes", near the end): the siblings arguing behind glass while the board waits; what Shiv says, and when.

## Try it

Write a death that the characters hear but don't see. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Take two or three characters with an unresolved relationship to a fourth: a parent, a boss, a partner.
2. Put the fourth somewhere far away, and let the news come by phone, in pieces.
3. Write what each character says into the phone. Give each one a reason to say something other than what they need to say.
4. Don't write the death. Write who has to tell whom, and in what order.

- **Stage:** The phone call is ideal for theatre: one end visible, the other only heard. Stage the person on the other end in darkness, or not at all.
- **Screen:** Write it as one continuous scene, without cutting away. Mark where the camera should be when each character learns.
- **Journalism:** When reporting a death, find out how the family heard. The order in which people learned often tells you who mattered to whom, but get it right and get consent before you use it.

**Check:** Without seeing the death, does the reader know exactly what each character lost?
