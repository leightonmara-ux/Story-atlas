## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*Autumn Sonata* (*Höstsonaten*, 1978) was written and directed by Ingmar Bergman, filmed in the autumn of 1977 and photographed by Sven Nykvist. Eva (Liv Ullmann), a pastor's wife in a quiet rural parsonage, invites her mother Charlotte (Ingrid Bergman), a celebrated concert pianist she hasn't seen in seven years, to stay after Charlotte's longtime companion Leonardo dies. Charlotte arrives to find that Eva has also taken in her other daughter, Helena (Lena Nyman), who is severely disabled and whom Charlotte had left in an institution. Over one visit, and above all one night, mother and daughter say what they have never said: it was Ingrid Bergman's last film for the cinema, and her only one with the director who shared her surname.

## Why it works

### One piece of music, played twice

About a third of the way in, early in the visit, Charlotte asks Eva to play for her. Eva plays Chopin's Prelude in A minor (Op. 28, No. 2) carefully and a little stiffly, and the camera stays on Charlotte as she listens, in a long close-up. Her face goes through a whole private argument: polite attention, discomfort, something like pity, a decision about how much to say. When Eva asks what she thought, Charlotte is evasive at first, then explains how the piece should go. Chopin, she says in effect, is about feeling, not sentimentality: the prelude is pain, held in check and never allowed to wallow. Then she sits down and plays it herself, beautifully. (The piano on the soundtrack is Käbi Laretei, the director's former wife.)

This time the camera watches Eva, watching her mother. There are no raised voices, yet everything the night will make explicit is here: Charlotte's gift, her need to be best in the room, attention that turns into correction, and Eva's lifelong effort to be good enough. The same music, played twice, becomes the whole relationship.

This is the principle *The face and the cut* teaches: the meaning lives in the listener's face, and in what the cut puts next to it. Bergman simply holds the face far longer than most directors would dare.

### The night confrontation as the speech in a crisis

Later, Charlotte wakes from a nightmare and goes downstairs. Eva finds her, they talk, and the polite surface gives way. What follows is the long centre of the film: Eva lays out her childhood. Her mother was away on tour for long stretches; when she was home, her attention was overwhelming and controlling; Eva shaped herself to please her and still failed. She blames Charlotte for what happened to Helena too.

The dialogue works because, as *Dialogue reveals character* puts it, a speech in a crisis is an action, not a summary of feelings. Eva isn't describing her pain; she is presenting a bill that has been accumulating for decades, and every sentence is aimed. Charlotte's replies keep shifting tactic: denial, wounded pride, self-justification, and finally something closer to confession. Much of it plays as two faces in close-up, the background kept dim, so there's nowhere to look but at them (see *The camera's eye* on shot size as emotional distance).

### Every wound has a wound behind it

Eva's case against her mother is the classic structure from *Want, need and wound*: a past injury, a false belief built on it (that she can't be loved as she is), and an adult life shaped by that belief. The film gives her a second wound too. Her small son Erik drowned shortly before his fourth birthday, and she keeps his room as it was and speaks of still feeling him near.

The scene's turn is that Charlotte answers with a wound of her own. Cornered, she talks about her own childhood, which she remembers as one without warmth, where music was the only thing that let her feel anything. The film doesn't let this excuse her, and it doesn't let Eva's charges stand as the whole truth either. Damage is being handed down a line of mothers and daughters, and the film's question is whether anyone can stop it. Bergman avoids the over-tidy backstory the lesson warns about by making both wounds contested.

### A cast of four, each pulling on the others

The film has a tiny cast and essentially one house, which is why it's often called a chamber film (see *Ingmar Bergman* and *Stage forms*). *The cast as a system* explains why that works. Each character is a different answer to the same question, what do we owe the people we love?

Charlotte gives her life to her art and offers love as performance. Eva gives her life to others, including her sister, partly to prove she isn't her mother. Viktor, Eva's husband, loves Eva patiently without being able to reach her. And Helena, who can barely speak, is the film's conscience: the child Charlotte left behind, now cared for by the daughter Charlotte failed. Charlotte's first reaction on learning Helena is in the house is dismay; then she goes upstairs and performs affection, and the performance is part of the point. Helena's presence upstairs means the confrontation below is never just between two people.

### The narrator who can't get in

The film opens with Viktor speaking straight to the camera while Eva sits writing at a desk in the background. He tells us how they met and what she is like, and makes clear that he loves her and that she doesn't quite believe it. It's the theatre's direct address (see *Mood on stage*), and it sets the frame: we will watch this family from the edge of the room, as Viktor does.

It also does the work of *Introducing a character* twice over. Eva is introduced through her husband's loving, slightly helpless description, before she has done anything. Charlotte is introduced in her absence, through Eva's letter of invitation and the household's preparations, so when she arrives (talking at length about Leonardo's death) she has already been built up as an event. The film returns to him at the end.

### Keeping the scales moving

*Whose side are we on?* separates alignment (whom we follow and how much of their mind we get) from allegiance (whom we side with). *Autumn Sonata* keeps adjusting both dials. For the first half we're mostly aligned with Eva, and Charlotte's vanity and evasions are easy to judge. In the night scene, flashbacks show Eva's memories of childhood (young Eva is played by Liv Ullmann's daughter Linn), which pulls us deeper into her version. Then Charlotte's account of her own past tips the scale back, and Ingrid Bergman's performance, bewildered rather than villainous, makes her hard to condemn.

## The shape

The film follows the old five-part arc of *Gustav Freytag* closely, with one difference: its climax is late and is entirely talk. The title also hints at sonata form (a theme stated, developed and returned to), and the prelude, played twice, works like that in miniature.

| Stage | What happens | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| Exposition | Viktor's direct address; Eva's letter; Charlotte arrives, and learns Helena is in the house | Opening to about a fifth |
| Rising action | Dinner and the Chopin prelude, played by Eva and then by Charlotte | About a fifth to two-fifths |
| Climax | Charlotte's nightmare; the night-long confrontation, with flashbacks | About half to five-sixths |
| Falling action | Charlotte leaves early; Eva is left with what has been said | About the last sixth |
| Resolution | Eva writes to her mother; Viktor seals the letter | Final minutes |

What Freytag's model doesn't capture is that nothing outward changes. No one leaves home for good, no one dies. The reversal happens entirely in what has been said aloud, and the resolution is a letter whose effect we never see.

## What people said

**Ingmar Bergman** wrote about the film in *Images: My Life in Film* (published in Swedish in 1990). He described the shoot as draining, partly because he and Ingrid Bergman worked so differently: she arrived having rehearsed her part on her own, with fixed ideas about how to play it, while he wanted something rawer. He was also critical of the finished film. He recalled a French critic's remark that here Bergman was doing Bergman, and admitted that it hit home: the film relied on his own familiar habits.

**Ingrid Bergman** was shocked on first reading the screenplay, which asked her to play a self-absorbed mother who neglects her children, a character uncomfortably close to criticisms of her own life (she had left her daughter Pia to work in Italy decades earlier). By several accounts she questioned much of the script during the shoot, and the two clashed often. She was also already ill with the cancer that killed her in 1982. In her memoir *Ingrid Bergman: My Story* (1980), written with Alan Burgess, she said that seeing the documentary about the making of the film, shot on set by Arne Carlsson, showed her how difficult she had been, and that she wished she'd seen it before filming. The performance earned her an Academy Award nomination for Best Actress, her last. **Liv Ullmann** discussed working with both Bergmans in an interview recorded for the Criterion Collection's 2013 release.

**The critics** were divided. Roger Ebert named it among the ten best films of 1978. Dave Kehr, in the *Chicago Reader*, praised it as good chamber music, a crafted miniature, while noting that Bergman's usual bombast was, for once, built into the story's demands: a compliment with a sting. Others on release called it a minor work by a great director, too talky and too familiar. The film's reputation has grown since, largely on the strength of the two lead performances.

## Watch for

- **The opening address** (first few minutes): Viktor speaking to us, Eva writing behind him. Notice how far away she is in the frame.
- **Charlotte's arrival** (about a tenth in): how much she talks, and how little she asks.
- **Learning about Helena** (about a fifth in): Charlotte's face when she's told, and then how she behaves upstairs.
- **The prelude** (about a third in): the long hold on Charlotte listening to Eva, then the switch to Eva watching Charlotte play. Watch with the sound off once.
- **The nightmare** (about halfway): how the night scene begins with Charlotte, not Eva, at her most exposed.
- **The flashbacks** (during the night scene): young Eva's memories, played almost without dialogue, against the two adult faces.
- **Helena upstairs** (during the confrontation): how the film keeps reminding us she is in the house and can hear.
- **The letter** (final minutes): Eva's words in voice-over, and Viktor sealing the envelope. Ask whether this is hope or another performance.

## Try it

Write a scene in which two characters do the same thing, one after the other, and the second version is the argument. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Pick two people with an old grievance between them, and one task both can perform: a song, a recipe, a story told at the table, a way of folding laundry.
2. Write the first performance, then the second, with as little dialogue about the grievance as possible. Let the second performer's "help" or "correction" carry the accusation.
3. Give the first performer no reply after the second version. Write only what their face, hands or body does.

- **Stage:** Stage both performances in the same spot, and put the watching character downstage, where the audience can see their face while the other performs.
- **Screen:** Script each performance as a held shot on the person *listening*, not playing. Note how long you hold before the cut.
- **Journalism:** In a profile, find two people who describe or do the same thing differently (a parent and child recounting the same family story). Place their versions side by side without commentary, and let the difference carry the meaning.

**Check:** If you cut every line about the grievance, would a reader still know what the two people are fighting about?
