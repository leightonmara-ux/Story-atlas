## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*La Notte* (*The Night*, 1961) was directed by Michelangelo Antonioni and photographed in black and white by Gianni Di Venanzo; it won the Golden Bear at the Berlin Film Festival that year. Giovanni Pontano (Marcello Mastroianni), a successful Milanese novelist, and his wife Lidia (Jeanne Moreau) spend one day and one night together: a visit to a dying friend in hospital, the launch of Giovanni's new book, a long walk Lidia takes alone through the city, and an all-night party at the villa of a rich industrialist, where Giovanni pursues the host's daughter, Valentina (Monica Vitti). Little happens in the usual sense; by dawn the marriage has been looked at squarely and found empty. It is the middle film of Antonioni's loose trilogy, between *L'Avventura* (1960) and *L'Eclisse* (1962).

## Why it works

### Buildings that are bigger than feelings

The film opens before it has any characters. Under the credits, the camera moves along the glass skin of Milan's new Pirelli Tower, then barely finished, while the street and the city slide across its surface as reflections.

That sets the visual rule for everything after. Antonioni and Di Venanzo keep framing people small against big, flat surfaces: blank walls, office facades, long pavements, a horizon. A face often takes one corner of the frame; the rest is architecture or sky. This is **negative space** used not for suspense but for weight: the emptiness around Giovanni and Lidia is the subject. *The camera's eye* explains that the wider the shot, the more a person belongs to their surroundings, and that a figure pushed to the edge of an empty frame feels isolated. *Light, colour and setting* would call it setting as character: the new Milan of glass and concrete isn't a backdrop to the marriage but a picture of it, prosperous and cold.

### Glass, reflections and the scene you can't hear

The film keeps putting something transparent between people. The opening is all glass. And in the rain sequence, about two-thirds through, Lidia leaves the party with Roberto, a guest who has been courting her, and drives off with him in his car. Antonioni keeps the camera outside the car. We watch the two of them through the rain-streaked windows, talking and laughing, their faces blurred and broken up by the water, and we hear none of what they say. Lidia seems, for a few minutes, to be enjoying herself; when they stop, she turns away from his kiss.

The wet glass says what the cast never does: these people can see one another but can't reach one another. And by withholding the dialogue, the film makes us read Lidia from the outside, as Giovanni can't. *Sound inside and outside the story* treats sound as something that places us; here the scene's sound places us firmly outside the car, outside the conversation, outside Lidia.

### Lidia's walk: a story made of looking

About a fifth of the way in, Lidia slips away from Giovanni's book launch and walks. The sequence runs for a long time with almost no dialogue. She drifts through central Milan, a small figure against walls and buildings, and ends up in the outlying neighbourhood where she and Giovanni lived when they were first married. There she comes on a fight between young men on a patch of waste ground and tries to stop it, and watches boys setting off rockets in an open field. Then she telephones him to come and fetch her. When he arrives, he barely registers where he is.

In conventional terms the walk is a pause; here it's the centre. It gives Lidia an inner life we're shown, never told, and makes the city a mirror for her: modern towers first, then the older outskirts and waste ground where her past is. *Light, colour and setting* would call this geography doing the work of argument, and it is close to the **long take** logic in *The camera's eye*: continuity of time and space is itself the meaning. We live her afternoon at walking pace, and the pace is the feeling.

### The party as a system of mirrors

The second half is one long night at the villa of Gherardini, an industrialist who offers Giovanni a well-paid job writing for his company: a writer being invited to sell out, and Giovanni doesn't say no. It's here that *The cast as a system* comes in. The three leads are variations on one problem, the inability to feel or commit.

Giovanni is the empty centre: charming, passive, drifting towards whatever is nearest. Lidia sees clearly but has stopped acting on what she sees. Valentina, whom Giovanni first finds alone reading Hermann Broch's novel *The Sleepwalkers*, is younger, cleverer and more honest than either; she plays a game sliding her compact across a chequered marble floor, and when Giovanni pursues her she is drawn in and then holds back. Late in the night, alone with him, she plays him a tape recording of herself reading something she has written; when he praises it, she erases it. Her refusal is what stops this becoming an affair story. The dying friend, Tommaso, is the fourth corner: the one man who loved Lidia without needing anything, and the measure of what Giovanni has lost. During the party, Lidia telephones the hospital and learns he has died.

### Jazz at the party, quiet everywhere else

Antonioni had the jazz musician Giorgio Gaslini write the score, and Gaslini's band appears on screen, playing at the party. So most of the film's music is inside the story: the guests dance to it, talk over it, ignore it. *Music and silence* would call it **anempathetic**: it carries on in its own cool, easy mood while the marriage at the centre quietly ends. Nobody, including the music, is paying attention to these two people.

Elsewhere the film is sparing with sound. The walk plays mostly to street noise and footsteps, so the boys' rockets land with real force. The rain sequence swaps conversation for the sound of water. Valentina's recorded voice, played and wiped, is the film's sharpest sound image: a person literally erasing what she said.

### An ending that refuses the climax

At dawn, Giovanni and Lidia walk out across Gherardini's private golf course. She tells him Tommaso has died, and then that she no longer loves him, and believes he no longer loves her. She takes out a letter and reads it aloud: a tender, articulate love letter, describing waking beside a sleeping woman and loving her. Giovanni asks who wrote it. She tells him he did, years ago. He didn't recognise his own words.

He responds by pulling her down into a sand bunker on the golf course and kissing her, almost desperately, while she says no and then stops resisting. The camera moves away from them across the empty landscape, the music comes in, and the film ends. Nothing is resolved; the image does the ending: two figures small against an empty morning.

This is what the counterpoint lessons describe. In *Robert McKee*'s terms, it's a **miniplot**: a passive protagonist, inner conflict, an open ending. In *Ursula K. Le Guin*'s, it's a carrier bag, not a spear: its meaning comes from how a hospital, a city, a party and a letter sit together. And the letter works like the turn in *Kishōtenketsu*: not a fight but a sudden shift in understanding. The past arrives in the present in Giovanni's own voice, and he can't hear it.

## The shape

*La Notte* has no inciting incident in the usual sense, no midpoint and no climactic act. Its shape is a day and a night, and it fits McKee's miniplot or a loose four-part *Kishōtenketsu* better than any three-act scheme.

| Part | What happens | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| Ki (setting) | The Pirelli Tower credits; the hospital visit to Tommaso; the book launch | Opening to about a fifth |
| Shō (development) | Lidia's walk through Milan to the old neighbourhood; evening at home and out | About a fifth to two-fifths |
| Ten (turn) | The villa party: Valentina, the news of Tommaso's death, the job offer, the rain, the car | About two-fifths to nine-tenths |
| Ketsu (gathering) | Dawn on the golf course: the letter, the embrace, the camera's withdrawal | Final ten minutes or so |

What drives it instead of plot is the clock, morning to night to dawn, and accumulating evidence: each episode adds one more proof about the marriage, until the letter makes the case undeniable.

## What people said

**Michelangelo Antonioni** spoke about the trilogy's theme most directly in a statement he made at Cannes in 1960, when *L'Avventura* was booed: in paraphrase, that modern people had acquired new scientific knowledge while still living by old, worn-out moral and emotional habits, and that love was the place where this showed most painfully. *La Notte* moves that idea from a remote island to Italy's most modern city.

**Ingmar Bergman**, who disliked most of Antonioni's work and in 2002 dismissed him as tedious, made a striking exception: he singled out *Blow-Up* and *La Notte* as masterpieces, and suggested that the second owed a great deal to the young Jeanne Moreau. The remark is widely reported; the precise wording varies between sources.

**Pauline Kael** gave the most famous dissent. In "The Come-Dressed-as-the-Sick-Soul-of-Europe Parties", an essay from the early 1960s collected in *I Lost It at the Movies* (1965), she grouped *La Notte* with *La Dolce Vita* and *Last Year at Marienbad* as fashionable portraits of empty lives. Her complaint was that the film shows people for whom life has lost meaning without giving any insight into why, and that calling Giovanni a writer lends him an importance his character never earns. **Jonathan Rosenbaum**, in his essay "A Cinema of Uncertainty", answered that Kael was so struck by the idle-rich settings these films shared that she ignored how differently Fellini, Resnais and Antonioni actually made them.

## Watch for

- **The opening credits** (first few minutes): the Pirelli Tower's glass, and the city reflected in it. Notice there are no people.
- **Lidia's walk** (about a fifth to a third in): how small she is in most shots, and how long the film lets her walk without explaining.
- **The rockets** (during the walk): the sudden noise and violence in an empty field, and Lidia's face watching.
- **Valentina and the compact** (about halfway): the game on the chequered floor, and who is watching whom.
- **The rain** (about two-thirds in): the guests in the pool, then Lidia and Roberto through the wet car window, with no dialogue on the soundtrack.
- **The tape recorder** (late in the night): Valentina's own voice, played back and erased.
- **Dawn on the golf course** (final ten minutes): the letter, Giovanni's question, and the camera moving away from the couple across the empty landscape.

## Try it

Write a scene where two people's relationship is shown by where they are placed, not by what they say. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Pick two characters whose relationship is ending, or has quietly ended, without either of them admitting it.
2. Put them in one large, specific place: an office lobby, a car park, a beach at closing time. Describe the space first, before either person appears.
3. Write the scene with no more than five lines of dialogue. Let distance, barriers (a window, a counter, a car windscreen) and the size of the space carry the feeling.

- **Stage:** Use the whole stage. Put the two characters as far apart as the space allows, and let one cross towards the other only once.
- **Screen:** Write action lines that imply wide shots. Keep the faces small in the frame until the one moment that matters, then cut close.
- **Journalism:** In a profile or scene, describe the setting your subject moves through as precisely as the subject. Let one physical detail (a closed door, a glass wall, an empty room) stand in for a relationship you can't state outright.

**Check:** If someone read only your stage directions or scene description, with every line of dialogue cut, would they know how these two people feel about each other?
