## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*Yi Yi* (*A One and a Two*, 2000) was written and directed by Edward Yang, and won him the Best Director prize at Cannes. It follows one middle-class Taipei family, the Jians, over a few months that begin with a wedding and end with a funeral. The father, NJ (played by the writer-director Wu Nien-jen), meets his first love again and wonders what his life might have been; his teenage daughter Ting-Ting falls into her first romance; his wife Min-Min falls apart; and his eight-year-old son Yang-Yang wanders through it all with a camera. The grandmother, who has had a stroke, lies in a coma at the centre of the flat, and the doctor tells the family to talk to her. Nearly three hours long, it was Yang's last film; he died in 2007.

## Why it works

### Bookends: a wedding, a funeral, and a birth between

The film opens at the wedding of Min-Min's younger brother, A-Di, whose bride is visibly pregnant, and which his ex-girlfriend crashes in a scene of loud public grief. It ends at the grandmother's funeral. Somewhere between, A-Di's baby is born. Yang said in a 2000 interview that in Chinese culture a wedding always stands for luck, something good, and that the film ends with its opposite.

This is a frame, not a plot. Nothing at the wedding causes the funeral. What the bookends give is *Frank Kermode & Peter Brooks*' "tick-tock": a beginning and an end that turn the months between into organized time. Because the frame is the life cycle itself (marriage, birth, death), every smaller story inside it reads as one more instance of the same cycle. The pregnant bride and the dying grandmother are in the same family photograph. The lesson's point about *concord* is that the end should confirm what the beginning set going; here the beginning sets going nothing but time.

### Three generations, one question

*The cast as a system* says each member of an ensemble should be a different answer to the same question. In *Yi Yi* the question is roughly: is it too late to live differently?

NJ, in his forties, gets a literal second chance when his old girlfriend Sherry reappears, first by a hotel lift at the wedding and later in Tokyo. Min-Min decides her daily life is so repetitive that she has nothing left to say, and leaves for a Buddhist retreat in the mountains. Ting-Ting, about sixteen, is living her first romance, with Fatty, the ex-boyfriend of the girl next door. A-Di keeps making bad business bets. Yang-Yang is just starting to notice that adults don't tell the truth. And the grandmother, silent in her bed, is the end every one of them is heading toward.

No one is the protagonist. As *The cast as a system* puts it of the ensemble, the meaning lies in the pattern: put a father's middle-aged regret beside a daughter's first date, and each becomes a comment on the other.

### Cross-cutting two first loves

The film's most famous sequence, about two-thirds of the way through, cuts between NJ walking with Sherry in Japan, remembering their first romance decades earlier, and Ting-Ting on an awkward first date with Fatty in Taipei. The father's memory and the daughter's present run in parallel, so that we watch NJ's youth being lived by someone else.

*Editing* calls this cross-cutting, and lists its classic uses as suspense and irony. Yang uses it for something rarer: rhyme. Neither strand needs the other to make sense, and neither affects the other's outcome. The cut just puts them side by side, and the comparison tells us what NJ can't say: that what he's trying to recover belongs to another stage of life, and is already being lived by his daughter. It's *The braided narrative*'s "contrast" mode in miniature: the strands never meet, and the meaning comes from holding them together.

### Showing people the backs of their heads

Yang-Yang asks his father a child's philosopher's question: if he can only see what's in front of him, how can he know what's behind him? Can we only know half of the truth? NJ answers, more or less, that this is why we need a camera. So Yang-Yang starts taking pictures. He photographs mosquitoes (the prints come out looking empty, and a teacher mocks him for them). Then he photographs the backs of people's heads, and hands his uncle a stack of prints of the back of his uncle's head, explaining that he can't see it himself, so Yang-Yang is helping.

This is the film's theory of itself. Much of *Yi Yi* is shot at a distance, in long takes, often from behind or from across a room, so that we see people as they can't see themselves (*The camera's eye*, on framing and distance). The boy's photography is also a small *künstlerroman* thread, the artist's formation from *The bildungsroman*: the youngest character is the one discovering a vocation, and it is to show people what they miss.

### Reflections in glass

Yang repeatedly films characters through windows at night, so that the city's lights are laid over their faces. In one shot Min-Min stands at her office window, the night city outside and the near-empty office reflected in the glass; in another, after her breakdown, the bedroom window turns into a dark mirror full of the traffic below. The effect is double exposure without a trick: the private crisis and the indifferent city occupy the same frame, and a single woman's misery becomes one light among thousands.

*Light, colour and setting* teaches that setting can be a character. Yang goes further: the glass makes setting and character literally the same image. Watch how rarely anyone in the film is filmed in plain, unobstructed close-up.

### Talking to someone who can't answer

The doctor's instruction, to talk to the grandmother every day, is a perfect dramatic device: it forces each character to deliver a monologue, and each monologue shows who they are (see *Dialogue reveals character*). Min-Min's breaks her. She tells NJ, in tears, that she has nothing to say because she says the same things every day, and that her life adds up to so little. NJ talks about work. Ting-Ting, who believes her grandmother's stroke happened because she forgot to take out the rubbish, comes to ask forgiveness. Yang-Yang refuses to talk to her at all.

The payoff comes at the funeral, when Yang-Yang finally speaks to her. He explains why he didn't talk before: she couldn't see what he saw, and he wants to show people what they don't know. He ends by saying that he feels old too. It's the film's last line, and it gives the youngest character the oldest thought. The patient who never answers has made everyone else speak.

## The shape

No arc model fits *Yi Yi* well, and that's instructive. There's no protagonist with a goal, and the climaxes in the separate strands (a murder in Ting-Ting's strand, NJ's choice in Tokyo) don't line up. *Jane Alison*'s **wavelets** describe it better: many small rises and falls, held inside a frame. Ursula Le Guin's carrier bag (*Ursula K. Le Guin*) fits too: a container holding many lives in relation.

| Part | What happens | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| Frame opens | A-Di's wedding; NJ meets Sherry; the grandmother's stroke that night | Opening to about a sixth |
| Strands set going | The family takes turns talking to her; Ting-Ting meets Fatty; NJ's business deal with a Japanese game designer, Ota | About a sixth to a third |
| Strands widen | Min-Min leaves for the retreat; NJ goes to Tokyo | About a third to a half |
| Parallel peak | NJ and Sherry in Japan, cut against Ting-Ting and Fatty in Taipei | About half to two-thirds |
| Strands break | Sherry and NJ part; Fatty commits a violent crime; A-Di's crises | About two-thirds to five-sixths |
| Frame closes | Min-Min and NJ come home; the grandmother dies; the funeral and Yang-Yang's speech | Last sixth |

Notice the movement at the end: nearly everyone who went looking for a different life comes home and reports that it wasn't so different after all.

## What people said

**Edward Yang** talked about the film's frame in interviews on its release, explaining the wedding as the conventional sign of luck and setting it against the closing funeral. He made the film after a long break, and it was his first to get regular American distribution, through a small company, despite six earlier features. Yang trained as an engineer and worked in computing in the United States before turning to film, and NJ's world of offices, deals and software has that insider's precision.

**The critics** were close to unanimous. A. O. Scott, in the *New York Times* (2000), began his review by describing watching the end credits through tears and trying to name what he felt. The National Society of Film Critics named it the best film of 2000, and it placed high in later polls of the decade's best films. Roger Ebert, reviewing it in 2001, built his review around Yang-Yang and the idea that none of these people can see more than part of the truth. Kent Jones's essay for the Criterion Collection release looks at how the film's scenes carry their resonance over into one another, and at how Yang's Taipei can seem impersonal while remaining, for each character, a matter of choice.

**The dissent** was mostly about the praise rather than the film. A reviewer for Detroit's *Metro Times* noticed how many critics insisted the three hours flew by, and suspected some of that was film-buff machismo; the film's length and slowness remain the usual objection from viewers who don't take to it. The film had little exposure in Taiwan itself on first release, an irony often noted, since it is one of the best portraits of Taipei on film.

## Watch for

- **The wedding** (opening minutes): the ex-girlfriend's scene, and how the film introduces almost the whole ensemble at one event before we know who matters.
- **NJ and Sherry by the lift** (about a tenth in): a whole failed past in a few lines between two people who haven't seen each other in decades.
- **The rubbish** (about a sixth in): the small, forgettable chore that Ting-Ting will blame herself for.
- **Min-Min's breakdown** (about a third in): her account of having nothing to say, and the window-as-mirror shot that follows.
- **Yang-Yang's photographs** (scattered, especially the middle): the mosquitoes, the teacher's mockery, the prints of his uncle's head.
- **Ota** (around the middle): the Japanese game designer, who becomes the film's most honest adult, and what NJ's partners do to him.
- **The cross-cut first dates** (about two-thirds in): father in Japan, daughter in Taipei. Note how the editing makes them rhyme.
- **The funeral** (final minutes): Yang-Yang's speech, the last words of the film.

## Try it

Write two short scenes, one from each end of a family, that rhyme without touching. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Pick a family and an experience that comes round twice in a lifetime: a first date, a first job, a first funeral, leaving home.
2. Write it for a young member happening now, and for an older one remembering it. Keep both short.
3. Interleave them in four or more pieces each. Don't let either character mention the other. End on the younger one.

- **Stage:** Play both scenes on the same stage at once, in two pools of light, with lines from one landing in the silence of the other.
- **Screen:** Write it as an INTERCUT, ending each piece on an image or line that the next piece seems to answer.
- **Journalism:** In a family profile, find two generations who did the same thing (emigrated, joined the same factory, married young). Alternate their accounts paragraph by paragraph, without commentary, and let the reader make the comparison.

**Check:** Could the older character's strand be cut without losing the meaning of the younger one's? If yes, the two aren't yet rhyming.
