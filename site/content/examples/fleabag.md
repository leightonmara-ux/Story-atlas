## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole series, including the ending.*

*Fleabag* is a comedy-drama written by and starring Phoebe Waller-Bridge, adapted from her own one-woman stage show and made by BBC Three with Amazon in two series of six half-hour episodes (2016 and 2019), directed by Harry Bradbeer. Its heroine, never named, runs a failing guinea-pig café in London, sleeps around, steals, and tells us all about it in asides to the camera, while avoiding the grief she can't face: the death of her best friend Boo, and her own part in it. Series 1 is the story of that avoidance; series 2 is a love story with a Catholic priest who notices her asides. The course's lesson *Phoebe Waller-Bridge* covers the big devices (the audience as confidant, the withheld wound, the Priest who sees the glance); this case study goes scene by scene.

## Why it works

### The cold open that promises a genre

Series 2 begins with Fleabag in a restaurant bathroom, wiping blood off her face. A woman asks if she's all right. Fleabag turns to the camera and tells us that this is a love story. Then the episode goes back to the start of the evening and shows how she got here: a family dinner to celebrate her father's engagement to her Godmother, with her sister Claire, Claire's husband Martin, and a priest who will marry the couple.

Two lessons meet here. *Story vs. telling* calls this kind of start *in medias res*: show a striking moment from later, then fill in how we got there. The bloody nose hangs over the whole dinner, so every polite exchange is charged; we know it ends in violence, and we watch for the fuse. And *Genre as a promise* explains the line to camera. It tells us what kind of story we're in, and it's a joke, because nothing at this table looks like romance. Except the Priest, who swears, drinks and laughs at the wrong moments. By the end of the episode we know whom the promise is about.

### A single dinner as an ensemble machine

That opening episode is almost entirely one table in one restaurant. It works because each person at it wants something the others threaten. The father wants a peaceful announcement; the Godmother wants to be gracious and to win; Claire wants to keep control, and, we learn, is miscarrying in the toilets; Martin wants to needle Fleabag; Fleabag wants to get through it without being the problem. When Claire's miscarriage comes out, Fleabag claims it as her own so that her sister can hide it: a lie that is also the kindest thing anyone does all night.

*The cast as a system* argues that characters are defined against each other, and this dinner is a model of it. Waller-Bridge doesn't need a plot outside the restaurant, because the cast itself generates conflict. Notice that almost no one has a name. "Godmother", "Priest", "Dad", "Fleabag": the roles are the characters, and every role is a pressure on someone else.

### Side characters who come in, make a speech, and change her

Two of the most quoted scenes in the series belong to people who barely appear. In series 1, the bank manager (Hugh Dennis) turns down Fleabag's loan in the first episode, after she takes off her jumper in his office and he takes offence. He returns in the last episode, and in a quiet conversation at the café she finally admits to someone how much she dislikes herself. His answer is plain and decent, and it's the first kindness she can't turn into a joke.

In series 2, episode 3, Belinda (Kristin Scott Thomas), an older businesswoman receiving an award, sits at a hotel bar with Fleabag and gives a speech about women, pain and the menopause: women carry pain inside them, she argues, and the menopause is the moment of release. It's funny, sad and slightly drunk.

*Memorable side characters* describes "the side character who changes the hero": someone who arrives, has one vivid trait and one strong view, and leaves the protagonist different. Neither of these people is part of the family plot. Each is placed at a point where Fleabag is about to repeat her pattern, and each offers a way of looking at herself that the family never can.

### The confession as dialogue under pressure

In series 2, episode 4, Fleabag goes to the Priest's church to make a confession, half as a joke. He tells her to kneel. What follows is the longest speech she gives in the series, and it's not a list of sins. She admits she wants someone to tell her what to do: what to wear, what to eat, what to believe, how to live. The comedy drains out of her voice. The speech ends where both of them have been heading for four episodes.

*Dialogue reveals character* says a speech in a crisis is an action, not a summary of feelings. Fleabag's confession is a request, made to the one man who has said he can't grant it. Every sentence moves her closer to him and further from her armour. Watch, too, what the scene does with her asides: for once she isn't talking to us. The person she's confessing to has taken the audience's place.

### The romance with a built-in barrier

*Genre as a promise* explains the romance convention of courtship and its barrier: two people drawn together, and something that keeps them apart. Series 2 picks the strongest barrier available, a vow of celibacy that the man actually believes in, and gives it six episodes. The Priest is no hypocrite; his faith matters to him, and the barrier is never mocked. That's why the ending can't be a wedding.

It ends at a bus stop, after he has married her father and the Godmother. She tells him she loves him. His answer is that it'll pass. He walks away; she stays. A fox, which has been a running joke all series (he is afraid of foxes and believes they follow him), trots after him. Then Fleabag gets up, walks off, and when the camera starts to follow, shakes her head at it. As *Frank Kermode & Peter Brooks* would put it, the ending reaches the concord the beginning promised by an unexpected road: it was a love story, but the relationship that ends happily is the one with us, because she no longer needs it.

### Short seasons, and stopping

Each series is six half-hours, with an arc that finishes. *Television structure* describes the engine problem: what keeps a show generating stories? *Fleabag*'s engine was always one woman's avoidance, and avoidance can't be renewed forever. Waller-Bridge ended after series 2, and the final shot dramatises the decision. It's a lesson for anyone writing in short runs: build an arc that can close, and close it.

## The shape

Series 2 can be laid out as a six-episode romance, with the confession as its midpoint and the bus stop as its ending. (Series 1 is shaped differently, around the withheld story of Boo, as the *Phoebe Waller-Bridge* lesson explains.)

| Romance beat | Episode | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| The promise | 1 (cold open) | Bloody nose, "This is a love story", then the dinner |
| The meeting | 1 | The Priest at the family table |
| Growing closer | 2–3 | Drinks and talk with the Priest; he notices her glance to camera (episode 3) |
| The barrier tested | 4 | The confession; the vow gives way |
| Pulling apart | 5 | Both retreat; the family plot (Claire, Martin, the wedding) presses in |
| Separation | 6 (last minutes) | The wedding, the bus stop, "It'll pass", the fox, the walk away from the camera |

What the table can't show is the second strand underneath: the sisters. Claire's story (her marriage to Martin, and a Finnish colleague named Klare she likes) resolves at the same wedding, when Fleabag tells her to go after him. The series pays off a love story for Claire too.

## What people said

**Phoebe Waller-Bridge** published both series' scripts with her commentary as *Fleabag: The Scriptures* (2019). In interviews around series 2, including with Terry Gross on NPR's *Fresh Air* in May 2019, she described the relationship between Fleabag and the camera as the show's core, and the asides as Fleabag's way of controlling how her life is told. She has said in several interviews that she ended the show after two series because the story felt complete.

**Critics** were close to unanimous about series 2. John Powers, reviewing it for NPR in 2019, argued that it made an already successful show much better, partly by exposing the first series' limits. Metacritic's tally of critics' reviews of series 2 was entirely positive.

The main dissent was about class rather than craft. In *The Guardian* (2019), Ellen E. Jones called the show a work of genius made for and about posh women, and argued that its near-universal praise drowned out viewers who didn't see themselves in it. Rhiannon Lucy Cosslett, in the *New Statesman*, objected to every new show by a woman being compared to *Fleabag*. Waller-Bridge, asked about the "posh" charge in 2019, rejected the idea that it undermined the story, while acknowledging that the show sees the world through a middle-class family.

## Watch for

- **The bank, series 1, episode 1** (early in the episode): the jumper, the offence, and how Fleabag narrates her own humiliation to us.
- **The bank manager, series 1, episode 6** (late in the episode): the only conversation in the series where she says what she thinks of herself without a joke.
- **The bathroom, series 2, episode 1** (first minute): blood, the line to camera, then the jump back in time.
- **The dinner, series 2, episode 1**: count how many people are hiding something, and when each secret comes out.
- **The noticed aside, series 2, episode 3**: the Priest asks where she just went. Watch Waller-Bridge's face.
- **Belinda's speech, series 2, episode 3**: a stranger's monologue that answers a question Fleabag hasn't asked.
- **The confession, series 2, episode 4** (end of the episode): listen to when the jokes stop.
- **The bus stop, series 2, episode 6** (last minutes): "It'll pass", the fox, and the shake of the head at the camera.

## Try it

Write a confession scene. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Pick a character who usually deflects with jokes. Put them somewhere they're required to tell the truth: a confession booth, a police interview, a hospital bed, a job interview gone wrong.
2. Let them start with jokes. Then write the moment the listener refuses the joke, with one short instruction or question.
3. Write the speech that follows as a *request*, not a description of feelings: what does the character want from this listener, right now?

- **Stage:** Place the listener where the audience can't see their face, so the speaker carries everything. Decide whether the speaker has been talking to the house before this scene, and stop it here.
- **Screen:** Shoot it as two singles. Mark the line where you cut to the listener for the first time; make it the line that costs the speaker most.
- **Journalism:** In an interview profile, find the moment your subject stopped performing. Build the piece so the reader reaches it in the same order you did, and keep their words exact.

**Check:** If you took out the jokes at the start, would the confession land as hard? If yes, the jokes aren't doing their job.
