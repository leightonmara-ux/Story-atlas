## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*The Virgin Suicides* (1999) was Sofia Coppola's first feature, which she wrote and directed from Jeffrey Eugenides's 1993 debut novel. It premiered at Cannes in the Directors' Fortnight in 1999 and opened in the United States in 2000. In a leafy Michigan suburb in the 1970s, the five Lisbon sisters, aged thirteen to seventeen, live under the strict rule of their religious mother (Kathleen Turner) and mild maths-teacher father (James Woods). Over one year, all five take their own lives, and the story is told, decades later, by the neighbourhood boys who were obsessed with them and still can't explain what happened.

## Why it works

### The narrator is "we"

The film is narrated by an adult voice (Giovanni Ribisi) speaking for a group: the boys, now grown men, who watched the Lisbon house from across the street. They speak as "we". None of them is singled out as the teller, and the film rarely bothers to tell them apart.

*Point of view and voice* asks who is speaking and how far we should trust them. A collective narrator is a special case. It sounds authoritative, like a chorus or a community's memory, but it is also a group of teenage boys whose knowledge of the girls was almost entirely from outside: glimpses through windows, a diary they got hold of, rumours, an evening of supervised visiting. The film never pretends otherwise. The narrator keeps admitting what they don't know, and the girls' inner lives remain closed. The unreliability isn't lying; it's the limit of looking.

This is also the subject of *Who gets to be the subject*. Mulvey's point about the gaze, who looks and who is looked at, is built into the film's structure. The Lisbon sisters are seen almost entirely through boys' longing, and the film makes that longing visible as a problem: the boys' devotion doesn't help them understand the girls at all.

### The ending told at the start

Within the first minutes, the narrator tells us that the suicides began with Cecilia, the youngest, and we see her in a bath after cutting her wrists. The title has already told us the rest. In the hospital, a doctor tells her she's too young to know how bad life gets, and she answers that he has obviously never been a thirteen-year-old girl. It's the best-known line in the film, and it's a challenge to every adult and every boy who will try to explain her.

*Story vs. telling* calls this a prolepsis, a flash-forward: the telling announces the outcome before the events. It kills one kind of interest, will they survive?, and creates another. Every innocent scene that follows, the girls in their bedrooms, the dance, a lawn in summer, is watched through the knowledge of how it ends. Suspense becomes elegy.

### Memory turns into mythmaking

*Hayden White & Joan Didion* quotes Didion on how we look for the sermon in the suicide, the lesson in the murder: we impose a story on scattered images in order to bear them. *The Virgin Suicides* is about people doing exactly that and failing.

The boys collect evidence like investigators. They read Cecilia's diary. The film intercuts interviews with adults years later, speaking almost to camera, most memorably Trip Fontaine, the school heartthrob who took Lux (Kirsten Dunst) to the homecoming dance, now a burned-out grown man (Michael Paré) recalling her as if she were a legend. The neighbours and the news offer explanations: the strict mother, the trees, adolescence, the times. The narrator offers his own. None fits. The more the boys gather, the more the girls become a myth and the less they remain people.

For a journalist, the film is an anatomy of the profile that can't get access: lots of testimony from the edges, nothing from the centre, and a writer whose own feelings fill the gap.

### The Air score and the needle drops

The French duo Air wrote the score, their first for a film: soft analogue synthesizers, slow tempos, a dreamy, slightly melancholy haze. One track written for the film, "Playground Love", has a vocal credited to Gordon Tracks, the alias of Thomas Mars of the band Phoenix. The score acts on the film as *Music and silence* describes a leitmotif: its returning textures become the sound of the girls as remembered, a feeling attached to absence.

The period songs do a different job. After the girls are taken out of school and confined to the house, the boys phone them, and instead of talking the two sides play records down the line to each other: soft-rock songs of the early 1970s, such as Todd Rundgren's "Hello It's Me" and Carole King's "So Far Away". Feeling that can't be spoken passes through other people's songs. Earlier, Trip's first appearance, walking down a school corridor in slow motion as girls turn to look, is scored to Heart's "Magic Man", a needle drop that tells us exactly how the school sees him, and gently mocks it.

### Mood over plot

Very little happens in the sense of action and consequence. There is a party in the Lisbon basement, held on a doctor's advice to cheer Cecilia up, from which she excuses herself and jumps from an upstairs window onto the iron fence below. There is the homecoming dance. Lux stays out with Trip and wakes alone on the football field at dawn, and their mother punishes all four girls by withdrawing them from the world, and makes Lux destroy her rock records. Workers come to cut down the neighbourhood's diseased elm trees. Then the end.

What holds it together is mood, in the sense *Edgar Allan Poe* meant by a single effect: every element is chosen to produce one impression, a hazy, sunlit, nostalgic sadness. Ed Lachman's photography is soft and often golden, with dreamy inserts (Lux's face floating in the sky), and *Light, colour and setting* would point to the suburb itself, tidy lawns and dying trees, as the film's argument about a place where nothing is allowed to be wrong. Coppola commits so fully to the mood that the absence of explanation feels like the point rather than a failure.

## The shape

The film doesn't follow a rising-and-falling arc so much as a telling built around a known outcome. *Story vs. telling* maps it best: the fabula is a single year in chronological order; the telling wraps it in a retrospective frame and pre-empts it with a prolepsis.

| Element | What happens | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| Frame and prolepsis | The adult narrator; Cecilia's first attempt; the doctor | Opening minutes |
| First death | The basement party; Cecilia's fall | About a fifth in |
| Interlude of hope | Trip pursues Lux; the date at the Lisbon house; homecoming | About a third to just past half |
| Confinement | Lux returns at dawn; the girls withdrawn from school; the records; the phone calls | About half to four-fifths |
| The last night | The girls signal the boys; the boys come, expecting escape, and find the deaths | About the last sixth |
| Coda | A neighbourhood party themed around asphyxiation; the narrator's admission that they will never understand | Final minutes |

Woven through, the adult interviews look back from the future, so the telling keeps reminding us that this is memory, not event.

## What people said

**Sofia Coppola** has said she was given the novel by a friend (the musician Thurston Moore), loved it for how accurately it described being a teenager, and began adapting a chapter almost as an exercise, without much expectation that she'd be allowed to make it, since another adaptation was already in development. Her script eventually won out. **Jeffrey Eugenides** visited the set and discussed the adaptation in a 2018 interview for the Criterion Collection's release.

**The critics** mostly admired it. **Emanuel Levy**, reviewing from Cannes for *Variety*, praised its assured handling of teenage suicide and its seriocomic tone. **Roger Ebert** (*Chicago Sun-Times*, 2000) admired Coppola's restraint, her refusal to hammer home interpretations, but noted that the reasons offered for the sisters' unhappiness are perfunctory. That reservation became the main line of dissent: some reviewers found the film beautiful but evasive, a mood piece that never decides what it is saying about the girls, and its reputation as a dreamy, drifting film is still divided between those who find that haunting and those who find it tedious. Its standing has risen considerably since, and it is now often treated as the first statement of Coppola's lasting subjects: privileged isolation, and young women watched by others.

## Watch for

- **The opening minutes:** the narrator's first words, and how quickly the film tells us the ending.
- **Cecilia and the doctor** (first five minutes or so): the line, and how the scene is played, flat rather than dramatic.
- **The basement party** (about a fifth in): Cecilia asking to be excused, and the sound that follows.
- **Trip's entrance** (about a third in): the slow motion and the song. Ask whose fantasy this is.
- **The adult Trip** (scattered throughout): how his testimony is framed, and what he leaves out.
- **Dawn on the football field** (just past half): Lux waking alone, and how long the shot holds.
- **The records down the phone** (about three-quarters in): a conversation conducted entirely through songs.
- **The asphyxiation party** (final minutes): the neighbourhood turning the deaths into a theme, and what that says about everyone who has tried to explain them, including the narrator.

## Try it

Write a short piece narrated by "we": a group trying to understand one person they watched but never knew. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Choose the group (classmates, neighbours, colleagues, a team) and the person at the centre, whom they never really spoke to.
2. Tell the outcome in your first paragraph. Then go back.
3. Assemble the middle from fragments: things seen from outside, an object or document, two or three conflicting testimonies. Let the narrators admit at least twice what they don't know.

- **Stage:** Write it as a chorus of three or four voices who speak as "we" but disagree on details. Keep the person at the centre silent, or present only in glimpses.
- **Screen:** Intercut the past with "interviews" of the grown-up witnesses, framed to camera. Give one witness a memory the footage contradicts.
- **Journalism:** For a profile where you can't reach the subject, make the reporting gap visible. Say plainly where the account comes from, and keep your own feelings out of the space where their voice should be.

**Check:** At the end, does the reader know more about the narrators than about the person they were watching, and is that your point?
