## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*Sentimental Value* (*Affeksjonsverdi*, 2025) was directed by Joachim Trier and written by Trier and Eskil Vogt. After their mother's death, two sisters in Oslo, Nora (Renate Reinsve), a stage actress, and Agnes (Inga Ibsdotter Lilleaas), a historian with a young son, are visited by their long-absent father, Gustav Borg (Stellan Skarsgård), a once-celebrated film director whose career has stalled. He has written a comeback film, to be shot in the family house, and offers Nora the lead. She refuses, and he casts a young American star, Rachel Kemp (Elle Fanning), instead. The film won the Grand Prix at Cannes in May 2025 and the Oscar for best international feature in March 2026, Norway's first. Because it's recent, this case study sticks to what reviews and the makers' interviews confirm.

## Why it works

### The house tells the first story

The film begins not with a character but with a building. Over its first few minutes, a narrator reads a school essay the young Nora once wrote from the point of view of the family house, and the images range across a century of its life: the people who lived there, were born there, died there. Trier told RogerEbert.com (2025) that he thought of the house as a constant, a structure that outlasts any human life.

*Light, colour and setting* teaches that a setting can carry meaning; here it carries memory too. The house is where Gustav's mother died, where the sisters grew up, and where Gustav now wants to make his film. By opening with it, the film tells us how to watch everything that follows: as one more layer in a long history, seen from slightly outside. (The *Joachim Trier* lesson traces this framing back through his earlier films.) The production built an exact replica of the house on a studio stage in Drammen, outside Oslo, so that it could be dressed for different periods, a detail that matters at the very end.

### Introduce her on her worst night

After the prologue, we meet Nora on the opening night of a play, so overwhelmed by stage fright that she tries to escape, while her colleagues go to comic lengths to get her on stage. Then, somehow, she goes on.

On the *Scriptnotes* podcast (2026), John August asked Trier whether to introduce a character on an ordinary day or an extreme one, and he chose extreme. *Introducing a character* says a first scene is a thesis, and this one states two: Nora's gift and her terror are the same thing, and acting is how she survives her feelings, not how she expresses them. Reviewers split over whether the sequence is comedy or alarm; it's both, and that mix of registers is the film's tone.

### A film within the film

Gustav's script is about his own mother, Karin, who was in the Norwegian resistance, was tortured during the German occupation, and later took her own life in the house when Gustav was seven. He never talks to his daughters about her, or about much else. The script talks for him.

That's the film's central device, and it's a version of what *Dialogue reveals character* says about subtext: people rarely say the important thing directly. Gustav can't tell Nora he's sorry, so he writes a part for her. Agnes, sensing that the family's damage runs back through Karin, goes to the National Archives to look for the record of what was done to her grandmother, then reads the script herself. She realizes that its final scene echoes something the sisters kept from their father: a suicide attempt of Nora's. The script, supposedly about the past, is about his daughter. *Want, need and wound* describes a wound shaping a character; here a wound is handed down three generations, and the film within the film is where it finally surfaces. Trier told Cineuropa (2025) that the story has no external antagonist: the conflict is inside the people.

### Polyphony: the father gets a point of view

Trier told August on *Scriptnotes* that the film began as the daughter's story, and that he and Vogt realized it also needed the father's point of view. It became, in his word, more polyphonic. *Point of view and voice* explains the term: several voices, none of them simply right.

You can see it in how the film handles Gustav. He is vain, tactless and selfish, and the film lets him be funny and sad too. Rachel Kemp is a third angle. The film could have made the Hollywood star a joke; instead, as she prepares for the role, she comes to understand that the part was written for someone else, and she withdraws. Each of the four leads gets scenes where we are with them rather than judging them, which is why the reconciliation, when it comes, feels earned rather than imposed. *Whose side are we on?* would call this keeping alignment moving between characters, so that allegiance never settles.

### The pull-back

After Rachel leaves, Nora agrees to play the part. The final sequence shows her performing the script's last scene in the house: alone in a room with a stool and a rope, apparently about to do what Karin did. For a few moments we may not realize it's a performance. Then the camera pulls back, in a single unbroken take, and reveals the studio around her: the room is a set, the house a replica, and this is Gustav's film being made.

*The camera's eye* covers how a camera move can change meaning. Here one move reframes the whole climax: the reconciliation between father and daughter happens through making art together, not through a conversation. *Frank Kermode & Peter Brooks* explain how an ending reorganizes what came before; this one tells us the film has been about whether art can carry what people can't say. Critics disagreed sharply on whether that works (see below).

## The shape

The film doesn't follow a strict act structure, but it maps loosely onto the arc in *The shared core*, with the climax delayed to the final scene.

| Stage | What happens | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| Setup | The house's history; Nora's stage fright | Opening minutes |
| Disruption | After their mother's death, Gustav returns and offers Nora the part; she refuses | First fifth |
| Rising | Rachel cast; Gustav's plans for the house; tension with Agnes over her son | About a fifth to a half |
| Midpoint and crisis | Agnes researches Karin and reads the script; what it reveals about Nora | About half to two-thirds |
| Climax | Rachel withdraws; Nora accepts; the final scene is shot | Last fifth |
| Resolution | The pull-back to the set | Final minutes |

## What people said

**Joachim Trier and Eskil Vogt** described the writing in many 2025–26 interviews. On *Scriptnotes* (2026), Trier said they spent around a year in a room together before a draft existed, starting from character rather than plot. To Cineuropa (2025), he described the film as a story with no outside villain, a "me-against-myself" story.

**Critics** reached for *Ingmar Bergman* almost at once. The RogerEbert.com review from the Toronto festival (2025) said it recalls Bergman more than anything Trier had made, and noted a direct visual allusion to *Persona*. *The New Republic* called it a veritable autumn sonata for viewers who know Bergman, and *Time Out*, giving it four stars, compared its family recriminations to Bergman's but with more dark humour. (See the *Autumn Sonata* case study.) Molly Haskell, writing in *Sight & Sound*, praised its handling of emotion.

The dissent was pointed. Peter Bradshaw in *The Guardian* (2025) called it baggy and sentimental in ways it didn't intend. Justin Chang (NPR, 2025) found it let its characters off the hook too easily, with coy and complacent moments next to its perceptive ones. *The New Republic*'s headline called it an upper-middle-brow crowd-pleaser, and one critic at *Spectrum Culture* found that the final pull-back turned the ending into a gimmick.

## Watch for

- **The house essay** (first few minutes): how the images move through time, and whose voice we're hearing.
- **Stage fright** (just after): the cutting as Nora panics, then her performance.
- **Gustav's offer** (about a fifth in): what he says instead of an apology.
- **Rachel preparing** (around the middle): how she learns about the family, and when she starts to see the part isn't hers.
- **Agnes at the archives** (past the middle): what she is looking for, and what it does to her.
- **Agnes reads the script** (about two-thirds in): watch her face as she reaches the last scene.
- **The final take** (last minutes): when you realize it's a performance, and where the camera ends up.

## Try it

Write a scene in which one character says something to another through a piece of art: a script, a song, a letter, a story. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Pick two people who can't say the important thing to each other directly.
2. Write the art object (a page of script, a verse, a paragraph) as the first character made it. It should seem to be about something else.
3. Write the scene in which the second character reads or performs it and understands what it's really about. Give them no line that says so.

- **Stage:** Let the second character rehearse the first one's words on stage, and let the meaning surface in the performance.
- **Screen:** End on a camera move that reveals the context of what we've been watching, as the final pull-back does.
- **Journalism:** In a profile, find a work your subject made (a song, a book, a building) that says something they won't say in an interview, and let the reader see the link.

**Check:** If you removed every line of dialogue about feelings, would we still know what the art object is saying?
