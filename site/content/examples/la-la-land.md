## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*La La Land* (2016) was written and directed by Damien Chazelle, who wrote the script years earlier but could only get it made after *Whiplash* succeeded. Music is by Justin Hurwitz, with song lyrics by Benj Pasek and Justin Paul, photography by Linus Sandgren and editing by Tom Cross. In present-day Los Angeles, Mia (Emma Stone), a barista on a studio lot who keeps failing auditions, and Sebastian (Ryan Gosling), a jazz pianist who dreams of opening his own club, meet, fall in love and push each other toward their ambitions over the course of a year. The ambitions are achieved; the love, in the end, is not.

## Why it works

### Seasons as chapters

The film is divided by title cards naming the seasons: it begins in winter and moves through spring, summer and fall, then returns to winter for an epilogue set five years later. The structure is borrowed from Jacques Demy's *The Umbrellas of Cherbourg* (1964), which Chazelle has repeatedly called his favourite film and his biggest influence, and which is also divided into parts by date and ends with former lovers meeting by chance years later.

The seasons do three jobs. They compress time, letting a year pass in two hours without explanation. They give each chapter a mood: winter is the frustrated loneliness of two people who keep passing each other; spring is the courtship; summer is the happy montage; fall is the rupture. They also make the affair feel as natural and inevitable as weather. *Story vs. telling* would call the season cards a signal of duration: the gaps between them are where time goes missing.

### The "I want" songs

*Stage forms* explains that in the integrated musical the hero sings an "I want" song early, stating the desire that drives the show. *La La Land* has two people with two wants, and it gives each a song.

Mia's want comes in "Someone in the Crowd", early in winter, as her roommates drag her to a party: the hope of being discovered, of being seen among the many. Sebastian's comes more quietly, in "City of Stars", which he sings alone on a pier, then later as a duet at the piano with Mia. In dialogue he states it plainly: he wants to own a club and keep "real" jazz alive.

The film's cleverness is that it states the wants as songs and then tests them with plot. Sebastian joins a commercially successful band led by an old acquaintance, Keith (John Legend), for steady money; the band's slick song, "Start a Fire", is the first number in the film that the hero performs without believing it. *Want, need and wound* is the lens: his want for security has taken over his want for the club. Late in the film, Mia's "Audition (The Fools Who Dream)" restates her want as a credo. Asked at an audition simply to tell a story, she sings about her aunt, who once jumped into a river in Paris, and about the foolish dreamers who keep trying. Sandgren holds it largely in a single slow push-in on her face, the musical's want song reborn as its climax.

### A love story that tells us the barrier early

*Genre as a promise* lays out Pamela Regis's elements of the romance: the meeting, the barrier, the attraction, the declaration, the point of ritual death, the recognition, the betrothal. *La La Land* plays most of them, out of order and with an ironic twist. The first meetings are hostile: Sebastian honks at her in traffic and later brushes past her in a restaurant. The attraction comes in "A Lovely Night", a tap duet on a hillside at dusk in which both insist, in song, that they're not interested. The declaration comes at the Griffith Observatory, where they float up into the planetarium's painted stars.

The barrier is the twist. In most romances the barrier is outside the couple (a family, a misunderstanding, pride). Here it is their dreams, the same things that drew them together. The fall chapter's ritual death is a dinner where Sebastian, home from touring, tells Mia that she liked him better when he was failing, and she tells him he's abandoned his dream. He then misses the opening night of her one-woman show. The recognition never leads to betrothal. On a bench above the city, after her audition, they agree she must go to Paris, and they say they'll always love each other. The genre's promise of union is consciously broken, which is why the film divides audiences.

### Colour as a code for the dream

*Light, colour and setting* describes how a palette sets tone and a colour motif carries meaning. Mia's wardrobe is in saturated single colours, blue, yellow, green, red, against Los Angeles streets often lit at the "magic hour" around sunset. When she and her roommates set out for the party, each wears a dress of a different bright colour, like the girls in a Demy musical. The palette is a promise that the world can be as heightened as a musical; as the relationship cools, the film's colours become more ordinary, until the epilogue brings them back as fantasy.

### The epilogue: a montage of the life not lived

Five years later, Mia is a film star, married to another man, with a young daughter. Out for the evening, stuck in traffic, they leave the freeway and drop into a jazz club. Its sign carries the name Mia once suggested to Sebastian, and he is at the piano. He sees her and plays the melody he was playing on the night they first nearly met.

Then the film breaks into a montage sequence in the sense *Editing* describes, compressing years into minutes, but with a difference: it compresses years that never happened. In this version Sebastian kisses her in the restaurant instead of walking past; he sits in the audience at her show; they go to Paris together; they have the child; they are the couple at the club tonight. It's staged as a full musical number, in painted sets and theatrical light, the film's style at its most artificial. Then the music ends, we're back in the club, and Mia leaves with her husband. At the door, she turns; she and Sebastian exchange a look and a small smile. He counts in the next tune.

*Music and silence* explains why the melody does so much. "Mia and Sebastian's Theme" is a leitmotif: it was the tune that drew Mia into the restaurant in winter, and hearing it now carries the whole relationship back. *Frank Kermode & Peter Brooks* explains why the epilogue gives the film its meaning: the end makes us reread the middle. The montage tells us the love was real, and that it was also the price.

### Dream versus love

The film's argument is a trade-off. Both characters get what they wanted in their "I want" songs; neither gets the other. Chazelle has said he wanted the ending to show the leads apart but meeting again, chasing the feeling of *The Umbrellas of Cherbourg*. Whether it's a tragedy depends on what you think the story's want was. If it was love, the ending is loss. If it was art, the ending is success at a cost, which is also the story of *Whiplash*.

## The shape

The seasons give the structure; Regis's romance elements show where the film departs from the genre.

| Season | What happens | Romance element | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|---|
| Winter | The freeway number; failed auditions; the near-meetings | The society; the meeting (hostile) | Opening to about a fifth |
| Spring | The party; "A Lovely Night"; jazz club; the observatory | Attraction; declaration | About a fifth to two-fifths |
| Summer | Montage of happiness; "City of Stars"; Sebastian joins Keith | The barrier forms | About two-fifths to three-fifths |
| Fall | Touring; the dinner fight; the empty show; Mia goes home | Ritual death | About three-fifths to four-fifths |
| Fall, continued | The audition; the bench above the city | Recognition, without union | About four-fifths to nine-tenths |
| Winter, five years later | The club; the alternate-life montage; the look | (No betrothal) | The last ten minutes or so |

## What people said

**Damien Chazelle** has spoken in many interviews about Jacques Demy and classic Hollywood musicals as his models, and about wanting a musical that keeps the old form's colour and song but lets the disappointments of real life intrude. **Tom Cross**, who also edited *Whiplash*, has said in craft interviews that much of the film was planned meticulously in advance, around the music.

The film opened the Venice Film Festival in 2016, where Emma Stone won the prize for best actress. It received fourteen Academy Award nominations, tying the record, and won six, including Best Director (Chazelle was the youngest winner), Best Actress, Best Cinematography, Best Production Design, Best Original Score and Best Original Song for "City of Stars". At the ceremony it was mistakenly announced as Best Picture before the error was corrected and the award went to *Moonlight*.

**The critics** divided. **Peter Travers**, in *Rolling Stone* (2016), praised it for jolting the movie musical to life for a new century. **Richard Brody**, in *The New Yorker* (2016), was among the most hostile: he wrote that Chazelle had slathered the film in a coercive kind of charm, a criticism that continued his complaint about *Whiplash*'s view of jazz. Others asked why a film that celebrates jazz, a predominantly Black art form, makes a white musician its defender and casts John Legend as the man who leads him toward commercial pop.

## Watch for

- **The freeway opening** (first five minutes): a whole traffic jam bursting into song, staged to look like one continuous take.
- **The near-meetings** (first fifth): count how many times Mia and Sebastian pass each other before they speak.
- **The theme in the restaurant** (about a seventh in): Mia stopping in the street when she hears Sebastian playing. Remember the melody.
- **"A Lovely Night"** (about a quarter in): a love duet in which both singers deny they're in love.
- **The planetarium** (about two-fifths in): the moment the film leaves gravity behind.
- **"Start a Fire"** (around halfway): Mia's face in the concert crowd while Sebastian plays.
- **The dinner fight** (about two-thirds in): the film's least musical scene, two people at a table, with the silences left in.
- **The epilogue montage** (last ten minutes): what changes in each "corrected" moment, and the look at the door.

## Try it

Write the life your characters didn't live, as a compressed montage at the end of a story. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Take a story of yours (or an idea) where two people part. List the three moments where it could have gone the other way.
2. Write the real ending in one short scene: the two meet again briefly, years later.
3. Then write the alternative as a montage: a series of very short beats, each "correcting" one of the three moments, ending where they would be now. Return to the real scene for the last line.

- **Stage:** Stage the alternate life in a different style from the rest (song, movement, changed light), then snap back to the real room.
- **Screen:** Script the montage shot by shot, each image an echo of a scene the audience has already seen, with one returning piece of music binding it.
- **Journalism:** In a piece about a choice someone made (a career, a move, a breakup), include a short passage, clearly marked as their own imagining, of the life they think they'd have had, and then return to the facts.

**Check:** Does the imagined life make the real ending sadder, wiser, or both, and does the reader know which you intended?
