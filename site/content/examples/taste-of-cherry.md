## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*Taste of Cherry* (*Ta'm-e gilas*, 1997) was written and directed by the Iranian filmmaker Abbas Kiarostami, and shared the Palme d'Or at Cannes that year with Shohei Imamura's *The Eel*. Mr. Badii (Homayoun Ershadi), a well-dressed, middle-aged man, drives his Range Rover slowly around the dusty, half-built hills on the edge of Tehran, looking for someone to do a job for a large sum of money. He has decided to kill himself that night and has dug a grave under a tree; he needs someone to come at dawn, call his name, and either help him out if he's alive or shovel earth over him if he's dead. We never learn why, and the film's ending, a switch to video footage of the film crew, is one of the most argued-over in modern cinema.

## Why it works

### The car as a stage

Most of the film takes place inside one car, and most of that inside the front seats. Badii picks people up, drives them along winding dirt roads, makes his offer and listens to their answers. The car works the way a small stage does: it's an enclosed space where two people must face each other, can't easily leave, and have to talk. The windscreen and side windows frame a landscape of quarries, building sites and bare hills, an unfinished world around a man trying to finish his life.

*The quest and the picaresque* traces the road movie back to the old picaresque: a traveller, a road, and a string of episodes with the people met along the way. Kiarostami strips it to its minimum. There's a goal (find someone willing) and a clock (tonight), but the drive itself goes in circles over the same few hills. By most accounts Kiarostami filmed the conversations without his actors sharing the car, often sitting in the passenger's or driver's seat himself and playing the other side of the dialogue. Whatever the method, the result is that each conversation feels like a private confession.

### Three passengers, three answers

The structure is three main encounters, each a different answer to Badii's request, and each a different version of the question of whether life is worth living.

The first is a shy young soldier, a conscript returning to his barracks. Badii is patient and persuasive, talks about his own army days, and drives him up to the grave under the tree. Once the request is clear, the boy is so frightened that he jumps out and runs off down the hill. The second is a seminary student from Afghanistan, who listens courteously and refuses on religious grounds: suicide is forbidden, and he will not help. The third is Mr. Bagheri (Abdolrahman Bagheri), an older taxidermist at the natural history museum, who needs the money for a sick child and agrees to do it, but spends the drive trying to talk Badii out of it.

*The cast as a system* explains that the best ensembles give each character a different answer to the same question. Here the answers move from fear (the soldier), to principle (the seminarian), to experience (Bagheri). The order matters: each refusal or acceptance is more considered than the last, and the film saves the most persuasive voice for the third act.

### The mulberry story

Bagheri doesn't argue; he tells a story. Years ago, newly married and in despair, he went out before dawn with a rope to hang himself from a mulberry tree. When he climbed up to tie the rope, his hand touched the ripe fruit, and he ate one, then another. The sun came up, schoolchildren passed and asked him to shake the branches for them, and he went home with mulberries for his wife. He decided to live.

*Dialogue reveals character* teaches that dialogue is action: people speak to get something. Bagheri's story is an action aimed at a single listener. It is concrete, sensory and personal, and it doesn't demand anything; it just offers an experience Badii can test against his own. Bagheri also takes Badii on a longer route home, a road he says is more beautiful, and asks him whether he wants to give up the sunrise, the moon, the taste of fruit. The film's title comes from this argument, from the small pleasures of being alive. Notice too how much of Bagheri's talk we hear while the camera looks at Badii's face, or at the car small in a long shot. *The face and the cut* shows that the reaction is often the scene: the story's effect plays out on the listener.

### Withholding the backstory

We never find out why Badii wants to die. He doesn't say, and when passengers ask, he deflects or tells them they wouldn't understand. There are no flashbacks, no family, no letter.

*Want, need and wound* warns against the over-explained character, whose past injury is revealed as the key to everything. Kiarostami goes to the opposite extreme. With the cause withheld, Badii's despair can't be solved or dismissed; it just exists, like anyone's. The passengers' responses become our responses, and the film becomes less about one man's psychology and more about a choice anyone might face. It's what *Robert McKee* calls miniplot: a passive, inward protagonist, a conflict that's mostly internal, and an open ending.

### Landscape and long shots

Kiarostami alternates close shots inside the car with very long shots of the car from far away, a small vehicle crawling along a zigzag road on a brown hillside while the conversation continues at the same volume. *The camera's eye* teaches that shot size is emotional distance. The cut from intimate close-up to the remote, almost aerial view puts one man's decision in proportion to a whole landscape: it matters enormously to him, and the earth doesn't notice.

The setting works the same way (see *Light, colour and setting*). In one scene, Badii sits near a construction site watching a machine dump loads of earth down a slope, and the falling dirt pours over his shadow on the ground. It's the clearest image of what he's planning, and nobody says a word about it.

### The coda that breaks the frame

That night, Badii takes a taxi up to the tree and lies down in the grave. Clouds cross the moon, there's thunder and lightning, and the screen goes dark for a long stretch, with only the sound of the storm and the rain. We don't learn whether he dies.

Then the film switches to grainy video footage in daylight, on the same hillside, now green with spring. Soldiers are marching and resting; the film crew is at work; Kiarostami is visible; Ershadi, the actor who played Badii, offers him a cigarette. Over the closing credits plays an instrumental version of "St. James Infirmary" by Louis Armstrong.

*Bertolt Brecht* describes the Verfremdungseffekt: devices that remind the audience they're watching a made thing, so they think rather than simply feel. *Bill Nichols* calls documentary that exposes its own making reflexive. The coda does both. It refuses to answer whether Badii lives, and replaces that question with another: the actor is alive, the hill is green, the people who made this film are here. *Frank Kermode & Peter Brooks* argues that endings give meaning to the middle. Kiarostami gives us two endings, one dark and one bright, and leaves us to put them together.

## The shape

The film is closest to a picaresque road story in three encounters, with a coda. Freytag's pyramid fits only if you treat Bagheri's story as the turning point.

| Stage | What happens | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Badii drives slowly past men looking for work; small offers turned down | Opening to about a sixth |
| First passenger | The soldier; the drive to the grave; he runs | About a sixth to a third |
| Interlude | Badii watching the falling earth | About a third |
| Second passenger | The seminarian refuses | About a third to a half |
| Third passenger | Bagheri agrees; the mulberry story; the longer road | About a half to three-quarters |
| Doubt | Badii seeks Bagheri out at the museum to ask him to make sure he's really dead | About three-quarters to five-sixths |
| Night | The taxi, the grave, the storm, the dark screen | About the last tenth |
| Coda | Video footage of the crew; "St. James Infirmary" | Final minutes |

The most important turn is quiet. After Bagheri's story, Badii goes back to find him, and his request has subtly changed: he now wants to be sure that, if he's only asleep, he'll be woken up. Something has shifted, and the film won't say how much.

## What people said

**Abbas Kiarostami** liked to say that the unexplained parts of his films are completed in the minds of the audience. In interviews around the film's release, including one with Miriam Rosen in *Artforum*, the film was linked to a thought from the Romanian philosopher E. M. Cioran: that the possibility of suicide is what makes life bearable, because it means we stay by choice. Kiarostami treated the coda as part of the film, though he did show it without the video ending in some places; it was dropped from the Italian theatrical release.

**The critics** split sharply. Jonathan Rosenbaum, in the *Chicago Reader* (1998), called it a masterpiece and found the epilogue full of wonder and euphoria despite its subject.

Roger Ebert, in the *Chicago Sun-Times* (1998), gave it one star and found it "excruciatingly boring", a film that he could experience only as a movie, never as a story he believed in. He was not alone: several critics thought the video coda a gimmick that undercut the drama, and some at Cannes reportedly urged Kiarostami to drop it.

## Watch for

- **The opening drive** (first few minutes): Badii looking at men by the road. Notice how the film makes you wonder what he wants.
- **The soldier at the grave** (about a quarter in): the moment the request becomes clear, and how quickly the boy goes.
- **The falling earth** (about a third in): Badii's shadow under the dirt. No dialogue.
- **The seminarian** (about two-fifths in): a calm, principled refusal. Compare its rhythm with the soldier's panic.
- **The mulberry story** (just past halfway): listen to where the camera is while Bagheri talks.
- **The longer road** (about two-thirds in): Bagheri's choice of route, and the shots of the car from far away.
- **The museum** (about three-quarters in): Badii seeking Bagheri out with a new request.
- **The dark screen and the coda** (final minutes): how long the darkness lasts, then the first video image. Ask whether it feels like a betrayal or a release.

## Try it

Write three short scenes in which three different people answer the same request, each refusal or acceptance more thoughtful than the last. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Choose a request that reveals something serious about the person asking, without saying why they're asking.
2. Write three encounters of a page or so each. Make the first answer emotional, the second principled, the third based on experience.
3. In the third scene, let the answer be a story the other person tells, not an argument. Don't reveal whether it works.

- **Stage:** Put two chairs side by side facing the audience, like car seats. Keep the asker in one chair throughout and change only the person in the other.
- **Screen:** Script the third scene so that most of the story is heard while we watch the listener's face, and cut once to a very wide shot.
- **Journalism:** Ask three people the same hard question (what made you stay, why you didn't leave) and structure the piece in rising order of depth, ending with a concrete anecdote rather than a quote that sums it up.

**Check:** Without explaining why the asker wants what they want, does the reader understand what's at stake by the third scene?
