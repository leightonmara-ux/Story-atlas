## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*About Time* (2013) was written and directed by Richard Curtis, the writer of *Four Weddings and a Funeral* and *Notting Hill*, and was presented at the time as his last film as a director. On New Year's Day, 21-year-old Tim Lake (Domhnall Gleeson) is told by his father (Bill Nighy) that the men in their family can travel back in time, though only within their own lives. Tim uses the gift to find a girlfriend, and after some false starts wins Mary (Rachel McAdams). The film then quietly turns into something else: a story about marriage, children, a sister in trouble and a father who is dying, and about what a time traveller should learn to do with an ordinary day.

## Why it works

### One piece of magic, and a dull way to use it

*Blake Snyder*'s rule of Double Mumbo Jumbo says an audience will accept one piece of magic per film, not two. *About Time* takes its single piece and almost refuses to dramatise it. No machine, no paradox, no flashes of light: Tim goes into a dark place (a wardrobe, a cupboard, a toilet), clenches his fists, thinks of the moment, and he's there. The father explains the rules in a few minutes of conversation in his study. You can't go forward, only back to moments you've lived.

Curtis has called it a kind of anti-time-travel film, and the design follows. The rule is kept so simple because the story isn't about time travel. It's about what you would do if you could redo anything, and the answer the film gives is: in the end, not much. The device is a lens held up to ordinary life. In the terms of *Genre as a promise*, the time-travel premise is the convention, and the invention is to aim it at dinner parties, weddings and a hospital corridor.

### The courtship as a comedy of redos

The first half plays the gift for laughs, and the jokes are all versions of Bergson's repetition (see *Comedy structure*): the same moment run again, slightly improved. Tim meets Mary at Dans le Noir, a London restaurant where diners eat in total darkness, so the couple fall for each other's voices before they've seen each other's faces. Then Tim uses time travel to rescue his flatmate Harry's opening night, when an actor forgets his lines, and in doing so erases the evening he met Mary. He has to win her again from scratch, with knowledge she doesn't know he has. Later he revisits their first night together more than once, trying to improve on it.

It's charming, and the film knows it's slightly dubious. Charlotte (Margot Robbie), a summer crush, rejects Tim no matter how many times he replays her farewell. The gift can rearrange chances; it can't make anyone love him. That's the first rule the film teaches its hero, and it's the one that matters for what follows.

### From romcom to family drama

Around halfway, the romance is effectively complete: proposal, then a wedding in a summer storm that blows the marquee about and soaks everyone, which the film plays as joyful chaos rather than disaster. Then a montage carries us through months of their life together in a single Underground passage, set to a performance of Jimmy Fontana's song "Il Mondo" (see *Editing* on the montage sequence).

After that, the film switches contracts, as *Tragedy and comedy* describes modern blends doing. The plots now are a newborn, Tim's sister Kit Kat's drinking and a car crash, and the father's cancer. Genre romance promises, as *Genre as a promise* puts it, a courtship and an optimistic union. *About Time* delivers that early and then asks what happens after the betrothal. It's the risk in the film, and many viewers say it's why it hits harder than Curtis's earlier comedies.

### The rule that costs something

A good magic rule should eventually hurt. The father mentions, almost in passing, that changes made before a child's birth can change the child. When Tim travels back to stop Kit Kat meeting the boyfriend who leads her into trouble, he returns to find that his baby daughter has been replaced by a son. He undoes it, and they help Kit Kat in the present instead, the slow way.

That rule pays off in the film's saddest stretch. After his father dies, Tim can still visit him in the past: they play table tennis, they talk. But Mary wants a third child, and once that child is born, Tim can never go back before the birth without losing it. So the plot forces a choice between the dead father and the unborn child. *Want, need and wound* says the classic climax makes a character choose between what they want and what they need. Tim wants his father; he needs to let him go and live forward. The last trip is to a beach in Tim's childhood, the two of them walking together, the father younger and Tim a small boy.

### A father written from life

The heart of the film is Bill Nighy's father, who has used his gift mostly to read books and to spend time with his family. Curtis has said that the table-tennis games came from his relationship with his own father, and that he lost both his parents in the years before making the film. The father is the mentor in *The cast as a system*'s terms, but also a foil: a man who has already learned what Tim is about to, and who chose an unambitious, contented life with a gift that could have bought him anything.

### Live each day twice

The film's thesis is delivered as a technique. The father's secret: live each day once as normal, then go back and live it again, noticing what you missed the first time. We see Tim do it: a stressful day at work, harried and rushed; then the same day again, with time to notice a shop assistant, enjoy a colleague's joke, take in the beauty of things. It's a Snyder-style Theme Stated beat that arrives late and is acted out instead of said.

Then Tim takes one step further. In the closing voice-over he says he no longer travels at all; he tries to live each day as if he had deliberately come back to enjoy it. The magic, finally, is given up. *Dan Harmon*'s circle ends with the hero returning to the familiar world, changed. Tim's change is that he no longer needs the gift.

## The shape

*Dan Harmon*'s circle fits well, because the film is literally a journey into the unfamiliar (the past) and a return to an ordinary present, with a heavy price paid at the bottom.

| Step | What happens | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| You | Tim's family in Cornwall; New Year's party | Opening |
| Need | He wants love; the father reveals the gift | About a tenth in |
| Go | London; Harry's house; Dans le Noir | About a fifth in |
| Search | Losing and re-winning Mary; the redo comedy | About a quarter to two-fifths |
| Find | Proposal, wedding, the Underground montage, a child | About half to three-fifths |
| Take | Kit Kat's crash; the child who changes; the father's cancer and death | About three-fifths to five-sixths |
| Return | The last visit; the beach; letting the father go | About five-sixths in |
| Change | Living each day once, without the gift | Final minutes |

Read across, the shape is also *Kurt Vonnegut*'s Boy Meets Girl in its first half, then something gentler and sadder that no single shape on his graph quite captures: good fortune that keeps rising while loss accumulates beside it.

## What people said

**Richard Curtis** said in interviews around the release that the film grew from conversations with friends about happiness and what an ideal ordinary day would be, and that the father drew partly on himself and partly on his own father. He described it as his last film as a director, to spend more time with his family and on writing. He has also described the film as a kind of anti-time-travel movie.

**The critics** were divided. Peter Bradshaw, in *The Guardian*, admitted the film needed a sweet tooth but found it heartfelt and well constructed, and was struck by how much Gleeson sounded like the young Hugh Grant. Deborah Ross, in *The Spectator*, was much harsher: her review argued that what Curtis is truly brilliant at is upper-middle-class lifestyle fantasy, with its big houses and untroubled money. Others pointed out that the time-travel rules bend whenever the story needs them to (the final beach trip is hard to square with the stated limits), and some found Tim's repeated do-overs with Mary closer to manipulation than romance. Audience affection has only grown, and the "live each day twice" scene is widely shared.

## Watch for

- **The revelation** (about a tenth in): how flatly the father explains the gift, and how quickly the film moves on.
- **Dans le Noir** (about a fifth in): a meet-cute played on voices in total darkness, then the reveal of faces outside.
- **Harry's opening night** (soon after): Tim saves the play and loses Mary. The first cost of the gift.
- **Charlotte's goodbye** (early, and again later): the one person the gift can't win, and Tim's later choice.
- **The wedding storm** (about halfway): disaster played as delight.
- **The Underground montage** (just after): time passing in one place, and what each pass shows.
- **The child who changes** (about two-thirds in): the rule that finally costs Tim something.
- **Living the day twice** (near the end): compare the two versions shot by shot.
- **The beach** (final stretch): the last trip, and which of them is leading the other.

## Try it

Write the same day twice. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Choose an ordinary day in a character's life, or your own: a commute, a shift, a dinner.
2. Write it once, fast, from inside the character's stress. Keep it to what they would notice in a hurry.
3. Write it again, same events, same lines, but now the character notices three things they missed: a person, a sound, a small kindness. Change nothing else.

- **Stage:** Stage both versions with the same blocking, and let the second differ only in where the character looks and how long they pause.
- **Screen:** Script both as matching shot lists. In the second, add inserts of the three noticed things and slow the cutting.
- **Journalism:** Report one day in someone's working life twice: once as the clock (a day-in-the-life piece), once rewritten around the three details that only the second look found.

**Check:** If a reader saw only the second version, would they know what the character had learned without being told?
