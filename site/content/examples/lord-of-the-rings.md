## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole trilogy, including the ending.*

*The Lord of the Rings* is Peter Jackson's three-film adaptation of J. R. R. Tolkien's novel: *The Fellowship of the Ring* (2001), *The Two Towers* (2002) and *The Return of the King* (2003). The screenplays are by Fran Walsh, Philippa Boyens and Jackson (with Stephen Sinclair also credited on *The Two Towers*), and the three films were shot together in New Zealand as one long production. A hobbit, Frodo Baggins (Elijah Wood), inherits a ring that turns out to be the One Ring of the dark lord Sauron, and must carry it across Middle-earth to be destroyed in the fire where it was made. *The Return of the King* won eleven Academy Awards, including Best Picture and Best Adapted Screenplay.

## Why it works

### A quest with a destination that never moves

*The quest and the picaresque* explains why a quest has built-in momentum: there is a destination, and every episode is a step toward it or away from it. Tolkien's story is an unusual quest because the goal is not to get something but to get rid of it. From the moment Gandalf (Ian McKellen) drops the ring into the fire at Bag End, early in the first film, and the hidden writing appears, the audience knows the end point: Mount Doom. Nine hours later, it is still the end point.

That fixed goal is what lets the trilogy sprawl. Battles, councils, cities and side journeys can pile up because the audience always knows how far Frodo is from the mountain. The films keep reminding us with a simple device: the Ring itself, which grows heavier, and Frodo, who grows thinner, greyer and more withdrawn with each film.

The lesson's other point, that the best quests reveal that the object was not what mattered, lands in the climax. On the edge of the fire, Frodo cannot let go. He claims the Ring and puts it on, and it is Gollum, biting it off his finger and falling, who destroys it. The hero's quest succeeds through his failure, and through a creature he chose to spare.

### The hero's journey, inverted at the end

*Joseph Campbell & Christopher Vogler* maps the monomyth: separation, initiation, return. The first film follows Vogler's early stages almost by the book. Frodo's ordinary world is the Shire at Bilbo's birthday party; Gandalf is the mentor; and the crossing of the first threshold is spoken aloud when Sam (Sean Astin) stops in a field and says that one more step will take him farther from home than he has ever been. The fall of Gandalf in Moria, near the end of the first film, is the mentor removed so that the hero must go on alone.

What makes the trilogy more than a template is how it treats the return. Campbell's hero comes home with a boon and becomes "master of the two worlds". Frodo comes home and cannot stay. In voice-over near the end, he asks how you pick up the threads of an old life when you have come to understand there is no going back. The boon goes to the Shire; the wound stays with Frodo. It is Sam, the supporting character, who gets the classic return: the marriage, the children, the front door.

### Cross-cutting the broken Fellowship

At the end of the first film the Fellowship breaks into three. Frodo and Sam head for Mordor; Merry and Pippin are carried off by orcs; Aragorn (Viggo Mortensen), Legolas and Gimli pursue them. From then on the films are a braid, and *The braided narrative* and *Editing* both explain how that works.

The strands are chosen to cover different ground: the small, intimate journey of two hobbits and a guide they can't trust; the war story in Rohan and Gondor; and, between them, Merry and Pippin, who connect the hobbits' world to the wider one. Tolkien's novel tells *The Two Towers* largely in two separate blocks, first the war, then Frodo's journey. The films alternate instead, so that each strand comments on the others.

The clearest example is the climax of *The Two Towers*, where the siege of Helm's Deep is cross-cut with the Ents marching on Isengard and with Frodo, captured by Faramir and taken to the ruined city of Osgiliath, nearly giving himself up to a Ringwraith. Then, as the battles turn, Sam speaks to Frodo about the stories that matter, the ones where people keep going, and the film cuts across the strands under his words. It's convergence by meaning before it is convergence by plot. In *The Return of the King* the strands converge literally: Aragorn leads an army to the Black Gate as a decoy, so that Sauron's eye is turned away while Frodo and Sam climb the mountain.

### Adaptation as restructuring: Shelob

The novel's *The Two Towers* ends with Frodo stung by the giant spider Shelob and Sam believing him dead. The films move this to *The Return of the King*. *The Two Towers* ends instead with Gollum, alone, deciding to lead the hobbits to an unnamed "her".

The move is a lesson in structure. The decision was made late, while the second film was being finished, and it gave both films a better shape. *The Two Towers* could end on its battle and on Gollum's turn, and *The Return of the King* gained a climax for the Frodo–Sam–Gollum strand. The writers also added a film-only turn to set it up: Gollum frames Sam for eating the last of the elvish bread, and Frodo sends Sam home. Frodo walks into Shelob's lair alone, and Sam's return becomes his finest moment. A plot event has been repositioned to sit at the bottom of a relationship's arc (see *The cast as a system*).

Not every change was welcomed. Faramir, who in the book resists the Ring almost at once, is made to take the hobbits towards Gondor, and many readers felt the character had been diminished.

### Leitmotifs that carry memory

*Music and silence* describes the leitmotif: a short musical idea tied to a character, place or idea, which returns and develops as the story does. Howard Shore's score is the largest modern example, built from dozens of themes in a deliberately Wagnerian way (the musicologist Doug Adams documented them in his 2010 book *The Music of the Lord of the Rings Films*).

You don't need to know the names of the themes to feel the system work. The Shire has a gentle folk melody, heard on whistle and fiddle, which returns whenever the hobbits think of home. Rohan has its own melancholy theme, led by a Norwegian Hardanger fiddle. The Fellowship theme is a heroic brass melody that sounds most fully while the company travels together in the first film, and after the group breaks, it tends to return in fragments. Because each theme is attached to a place or a group, the score can remind us of what is absent: a few bars of the Shire tune in Mordor are a memory of home, and the audience feels the distance without a line of dialogue.

The lighting of the beacons in *The Return of the King*, a chain of signal fires leaping from mountain to mountain, is almost pure music and landscape, and it shows how far the films trust the score to carry story.

## The shape

Vogler's twelve stages fit the whole trilogy, stretched across nine hours (theatrical cuts; positions are approximate):

| Stage | What happens | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary World | The prologue, then the Shire and Bilbo's party | Opening of the first film |
| Call and refusal | Gandalf reveals the Ring; Frodo begs him to take it | First quarter of the first film |
| Mentor, threshold | Gandalf; leaving the Shire; Rivendell and the Council, where Frodo volunteers | First film, first half |
| Tests, allies, enemies | Moria, Gandalf's fall, Boromir's betrayal and death, the Fellowship's breaking | End of the first film through the second |
| Approach | Gollum's guidance, Osgiliath, the stairs of Cirith Ungol, Shelob | Second film into the first half of the third |
| Ordeal | Mount Doom: Frodo claims the Ring; Gollum takes it and falls | Near the end of the third film |
| Road back, return | Rescue by eagles, the coronation, the hobbits come home | Final stretch of the third film |
| Return with the elixir | Sam's marriage; Frodo leaves Middle-earth from the Grey Havens | Last minutes |

The inversion is the point: the elixir is brought home, but the hero cannot keep it.

## What people said

**The makers.** Jackson, Walsh and Boyens recorded commentaries for the extended editions and talked at length in the appendices about restructuring Tolkien, including moving Shelob and changing Faramir. Boyens has acknowledged in interviews that the team took a great deal of criticism for the multiple endings, and the writers have defended them as true to the book, where Frodo's departure from the Grey Havens is the real end. **Howard Shore** won Academy Awards for the scores of *The Fellowship of the Ring* and *The Return of the King*, and he shared the Best Original Song award for "Into the West" with Fran Walsh and Annie Lennox.

**The critics.** Most reviews were enthusiastic, especially by the third film. Roger Ebert, reviewing *The Fellowship of the Ring* in 2001, was more reserved: he felt the film had turned Tolkien's book into a modern sword-and-sorcery epic, with the hobbits pushed off centre stage, and missed the novel's pauses for songs and poems. The endings of *The Return of the King* became the standard complaint in 2003. Peter Travers in *Rolling Stone* declined to join the chorus against them, pointing out that they are in the book, though he still found the closing hobbit embraces too much. On the twentieth anniversary, writers at *Paste* and *The Ringer* published defences of the endings, arguing that after nine hours the audience needs the time to come down.

The debate maps neatly onto *Frank Kermode & Peter Brooks*. One side wants Kermode's concord, a single decisive ending once the Ring is gone. The other argues that the story's real question was never whether the Ring would be destroyed, but what it would cost Frodo, and that question can only be answered at the Grey Havens.

## Watch for

- **The prologue** (first minutes of the first film): how much history Galadriel's narration packs in, so the story can start small in the Shire.
- **The Ring in the fire** (first film, about a sixth in): the moment the destination is fixed.
- **Sam in the cornfield** (first film, about a quarter in): the threshold, said out loud.
- **The breaking of the Fellowship** (end of the first film): three strands set going at once, and the Fellowship theme in pieces.
- **Gollum talking to himself** (the second film, about midway): a split character staged as a dialogue in shot/reverse shot.
- **Helm's Deep cross-cut with Isengard and Osgiliath** (last third of the second film): watch for where each cut lands.
- **The beacons** (third film, about a quarter in): story told by landscape and score.
- **The endings** (the last stretch of the third film): count them, and ask which one the story actually needs.

## Try it

Map your story's strands, then move one event to a different place in the braid. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. List the two or three main lines of action in your work-in-progress, and write the main beats of each in a column.
2. Find one big event that currently sits in the middle of a strand. Move it to the end of a different section, or to the start of the next act, as the films did with Shelob.
3. Write a short paragraph on what the move changes: which relationship it now pays off, and what each section now ends on.

- **Stage:** Cross-cut on stage by splitting the space: two scenes playing in different areas, with the light shifting between them. Where does one scene's line comment on the other?
- **Screen:** Rewrite a climax so that it cuts across at least two strands. Mark each cut, and note what the audience knows at that moment that the characters don't.
- **Journalism:** In a long feature with several people, try alternating their sections rather than telling them in blocks, and end each section where the reader wants more.

**Check:** After the move, does every strand end each section on a question, and does the climax answer the question that has been there since the start?
