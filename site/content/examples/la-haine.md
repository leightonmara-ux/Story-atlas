## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*La Haine* (*Hate*, 1995) was written and directed by Mathieu Kassovitz, then in his late twenties, and shot in black and white by Pierre Aïm, largely on a housing estate in the suburbs west of Paris. It follows three young friends from a *banlieue* (one of the poor high-rise suburbs ringing Paris) through the day after a riot: Vinz (Vincent Cassel), who is Jewish; Hubert (Hubert Koundé), who is Black; and Saïd (Saïd Taghmaoui), who is of North African descent. A friend of theirs, Abdel, is in hospital in a coma after a police beating, and Vinz has found a police revolver lost in the riot and swears that if Abdel dies, he'll kill a cop. Kassovitz won Best Director at Cannes.

## Why it works

### A clock on the screen

Every so often the image cuts to black, a ticking sound starts, and a time appears in white figures. The film covers less than a day, from one morning to about six the next morning, and these cards are how it tells us so.

*Aristotle* noted that tragedy tends to stay "within a single revolution of the sun", and the later "unity of time" grew out of that remark. *La Haine* takes the old idea literally and puts a clock on it. The effect is the one *Frank Kermode & Peter Brooks* describe with the ticking of a clock: we hear *tick-tock*, a beginning and an end, and the interval between becomes charged. Nothing much happens in many of the scenes (the boys loaf on a rooftop, trade stories, wait for trains), but the ticking turns this idle time into countdown. In Kermode's terms the film converts *chronos*, mere passing time, into *kairos*, time that matters because of how it will end. We don't know what the clock is counting down to, only that it is counting.

### The falling man, told twice

The film opens in the dark, with Hubert's voice telling a story. A man falls from the top of a fifty-storey building, and as he passes each floor, to reassure himself, he keeps saying: so far, so good. *Jusqu'ici tout va bien.* What matters, the story ends, isn't the fall; it's the landing. Then an image of the Earth, a petrol bomb falling toward it, and an explosion, before documentary footage of real riots.

At the very end, in the middle of a standoff, Hubert's voice returns with the same story, slightly changed: now it's the story of a society that is falling. The parable becomes a frame. *Story vs. telling* explains how the order of telling can differ from the order of events; here the film puts its moral first, as a riddle, and only at the end shows us what it meant. On a second viewing the opening becomes a prophecy, and every "so far, so good" moment in the middle (the boys getting away with something, getting out of trouble once more) becomes another floor going past.

### Three friends as a cast system

*The cast as a system* says each character should show something about the others that no one else could. Kassovitz builds his trio as three answers to the same situation.

Vinz is anger: he swaggers, imitates De Niro's "you talkin' to me?" scene from *Taxi Driver* in the bathroom mirror, and wants revenge. Hubert is the one who wants out: a boxer whose gym was burned in the riot, he deals a little hash and dreams of leaving the estate, and he argues that hate only breeds more hate (the line from which the film takes its title). Saïd is the go-between, a joker who talks constantly and keeps the other two from fighting. Each is a **foil** for the others, in the lesson's sense: three young men in exactly the same trap, making different choices.

The cast is also a statement. A Jewish, a Black and an Arab young man, friends, in one estate: Kassovitz has said the black-and-white, multi-ethnic world of the film was meant to look like nowhere in particular, so that it could be Paris or Brooklyn. The trio is the system; the police are its pressure.

### The gun on the wall

*Anton Chekhov* gives the most quoted rule in drama: a gun that appears in the first act should go off later. *La Haine* hangs its gun on the wall in the first minutes, when Vinz shows his friends the revolver. All day we wait for him to use it. He threatens to. At night, he finally has his chance: a brawl with a gang of skinheads leaves Vinz with one of them (played, in a cameo, by Kassovitz) at gunpoint. Hubert, furious, urges him to shoot, to show him what killing actually means. Vinz can't do it, and lets the boy go.

In the morning Vinz hands the gun to Hubert. Moments later a policeman, toying with Vinz, holds his own pistol to Vinz's head, and it goes off. The gun that fires isn't the one we were watching. That's the film's reversal, what *Aristotle* calls *peripeteia*: the end arrives by a road we didn't expect, and it is worse than the one we feared. Then Hubert, holding the gun he never wanted, faces the policeman, each aiming at the other. The camera moves in on Saïd, who closes his eyes; we hear a shot; the film ends without showing who was hit.

### Black and white, and a city seen from the outside

Kassovitz has said that black and white was a way of telling the audience they weren't watching a comedy or a sentimental drama, and of making the film feel universal. *Light, colour and setting* treats colour as a code; removing it is a code too. It links the fiction to the news footage of the opening and strips the estate of any prettiness.

The camera changes style with the setting. On the estate it moves freely and fluently among the towers, most famously in a shot that rises out of a window where a DJ is mixing records and floats across the blocks as the sound spills out. When the boys go into Paris, the framing turns cold and estranged. As they look out over the city from a terrace, the background seems to slide away from them while they stay the same size, a lens-and-camera effect (a zoom set against a dolly move) that makes the capital look like a place they can't reach (see *The camera's eye*).

## The shape

The film works as a countdown divided by place: estate, Paris, estate. Its time cards make the structure unusually easy to map.

| Part | What happens | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| Frame | The falling man; the Earth and the petrol bomb; real riot footage | First few minutes |
| Morning on the estate | The three wake up; news of Abdel; Vinz shows the gun; the rooftop; the police | About a twentieth to a third |
| Into Paris | The train; an acquaintance's flat, to collect money owed; Hubert and Saïd arrested and abused in a police station | About a third to three-fifths |
| Night in Paris | Last train missed; an art gallery; an old man's story in a public toilet; news that Abdel has died | About three-fifths to four-fifths |
| The test | The skinheads; Vinz can't shoot | About four-fifths |
| Dawn | Back on the estate; Vinz gives up the gun; the police; the shot; the standoff | Last few minutes, about six in the morning |

The middle third is almost a road movie, a picaresque in the sense of *The quest and the picaresque*: one encounter after another, tied together by the clock and by a destination, home, that they can't get back to.

## What people said

**Mathieu Kassovitz** has said he began writing the script in April 1993, on the day a young man from Zaire, Makome M'Bowolé, was shot dead while in police custody in Paris. In a *Sight and Sound* interview marking the film's twenty-fifth anniversary, he said simply that he made the film because kids die. He and his actors spent time on the estate before filming, and the three leads play characters named after themselves.

**The critics** gave it a strong reception, and the Cannes jury gave Kassovitz Best Director. The film scholar Ginette Vincendeau later wrote a book-length study of it for the BFI Film Classics series. It was widely reported that the prime minister, Alain Juppé, arranged a screening of it for members of his government.

**The dissent** was loud. The police on duty at the Cannes screening turned their backs on Kassovitz and his cast in protest, seeing the film as an anti-police pamphlet. Among critics, as the *A.V. Club* later summed it up, one camp hailed a new French realism while another dismissed the film as slick and Hollywood-influenced, more interested in style and attitude (the *Taxi Driver* homage, the flashy camera) than in the estate's actual life.

## Watch for

- **The opening voice** (first minute): the falling man story, in the dark. Listen for how calm Hubert sounds.
- **The first clock** (within the first few minutes): the ticking, and how it frames what follows.
- **The mirror** (early on): Vinz rehearsing *Taxi Driver*. Who is he performing for?
- **The DJ shot** (in the first half, on the estate): the camera leaving the window and drifting over the estate.
- **The terrace in Paris** (soon after they reach the city): the city sliding away behind the three of them.
- **The police station** (about half in): two officers teach a rookie how to humiliate a suspect. Watch the rookie's face, not theirs.
- **The old man in the toilet** (about two-thirds in): a story about a deportation train with no obvious point. Ask why Kassovitz put it there.
- **The last minute**: the voice returning, Saïd's closed eyes, a shot we don't see.

## Try it

Write a scene with a clock and a parable. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Choose three characters in one bad situation, each with a different answer to it: fight, flee, joke, deny.
2. Write a short story or saying that one of them tells, which is secretly about how the situation will end. Put it first.
3. Write three short scenes, each headed with a time. Keep the time between them uneven. In the last, bring the opening story back in changed words.

- **Stage:** Put a real clock on the set, or have a character announce the time at each scene change. Let the audience hear it.
- **Screen:** Use time cards over black, and decide what sound accompanies them. End on the face of the character who doesn't act.
- **Journalism:** Structure a feature on one tense day (a vote, a verdict, a storm) as a timeline of short, timed sections, opening with a quotation or anecdote that the ending will reframe.

**Check:** Read your opening parable after your ending. Does it now mean something it couldn't have meant the first time?
