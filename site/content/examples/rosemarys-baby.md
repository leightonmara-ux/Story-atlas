## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*Rosemary's Baby* (1968) was written and directed by Roman Polanski, adapted closely from Ira Levin's best-selling 1967 novel, and produced by William Castle for Paramount. Rosemary Woodhouse (Mia Farrow) and her husband Guy (John Cassavetes), a struggling actor, move into the Bramford, a grand old Manhattan apartment building with a dark history. Their elderly neighbours, Minnie and Roman Castevet (Ruth Gordon and Sidney Blackmer), take a smothering interest in them; Guy's career suddenly takes off; and Rosemary becomes pregnant after a night she remembers only as a nightmare. As the pregnancy goes wrong, she starts to suspect that everyone around her, her husband included, wants her baby for something.

## Why it works

### We know only what Rosemary knows

The film almost never leaves Rosemary. She is in nearly every scene, and when Guy goes next door to spend evenings with Roman, we stay home with her, hearing muffled voices through the wall. In the terms of *Story vs. telling*, the film is focalized through her: the camera may be outside her head, but it is restricted to what she can see and hear.

This is the engine of the dread. A thriller that cut away to the Castevets plotting would give us suspense of the bomb-under-the-table kind (see *Genre as a promise*): we'd know, and wait for her to find out. Polanski chooses something stranger. We share Rosemary's partial knowledge, so we share her uncertainty about whether to trust what she half-notices: an over-friendly neighbour, a husband who won't meet her eye, a doctor who tells her not to read books or listen to her friends.

A celebrated small instance comes at the first dinner with the Castevets. Minnie goes into a bedroom to make a phone call, and the shot from the living room shows her only partly, framed by the doorway, her body cut off by the door frame. The cinematographer, William Fraker, later told the story that audiences leaned in their seats as if they could see around the frame. Whether or not every audience did, the shot does what *The camera's eye* describes: framing decides what we're allowed to see, and what we aren't allowed becomes the point.

### The apartment as the antagonist

The Bramford (exteriors shot at the Dakota on Central Park West) is introduced in the credits, as the camera drifts high over Manhattan and settles on the building, and then in a tour. The previous tenant, an old woman, has died, and the couple notice that a heavy secretary desk has been pushed across a closet door. Nobody makes much of it. It pays off in the final minutes, when Rosemary opens that closet and finds a passage into the Castevets' apartment.

*Light, colour and setting* teaches that a good setting embodies the conflict. Here the walls are the conflict: thin enough to hear through, with a door that was hidden. Rosemary redecorates in bright, modern colours, papering over the building's gloomy history, and the film's daylight brightness makes the horror domestic. There is no castle, no storm, only a pleasant flat, neighbours bearing chocolate mousse, and an old friend, Hutch (Maurice Evans), telling her stories about the building's past that she treats as gossip.

### Dread instead of shocks

Edgar Allan Poe argued that every part of a work should serve a single effect (see *Edgar Allan Poe*). Polanski's effect is unease that never discharges into a jump. The violence mostly happens offscreen or is reported: a young woman, Terry, whom Rosemary meets in the laundry room, falls to her death from a window, and we arrive with Rosemary at the body on the pavement. An actor who beat Guy to a part suddenly goes blind, and we hear about it on the phone.

The one sequence of open horror, the night of the conception, is a dream: shards of a yacht, the Pope, naked elderly neighbours chanting, a creature over her. It's staged with enough dream logic that Rosemary, and we, can half-dismiss it in the morning. Guy explains the scratches on her body away, and she lets him. The film's horror is that she keeps accepting explanations.

Krzysztof Komeda's score supports this. Over the opening credits, Mia Farrow's own voice hums a wordless lullaby, sweet and a little off; it returns at the end over the final shots. *Music and silence* calls this kind of music anempathetic in effect: it refuses to sound frightened, and is more frightening for it.

### Ambiguity held until late

Carroll's account of horror in *Genre as a promise* says horror plots are plots of proof: someone must discover the monster and persuade the sceptics. For most of its length, *Rosemary's Baby* withholds the proof. Every clue has a mundane reading. The tannis-root charm, the herbal drinks Minnie brings, Rosemary's dramatic weight loss and pain, her craving for raw meat (she catches herself chewing a raw chicken heart in the kitchen), Dr. Sapirstein's insistence that the pain is normal: all of it could be a frightened pregnant woman, isolated and dismissed, imagining a conspiracy.

Polanski wrote in his autobiography *Roman* (1984) that, as a sceptic who didn't believe in Satan, he wanted to leave open the possibility that Rosemary's experiences were in her imagination. The structure obliges. Even the decisive evidence comes through a puzzle: after Hutch falls into a coma, Rosemary receives his book on witchcraft, and with Scrabble tiles she finds that "Roman Castevet" is an anagram of the name of a notorious Satanist's son. It's proof only if you already believe.

### An ending that refuses rescue

The film's climax is a sequence that *Frank Kermode & Peter Brooks* would recognize as the end by an unexpected road. Rosemary is told her baby died at birth. She hears crying through the wall, takes a kitchen knife, goes through the closet, and finds the coven gathered in the Castevets' living room around a black bassinet. She looks in and is horrified, though we never see what she sees. She is told who the father is. Then Roman asks her to be a mother to the child, and the film ends with her rocking the cradle.

The ambiguity ends: it was all true. But the genre's promised confrontation turns into something else, an acceptance, and the knife is never used. The final turn is that her love for the child is real. *Whose side are we on?* is useful here: we've been aligned with Rosemary for two hours, and the ending leaves that alignment intact while taking away every outcome we wanted for her.

## The shape

The film follows Carroll's "complex discovery plot" from *Genre as a promise*, but stretches discovery across most of the film and turns confirmation and confrontation inside out.

| Stage | What happens | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| Setup | The Woodhouses take the apartment; Hutch's warnings; Terry's death; dinner with the Castevets | Opening to about a quarter |
| Onset | Guy's sudden luck; the mousse and the dream; the pregnancy | About a quarter to two-fifths |
| Discovery | Pain, weight loss, Rosemary's friends urging a second opinion; Hutch's coma and book; the anagram | About two-fifths to three-quarters |
| Confirmation (failed) | Rosemary goes to another doctor, who calls her husband and Sapirstein instead of believing her | About four-fifths |
| Confrontation, then acceptance | The birth; the closet; the coven; Rosemary rocks the cradle | Last ten minutes or so |

The sceptic who must be persuaded is the audience as much as any character.

## What people said

**Roman Polanski** discussed the film in his autobiography, *Roman* (1984). He admired Levin's novel as a thriller and adapted it faithfully, but, by his account, he wanted to keep open a rational reading of Rosemary's experiences, since he didn't believe in the supernatural himself. His screenplay was nominated for an Academy Award, and **Ruth Gordon** won Best Supporting Actress for Minnie, a performance of comic busybody warmth that makes the menace harder to see.

**The critics** were largely, though not entirely, admiring. **Roger Ebert**, in the *Chicago Sun-Times* (1968), called it very good and argued that it didn't depend on a shock ending, so knowing the novel didn't spoil it. **Renata Adler**, in *The New York Times* (1968), was cooler: she praised Mia Farrow but felt that the film, though pleasant, didn't work on its own dark or powerful terms, partly because its world felt too ordinary to frighten. Later critics have often turned that ordinariness into the film's chief virtue, and it is widely regarded as one of the defining American horror films.

**The film is now inseparable from its director's history.** In 1969, the year after it was released, Polanski's wife, the actress Sharon Tate, was murdered with four others by members of the Manson "family". In 1977 Polanski was arrested in Los Angeles and charged with drugging and raping a 13-year-old girl, Samantha Gailey (now Samantha Geimer). He pleaded guilty to unlawful sexual intercourse with a minor and, in 1978, fled to France before sentencing; he has not returned to the United States. Other women have since accused him of sexual assault, which he has denied, and the Academy expelled him in 2018. Many viewers and critics now find that this history shadows a film about a woman whose body is used without her consent by people she trusted.

## Watch for

- **The opening credits** (first two minutes): the slow aerial drift over the city to the Bramford, with Farrow's humming lullaby.
- **The secretary desk** (the apartment tour, first few minutes): the heavy piece of furniture pushed across a closet. Remember it.
- **Terry in the laundry room** (about a tenth in): the charm she wears, and what happens to it.
- **Minnie's phone call** (first dinner with the Castevets, about a fifth in): how much of her the doorway hides.
- **The dream** (about a third in): how Polanski mixes the real (the bedroom, Guy) with the impossible, so that the morning's explanations almost work.
- **The raw chicken heart** (past halfway): Rosemary catching her own reflection.
- **The phone booth** (about four-fifths in): Rosemary waits for a call, and a man stands outside with his back to her. Notice how much fear comes from what she can't see.
- **The bassinet** (last minutes): we never see the baby. Watch Farrow's face, and what she does with the knife.

## Try it

Write a scene in which your protagonist half-notices something wrong, and the reader can't be sure whether to trust them. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Choose one character and restrict yourself to what they see, hear and are told. No cutaways, no scenes they aren't in.
2. Plant three details that have both an innocent and a sinister explanation. Write each so the innocent reading is slightly easier.
3. Give another character a plausible explanation for the strangest detail, and let your protagonist accept it.

- **Stage:** Put the threat behind a wall, a door or offstage. Use sound, a muffled voice, a knock, to make the audience lean toward what the character can't see.
- **Screen:** Frame one key action so that the door, a shoulder or the edge of the frame hides what matters. Note in the script what is withheld.
- **Journalism:** In a piece about someone who suspected wrongdoing before it was proven, tell the early part strictly from what they knew at the time, and resist telling the reader what was later confirmed until the point they learned it.

**Check:** Could a reader finish your scene and argue honestly for either explanation?
