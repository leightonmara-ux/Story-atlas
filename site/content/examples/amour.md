## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*Amour* (2012) was written and directed by the Austrian filmmaker Michael Haneke, shot in French and photographed by Darius Khondji. Georges (Jean-Louis Trintignant) and Anne (Emmanuelle Riva) are retired piano teachers in their eighties, living in a large, book-lined apartment in Paris. One morning Anne has a stroke; an operation fails and leaves her partly paralysed, and she makes Georges promise never to send her back to hospital. The rest of the film follows him caring for her at home as she declines, until he ends her life himself. It won the Palme d'Or at Cannes in 2012, Haneke's second, and the Academy Award for best foreign-language film.

## Why it works

### Telling the ending first

The film opens with the police breaking down the apartment's front door. The flat smells; the men cover their noses and open windows. A bedroom door has been sealed with tape. Inside, Anne lies dead on the bed, dressed, with cut flowers arranged around her head. Then the title, and the story goes back to the beginning.

*Story vs. telling* calls this a prolepsis, a flash forward: the telling jumps ahead of the events. Here it changes everything that follows. Because we know how it ends, we can't watch for what will happen. We watch for how, and for when, and every small domestic scene becomes a step toward that sealed room. *Frank Kermode & Peter Brooks* argues that we read for the end, using it to make sense of the middle. Haneke hands us the end on page one, and the effect is not to kill suspense but to turn it into dread. And since no rescue is coming, we attend to the care itself.

### One apartment as the whole world

Apart from the opening concert and a short trip home on the bus, almost the entire film happens in the apartment. Haneke has said its floor plan reproduces that of his parents' flat in Vienna, rebuilt with Parisian decor, and that imagining his characters in a space he knew helped him write. We learn the rooms the way the characters live in them: the hallway, the kitchen where they eat breakfast, the bedroom, the salon with the grand piano and books.

*Light, colour and setting* teaches that a good setting embodies the conflict. As Anne's world shrinks, so does the film's. The apartment starts as a home full of culture; it becomes a nursing ward, then a sealed room. *Ingmar Bergman*'s lesson on the chamber film explains why so few elements can grip: one place means pressure builds rather than leaking away. And Chekhov's frame of arrivals and departures (see *Anton Chekhov*) structures the outside world's visits: the couple's daughter Eva (Isabelle Huppert), a former pupil, the building's concierges, hired nurses. Each arrives, disturbs the routine, and leaves Georges alone with Anne again.

### Long takes that refuse to look away

Haneke's camera is mostly still, and his takes are long. The first scene after the opening is a single fixed shot of a concert-hall audience, seen from the stage, as people take their seats and a pianist (Alexandre Tharaud, playing a former pupil of Anne's, under his own name) begins a recital. We are never shown the pianist during the performance; it can take a while even to find Georges and Anne in the crowd. Then the camera simply waits.

*The camera's eye* explains that a long take earns its cost when continuity of time and space is the meaning. Here the meaning is the passage of time. Haneke holds scenes of washing, feeding, slow walking exercises and dressing far longer than a conventional film would, and lets the camera stand at a distance in doorways. The duration is the point: caregiving is made of repetition, and the film makes us feel the hours. It also refuses voyeurism. When things are hardest, the camera often stays at a respectful distance in the next room.

### The first sign, in silence

The stroke itself is shown without drama. At breakfast, Anne suddenly stops responding. She sits with her eyes open, unseeing, while Georges talks to her, then dabs her face with a wet cloth. He leaves the tap running. When he goes to get dressed and fetch help, he hears the tap being turned off, and returns to find her back to herself, with no memory of what happened.

This is *Dialogue reveals character* by subtraction. There's no score and no speech to explain what we're seeing; only the sound of the water, and its sudden stop. Their half-joking exchanges before and after tell us about the marriage: decades of routine, affection carried in small irritations. When Anne insists she is fine, and Georges insists something is wrong, the argument is already the whole film in miniature.

### Offscreen sound, silence and no score

*Amour* has no music score at all. All the music we hear exists inside the story: the recital, a piano piece Anne plays, a CD in the apartment. *Music and silence* explains that withholding a score creates its own tension, and that silence makes us lean in. Here it also means that the music we do hear is precious and specific to these characters' lives. In one scene we see Anne at the piano, healthy, playing Schubert; then the film cuts to Georges at the CD player, turning it off. The memory, or fantasy, is interrupted by the machine.

Much of the most painful material is heard rather than seen. After a second stroke Anne loses most of her speech and repeats a single word, *mal* (it hurts), from the bedroom while Georges is elsewhere in the flat. *Sound inside and outside the story* shows how offscreen sound widens the world beyond the frame. Haneke uses it to put us where Georges is: in the next room, listening. The film also plants an unseen threat early on. Returning from the concert, Georges and Anne find that someone has tried to force their front door. Nothing more comes of it, except that the first image of the film was a door being broken down.

### The pigeon

Twice a pigeon flies into the apartment through an open window in the hall. The first time, Georges shoos it out. The second time, late in the film, after Anne's death, he follows it patiently, throws a blanket over it and catches it, then sits holding it in his arms.

Haneke has said he has no interpretation of the scene, and that if asked how the pigeon got into the film, his answer is that it flew in through the window. Viewers have read it as death, as Anne's spirit, as the outside world, as Georges finally able to hold something gently. What matters for a writer is that the image is concrete before it's symbolic: it is first of all a real nuisance in a real flat, and its second visit rhymes with the first. *The face and the cut* teaches that the audience completes the shot. Haneke trusts that the image, placed where it is, will carry more than any line could.

## The shape

The film's structure is a long, slow decline told after its ending. *Gustav Freytag*'s pyramid fits loosely, but the most useful frame is *Story vs. telling*: chronological order, with one flash forward at the front.

| Stage | What happens | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| Prolepsis | The police break in; Anne's body among flowers | First few minutes |
| Exposition | The recital; the tampered lock; home life | Up to about a tenth |
| Inciting incident | The breakfast stroke; the failed operation | About a tenth to a fifth |
| Rising action | Anne home in a wheelchair; the promise; daily routines; Eva's visits; the former pupil's visit | About a fifth to a half |
| Further decline | A second stroke; speech lost; nurses hired and fired; Georges's exhaustion | About a half to five-sixths |
| Climax | Georges tells Anne a story from his childhood to calm her, then smothers her with a pillow | About five-sixths |
| Falling action | Georges cuts flowers, seals the room, catches the pigeon | Final stretch |
| Coda | Anne, well again, washing dishes; she and Georges leave the flat together. Eva walks into the empty apartment | Last minutes |

What the pyramid misses is that there is no rise. The line simply falls, and the climax is an act of love that looks like a killing. The coda, with Anne restored and leading Georges out, is shown plainly, as if real, and left for us to interpret.

## What people said

**Michael Haneke** has said that the starting point was his aunt, who raised him; elderly and in great pain, she asked for his help in ending her life. He has insisted the film isn't about his parents, although the apartment's plan comes from theirs. He wrote the part of Georges for Jean-Louis Trintignant. He has said that the film is about how to deal with the suffering of someone you love, and he has generally declined to explain its images, the pigeon included. **Emmanuelle Riva**, then 85, was nominated for the Academy Award for best actress, at that time the oldest nominee in the category.

**The critics** were overwhelmingly admiring. Peter Bradshaw, in *The Guardian* (2012), wrote from Cannes that it was filmmaking of the highest intelligence and insight, and later named it his film of the year. A. O. Scott, in *The New York Times* (2012), also put it at the top of his year's list, and argued that Haneke's restraint keeps the story from tipping into melodrama.

The dissent came from those who found it controlled to the point of coldness. Richard Brody, in *The New Yorker* (2012), felt that Haneke's gaze was as fixed and chilly as ever, and that the film didn't take in the energy of its two great actors. Some festival reviewers found it too buttoned-up and polished, built for prizes.

## Watch for

- **The opening** (first few minutes): the door, the sealed room, the flowers. Notice what we learn before we know who she is.
- **The concert** (just after): the one long shot of an audience. Try to find Georges and Anne.
- **Breakfast** (about a tenth in): the running tap, and the moment it stops.
- **The promise** (about a fifth in): Anne in her wheelchair, asking Georges never to send her back.
- **The photo album** (around halfway): Anne turning the pages and remarking on how beautiful, and how long, life is.
- **The piano and the CD player** (later): Anne playing, then Georges switching the music off.
- **The pigeon, twice** (once in the middle, once near the end): how Georges deals with it each time.
- **The last shot** (final minutes): Eva alone in the empty apartment. Notice where she sits.

## Try it

Write the last scene of a story first, then write a scene from the middle that the ending changes. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Write a short opening that shows the outcome: an empty room, a final document, a body, a closed shop. No explanation.
2. Write a scene from much earlier, an ordinary domestic moment (a meal, a chore, a joke), in which nothing seems to go wrong.
3. Reread the second scene with the first in mind. Change one detail so that, knowing the ending, the reader feels it as a warning.

- **Stage:** Open with the final image as a tableau before any dialogue, then bring the lights up on the earlier scene in the same set.
- **Screen:** Shoot the domestic scene in one or two long, static takes. Put one important sound offscreen.
- **Journalism:** Open a feature with the end of the story (a verdict, a death, a closure), then return to an ordinary day long before. Let the reader watch the ordinary day knowing what's coming.

**Check:** Does knowing the ending make the middle scene more tense, not less? If the earlier scene reads the same either way, find the detail that the ending should change.
