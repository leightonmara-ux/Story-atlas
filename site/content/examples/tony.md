## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*Tony* (2026) is A24's film about Anthony Bourdain, directed and co-written by Matt Johnson (*BlackBerry*), with Dominic Sessa (*The Holdovers*) as the 19-year-old Bourdain, Antonio Banderas as the chef who runs the kitchen, Leo Woodall as a cook called Sal and Emilia Jones as Nancy. It opened in US cinemas on 7 August 2026, after a preview at the Provincetown International Film Festival in May. It covers one summer: in 1975 a privileged college kid who wants to be a novelist misses out on a writing fellowship, follows a girl to Provincetown, Massachusetts, and ends up washing dishes in a seafood restaurant. The famous Bourdain never appears; the film stops as the boy starts to become a cook.

## Why it works

### One summer instead of a life

Most biopics march from childhood to death, touching every famous moment. *Tony* skips that. Johnson has said he took stories from the early chapters of Bourdain's memoir *Kitchen Confidential* (2000) and from interviews with people who knew him in Provincetown, and compressed several summers into a single one, set in 1975.

That choice turns a biography into a coming-of-age story. *The bildungsroman* promises formation, an education from mistakes, mentors and work, and an ending that answers "who will they be?", and it advises picking the one season where formation happens fastest. Johnson did exactly that, with a twist on the *künstlerroman*, the artist's coming-of-age: Tony arrives believing he is a writer and discovers he is meant to be a cook. We know what he doesn't: the kitchen will give him his subject.

### A lie as the engine

The film opens at Vassar, where Tony is trying for a summer fellowship to write a novel, and it falls through. In Provincetown he tells Nancy, his high-school crush, that he's there for a few months to research and write one. The rest of the plot grows from that lie.

*Want, need and wound* gives the vocabulary. Tony's **want** is concrete: Nancy, and the status of being a writer. His **need**, which the film shows him meeting without ever saying it aloud, is to find work he is actually good at, among people who judge him by what he does rather than what he claims. The want keeps him performing; the dish pit, where nobody cares about his novel, exposes the performance. Reviewers found the romance the weaker strand (the *Daily Beast* called Nancy little more than a plot device), which is telling: she matters as the reason for the lie, while the kitchen is where the story happens.

### Making us care about a cocky liar

Tony is privileged, arrogant and dishonest, and the film doesn't hide it. Reviewers praised the balance Sessa strikes between cocky and lost; Owen Gleiberman in *Variety* singled out the anger and heat in the performance.

*Making us care* starts from the point that caring isn't liking. Tony starts at the bottom, a dishwasher in a hierarchy with no use for a Vassar boy: close to Michael Hauge's undeserved misfortune, except that Tony half deserves it. Above all, the film relies on Emma Coats's rule that we admire a character more for trying than for succeeding: what binds us is watching him start, grudgingly, to work.

### Two mentors who pull in opposite directions

The kitchen gives Tony two teachers. Banderas plays the owner and head chef, known simply as Chef, whom some critics thought stole the film. Woodall's Sal is the reckless cook who befriends Tony and draws him into the kitchen's excesses. Reviews describe Sal unravelling until a violent clash with Chef gets him fired, and his departure is what moves Tony from the sink to the stove.

This is *Memorable side characters* at work: the minor character who changes the hero. It is also *The cast as a system*. Chef and Sal are two answers to the question of what kind of cook, and man, Tony might become: one disciplined, the other charismatic and self-destructive. Anyone who knows how Bourdain's life went will feel the weight of that, because both paths were in him.

### Introducing a man everyone already knows

*Tony*'s audience arrives with a fully formed Bourdain: the voice, the swagger, the television persona. Johnson has said that focusing on the young Tony freed him from recreating the older, famous man, and Sessa has described not doing an impression. Bourdain was in his forties when he wrote *Kitchen Confidential*, so Sessa tried to imagine him at 19.

*Introducing a character* describes the delayed entrance, where others' talk builds a figure before he appears and the gap between reputation and reality becomes the point. *Tony* stretches that across a whole film: our memory of Bourdain does the build-up, and we meet a boy who isn't him yet, so every flicker of the later man lands as recognition.

### Invention in a true story

*Tony* invents freely. *Rolling Stone* reported that Chef is a composite of several mentors from Provincetown and New York. NPR's John Powers noted that there was no Sal, and that the real Bourdain wasn't the innocent the film makes him seem. The Nancy plot is also invented. The real restaurant, the Flagship, which Bourdain called the Dreadnaught in *Kitchen Confidential*, was merged with another Provincetown restaurant for the film.

*Janet Malcolm & John Hersey* sets out the terms: composites, compression and invented events would be unacceptable in journalism, while "based on a true story" carries a looser reader's contract, and the test is whether the invention changes the meaning of what happened. Powers expected the filmmakers to answer that the film is emotionally rather than literally true. Johnson has said in interviews that he didn't feel bound by factual accuracy but did feel an obligation to get at the truth,, and that the Provincetown locals he interviewed disagreed about how much Bourdain had exaggerated.

## The shape

The film follows the classic *bildungsroman* pattern, compressed into one season and ending in accommodation: Tony finds his place, though not the one he came looking for. Positions are approximate.

| Stage | What happens | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| Departure | Vassar; the fellowship falls through; Tony follows Nancy to Provincetown | Opening |
| The false self | The lie that he's writing a novel; broke, he takes a dishwashing job | First third |
| Education | Life in the kitchen under Chef, alongside Sal | Middle |
| Crisis | Sal's fall and firing; Tony moves from the sink to the stove | About two-thirds to three-quarters |
| Accommodation | The clambake fundraiser; Tony gives the money to the damaged restaurant | Final stretch |
| Coda | A glance toward the man he will become | Last minutes |

## What people said

**The makers.** Johnson has said he read everything Bourdain wrote and set out to meet everyone in Provincetown who had known him, living there for months before filming (in interviews with IndieWire, CBC and others). Sessa has said he knew little about Bourdain before taking the part, and that the role changed his life (TheWrap); in NME he argued that Bourdain's life was not a tragedy but a symbol of hope.

**The critics** were mostly warm (Metacritic: 75, "generally favorable"). Gleiberman in *Variety* called it Sessa's first movie-star performance. NPR's Powers (7 August 2026) found it fast, funny and touching while listing what it invents. RogerEbert.com called it an excellent mini-biopic that avoids the easy recipes. Clarisse Loughrey in *The Independent* found it lovely and likeable, but thought the writers seemed obliged to gesture at Bourdain's death at the end, when Sessa's performance had already made the sadness clear.

**The dissent** was sharp. Coleman Spilde in *Salon* (August 2026) argued that Johnson was so devoted to the nostalgic public idea of Bourdain that the film plays it far too safe. *Rolling Stone*'s review dismissed it as superhero origin-story fan fiction. *Screen Daily* felt clichés and feel-good bromides crept in near the end, and *Pajiba* complained that the film never really shows how Tony falls in love with cooking.

## Watch for

- **Vassar** (opening minutes): how quickly the film sets up Tony's ambition and entitlement.
- **The lie to Nancy** (early): note what Tony claims to be, then count how often later scenes test it.
- **The dish pit** (first third): where the camera puts Tony in the kitchen's hierarchy, and who ignores him.
- **Chef and Sal** (middle): set their scenes with Tony side by side. What does each one teach him?
- **Sal's exit** (about two-thirds in): the fight with Chef, and how one man's fall becomes the other's chance.
- **The clambake** (final stretch): what Tony does with the money, and whether you believe it.
- **The last minutes:** does the nod to the later Bourdain earn its place, or had the performance already said it, as Loughrey argued?

## Try it

Write the "before" scene of a real person, or a character based on one, at the moment before they became who they're known as. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Choose the subject and one season of their life when everything was still undecided.
2. Give them a pretense to keep up, and put them somewhere that doesn't care about it (a job, a team, a kitchen).
3. Write a scene where the pretense and the work collide. Let one action, not a speech, hint at the later person.
4. List every invented or compressed element in the margin.

- **Stage:** One location, one night. The audience's knowledge of who the person becomes is your offstage build-up; don't name it.
- **Screen:** Write the introduction line for your subject as they are *now*, not as they'll be. Then find the first action that hints at the future without any dialogue.
- **Journalism:** Reconstruct a formative moment in a real person's life from interviews only. Mark each sentence with its source; anything you couldn't source goes, and any compression gets a note to the reader.

**Check:** Could a reader who knows nothing of the famous version still care about this person, and does your margin list contain anything that changes what really happened?
