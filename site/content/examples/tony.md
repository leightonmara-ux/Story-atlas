## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*Tony* (2026) is A24's film about Anthony Bourdain, directed and co-written by Matt Johnson (*BlackBerry*), with Dominic Sessa (*The Holdovers*) as the 19-year-old Bourdain, Antonio Banderas as the chef who runs the kitchen, Leo Woodall as a cook called Sal and Emilia Jones as Nancy. It opened in US cinemas on 7 August 2026, after a preview at the Provincetown International Film Festival in May. Instead of a life story, it covers one summer: in 1975 a privileged college kid who wants to be a novelist misses out on a writing fellowship, follows a girl to Provincetown, Massachusetts, and ends up washing dishes in a seafood restaurant. The famous chef, writer and television host never appears; the film stops just as the boy starts to become a cook.

## Why it works

### One summer instead of a life

The usual biopic marches from childhood to death and touches every famous moment. NPR's critic praised *Tony* for skipping that entirely. Johnson has said he took stories from the early chapters of Bourdain's memoir *Kitchen Confidential* (2000) and from interviews with people who knew him in Provincetown, and compressed several summers into a single one, set in 1975.

That choice turns a biography into a coming-of-age story, and *The bildungsroman* explains why it's the stronger shape. The form promises formation (we watch someone become someone), an education from mistakes, mentors and work, and an ending that answers "who will they be?" It also advises the stage writer to pick the one season where formation happens fastest. Johnson did exactly that. The film has a neat twist on the *künstlerroman*, the artist's coming-of-age: Tony arrives believing he is a writer and discovers he is meant to be a cook. The audience knows something he doesn't, that the kitchen will eventually give him the thing he came to write about.

### A lie as the engine

The film opens at Vassar, where Tony is trying for a summer fellowship to write a novel, and it falls through. In Provincetown he tells Nancy, his high-school crush, that he's there for a few months to research and write one. The rest of the plot grows from that lie.

*Want, need and wound* gives the vocabulary. Tony's **want** is concrete: Nancy, and the status of being a writer. His **need**, which the film shows him meeting without ever saying it aloud, is to find work he is actually good at, among people who judge him by what he does rather than what he claims. The want keeps him performing; the dish pit, where nobody cares about his novel, keeps exposing the performance. Reviewers agreed that the romance is the weaker strand (the *Daily Beast* found Nancy little more than a plot device), and in structural terms that's telling: she matters as the reason for the lie, while the kitchen is where the story happens.

### Making us care about a cocky liar

Tony is privileged, arrogant and dishonest, and the film doesn't hide it. Several reviewers singled out the balance Sessa strikes between cocky and lost, and Owen Gleiberman in *Variety* praised the real anger and heat in the performance.

*Making us care* explains how a film holds us to a character like that. Caring isn't liking. Tony starts at the bottom (a dishwasher in a hierarchy that has no use for a Vassar boy), which is close to Michael Hauge's undeserved misfortune, except that Tony half deserves it, and the film lets him be funny about it. Above all, the film relies on Emma Coats's rule that we admire a character more for trying than for succeeding: what binds us is watching him start, grudgingly, to work.

### Two mentors who pull in opposite directions

The kitchen gives Tony two teachers. Banderas plays the owner and head chef, known simply as Chef, whom several critics called the best thing in the film. Woodall's Sal is the reckless cook who befriends Tony and draws him into the kitchen's excesses. Reviews describe Sal's drug use getting worse until a violent clash with Chef gets him fired, and his departure is the opening that moves Tony from the sink to the stove.

This is *Memorable side characters* at work: the minor character who changes the hero. It is also *The cast as a system*. Chef and Sal are two answers to the same question, what kind of cook (and man) Tony might become: one disciplined and generous, the other charismatic and self-destructive. Viewers who know how Bourdain's life went will feel the weight of that choice, because both paths were in him.

### Introducing a man everyone already knows

Most introductions build a character from nothing. *Tony* has the opposite problem: the audience arrives with a fully formed Bourdain, the voice, the swagger, the television persona. Johnson has said that focusing on the young Tony freed him from recreating the older, famous man, and Sessa has described not doing an impression. Johnson gave him *Kitchen Confidential* and particular episodes of Bourdain's shows, but because Bourdain was in his forties when he wrote the memoir, Sessa tried to imagine who he might have been at 19.

*Introducing a character* describes the delayed entrance, where others' talk builds a figure before he appears, so that the gap between reputation and reality becomes the point. *Tony* applies the trick to a whole film. Our memory of Bourdain does the build-up, and what we meet is a boy who isn't him yet. Every flicker of the later man in Sessa's performance lands as recognition.

### Invention in a true story

*Tony* invents freely, and the press asked how much. Rolling Stone reported that Chef is a composite of several mentors from Provincetown and New York. NPR's John Powers noted that there was no Sal, and that the real Bourdain wasn't the innocent the film makes him seem. The Nancy plot is also invented. The real restaurant, the Flagship, which Bourdain called the Dreadnaught in *Kitchen Confidential*, was merged with another Provincetown restaurant for the film.

*Janet Malcolm & John Hersey* sets out the terms. Composites, compression and invented events would be unacceptable in journalism. The lesson notes that "based on a true story" carries a looser reader's contract, and its test is whether the invention changes the meaning of what happened. Powers framed the filmmakers' answer as emotional rather than literal truth, and found the film emotionally satisfying. Johnson told IndieWire that he didn't feel bound by factual accuracy but did feel an obligation to get at the truth, and that the people he interviewed disagreed with one another: some said Bourdain's own stories were exaggerated, others that the reality was wilder. *Tony* is a useful case for any writer weighing how far to bend a real life.

## The shape

The film follows the classic *bildungsroman* pattern, compressed into one season and ending in accommodation: Tony finds his place, though not the one he came looking for. Positions are approximate, from reviews and summaries rather than timings.

| Stage | What happens | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| Departure | Vassar; the fellowship falls through; Tony follows Nancy to Provincetown | Opening |
| The false self | The lie that he's writing a novel; broke, he takes a dishwashing job | First third |
| Education | Life in the kitchen under Chef, alongside Sal | Middle |
| Crisis | Sal's fall and firing; Tony moves to the stove; the lies catch up with him | About two-thirds to three-quarters |
| Accommodation | The clambake fundraiser; Tony gives the money to the damaged restaurant | Final stretch |
| Coda | A glance toward the man he will become | Last minutes |

## What people said

**The makers.** Johnson has said he read everything Bourdain wrote and set out to meet everyone in Provincetown who had known him, living there for months before filming (IndieWire, *Rolling Stone*, CBC). He has also discussed meeting Bourdain briefly at a magazine photo shoot in 2005. Sessa has said he knew little about Bourdain before taking the part, and that preparing for it changed his life (TheWrap); in NME he argued that Bourdain's life was not a tragedy but a symbol of hope.

**The critics** were mostly warm: Metacritic's score settled at 75, "generally favorable". Gleiberman in *Variety* called it Sessa's first movie-star performance. NPR's Powers, on 7 August 2026, found it fast, funny and touching, while listing what it invents. RogerEbert.com praised it as a mini-biopic that avoids the easy recipes, adapting a couple of early chapters of *Kitchen Confidential*. Clarisse Loughrey in *The Independent* found it lovely and likeable, but thought the writers seemed obliged to gesture at Bourdain's death at the end, when Sessa's performance had already made the sadness clear.

**The dissent** was sharp. Coleman Spilde in *Salon* (August 2026) argued that Johnson was so devoted to the nostalgic public idea of Bourdain that the film plays it far too safe. *Rolling Stone*'s review dismissed it as superhero origin-story fan fiction. *Screen Daily* felt clichés and feel-good bromides crept in near the end, and *Pajiba* complained that the film never really shows how Tony falls in love with cooking.

## Watch for

- **Vassar** (opening minutes): how quickly the film establishes Tony's ambition, and his sense of entitlement, before Provincetown.
- **The lie to Nancy** (early): note what Tony claims to be, then count how often later scenes test it.
- **The dish pit** (first third): where the camera puts Tony in the kitchen's hierarchy, and who ignores him.
- **Chef and Sal** (middle): set their scenes with Tony side by side. What does each one teach him?
- **Sal's exit** (about two-thirds in): the fight with Chef, and how one man's fall becomes the other's chance.
- **Tony at the stove** (after Sal leaves): Sessa's physical confidence compared with the dish pit.
- **The clambake** (final stretch): what Tony does with the money, and whether you believe it.
- **The last minutes:** decide whether the nod to the later Bourdain earns its place, or whether, as Loughrey argued, the performance had already said it.

## Try it

Write the "before" scene of a real person, or a character based on one, at the moment before they became who they're known as. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Choose the subject and one season of their life when everything was still undecided.
2. Give them a lie or pretense they are keeping up, and put them somewhere that doesn't care about it (a job, a team, a ward, a kitchen).
3. Write a scene in which the lie and the work collide. Let one action, not a speech, show the later person briefly showing through.
4. List every invented or compressed element in the margin.

- **Stage:** Keep it to one location and one night. The audience's knowledge of who the person becomes is your offstage build-up; don't name it on stage.
- **Screen:** Write the introduction line for your subject as they are *now*, not as they'll be. Then find the first action that hints at the future without any dialogue.
- **Journalism:** Reconstruct a formative moment in a real person's life from interviews only. Mark each sentence with its source; anything you couldn't source goes, and any compression gets a note to the reader.

**Check:** Could a reader who knows nothing of the famous version still care about this person, and does your margin list contain anything that changes what really happened?
