## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole series, including the ending.*

*I May Destroy You* is a twelve-episode drama created and written by Michaela Coel, who also stars and co-directed, broadcast by BBC One and HBO in the summer of 2020. Arabella Essiedu, a London writer whose online essays made her a minor celebrity, is behind on her second book. She breaks off an all-night writing session for a drink with friends and wakes the next day with a cut on her head, a cracked phone and almost no memory; slowly she understands that her drink was spiked and she was raped. The series follows her and her two closest friends, Terry (Weruche Opia) and Kwame (Paapa Essiedu), through a year of reckoning with what consent means, and it ends not with one finale but with several. Coel has said publicly that the story began with her own assault, which happened while she was writing the second series of her sitcom *Chewing Gum*.

## Why it works

### A crime the heroine can't remember

Most crime stories have a detective who wasn't there. Here the investigator is the victim, and the evidence is her own missing memory. In the first episode, Arabella's recall of the night comes back as a single flash: a man standing over her in a toilet cubicle. The series returns to that image again and again, a little longer and a little clearer each time, until, near the end, she sees the man in the same bar.

*Story vs. telling* separates the events (the fabula) from the order in which we learn them (the syuzhet). Coel builds the whole season on that gap. The events of the night take minutes; their telling takes twelve episodes, because the telling follows how traumatic memory actually returns, in fragments and out of order. The repeated flash is what Genette would call repetitive frequency: one event told many times, each time meaning something different, because Arabella is a different person each time she sees it.

### Three friends, three versions of the same question

Arabella's assault is the spine, but it isn't the only story. Episode 3 goes back to a holiday Terry took in Italy, where a night with two men she met separately turns out, she realises afterwards, to have been arranged between them. In episode 4, Kwame meets a man for sex, and after it's over the man pins him down and assaults him. In episode 5, Kwame tries to report it, and the police interview, with officers who don't know what to do with a gay man reporting rape, becomes a second violation.

*The cast as a system* argues that a well-built cast answers one question in several ways. Each friend's experience sits somewhere different on the line between consent and assault, and none fits the others' categories. Terry agreed to sex, but not to being deceived. Kwame agreed to the first encounter, not the second. Arabella, in another storyline, agreed to sex with a condom, not without one. By the time we're halfway through, the series has stopped asking "was it rape?" and started asking a harder question: what exactly did each person agree to, and who decided?

### Naming the thing

Around episode 4, Arabella sleeps with Zain, a writer she's working with. Partway through, out of her sight, he takes off the condom. She doesn't know until afterwards. Later she learns that there's a word for this, stealthing, and that under British law it can count as rape. At a public reading where both of them are on stage, she calls him a rapist.

*Want, need and wound* explains how a wound shapes what a character wants. Arabella's response to the first assault is to want a name, a culprit, an outcome. Zain gives her one, and the reading scene plays as a victory. The series then shows what that kind of victory costs: what it does to her online following, to her self-image, and to her friendships. The episode works because Coel refuses to let the act of naming be the end of the story.

### Social media as a stage

Arabella is famous for posting. After the assault, she becomes a voice online about it, and her following grows. The series keeps showing her phone: the posts, the replies, the numbers. It's a portrait of a writer whose public persona and private collapse are happening on the same screen.

*Who gets to be the subject* asks whose stories get told, and by whom. *I May Destroy You* turns the question on its heroine. When Arabella tells her story online, she gains authority and support; she also turns her friends' experiences into material, and starts to judge them by the standards of her feed. The series is sympathetic and unsparing at once. Coel was writing about the same pressure she lived under: a writer who had already used her own life in *Chewing Gum*, deciding how much more of it to make public.

### Several endings

The last episode, "Ego Death", begins with Arabella in the bar where she was drugged, seeing the man who raped her. What follows is not one ending but a series of alternatives. In one, she and Terry trap him, she attacks him, and his body ends up under her bed. In another, Terry's plan plays out differently, and Arabella ends up counselling him as he's arrested. In a third, Arabella approaches him herself and they sleep together; the next morning he says he won't leave until she tells him to, and she tells him to go. Critics read the alternatives as possibilities Arabella is imagining, or drafting, as she decides whether to go out at all. By the end she has let him go, in her head and on the page, and the book she has been struggling with all series is finished.

*Frank Kermode & Peter Brooks* describes how readers want the end to give meaning to everything before it. Coel withholds the single ending that a crime story promises (an arrest, a verdict, revenge) and gives several instead, each of which tests one of the ways stories usually resolve assault. Her answer is that the resolution isn't an event but a change in the storyteller. The finale is the clearest case in the course of an ending that comments on endings.

### A series made to its own rules

*Television structure* explains how episode length, act breaks and season arcs come from business pressures. Coel's episodes vary: some carry the main plot, some go off to Italy or back to school or into one friend's life for a whole half-hour. Episode 6, for example, goes back to Arabella and Terry's schooldays and to a classmate named Theo, whose story of assault complicates the present one. The structure follows the subject, not a formula, and Coel's account of the deal behind it (below) explains why she could do that.

## The shape

No single framework fits, and that's worth noticing. A crime story would put the discovery of the attacker at the climax; *I May Destroy You* puts him in the last episode and then refuses to settle what happens. The closest match is a three-part movement, with episodes that step sideways:

| Movement | Episodes | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| The gap | 1–2 | The night, the flash in the cubicle, the police, the slow realisation |
| Widening the question | 3–6 | Terry in Italy; Zain and stealthing; Kwame's assault and the police; back to school with Theo |
| The cost of the answer | 7–10 | The police case stalls; Arabella's public voice grows as her private life frays; friendships strain |
| Return to the scene | 11–12 | Memory sharpens; the bar; the alternative endings; the finished book |

The structure resembles a spiral more than an arc: it keeps returning to the same night from further out.

## What people said

**Michaela Coel** gave the MacTaggart Lecture at the Edinburgh International Television Festival in August 2018, one of the most prestigious speeches in British television. She spoke about growing up, about working in the industry as a Black woman from a working-class background, about the lack of care she'd met from some producers, and about being sexually assaulted while writing *Chewing Gum*'s second series. She expanded the lecture into a book, *Misfits: A Personal Manifesto* (2021). She later told *Vulture* (2020) that Netflix had offered around a million dollars for the series in 2017, and that she turned it down when they refused to let her keep even a small share of the copyright; the BBC agreed to her terms. She has described writing nearly two hundred drafts. Accepting the 2021 Emmy for writing, the first Black woman to win in that category, she dedicated the story to survivors of sexual assault and told writers:

> Write the tale that scares you, that makes you feel uncertain, that isn't comfortable.
— Michaela Coel, Emmy Awards (2021)

**Critics** in Britain and the US were largely ecstatic. Lucy Mangan in *The Guardian* (2020) gave it full marks for its blend of comedy and pain. Reviewing the finale, Melanie McFarland in *Salon* (2020) read it as a story of disintegration and rebirth. Others were less certain: *The Hollywood Reporter* (2020) said the series was not really a comedy and that its humour can punish viewers for laughing, and several writers admitted they found the multiple endings frustrating at first. The series was left out of the 2021 Golden Globe nominations, which prompted public criticism, including from a writer on another nominated show.

## Watch for

- **The flash** (episode 1, near the end): the first glimpse of the cubicle. Note how short it is, then count how many times it returns.
- **Terry in Italy** (episode 3): the moment she sees the two men together and understands. No speech, just a look.
- **Zain and the condom** (around episode 4): how the camera handles what Arabella can't see.
- **Kwame at the police station** (episode 5): an interview where the officers are polite and still get everything wrong.
- **The reading** (in the episodes after the stealthing): Arabella on stage, naming Zain. Watch who in the room isn't applauding.
- **The phone screen** (throughout): count how often we see Arabella's posts before we see her face.
- **The bar, "Ego Death"** (episode 12, opening): the man across the room.
- **The last alternative** (episode 12): the morning after, when he says he'll stay until she asks him to leave. Who has the power in that bedroom?

## Try it

Write three endings to one scene. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Choose a scene in your work where a character finally faces the person who wronged them.
2. Write it three ways, a page each: one where the character takes revenge, one where they understand the other person, one where they take control in a way neither expected.
3. Read them together. Write one sentence about what each ending says about the story you've been telling.

- **Stage:** Stage all three in sequence, reset each time with a sound or light cue, and let the audience see the actor choose.
- **Screen:** Begin each version from the same shot, so the viewer knows they're seeing alternatives. Decide what the final image after all three will be.
- **Journalism:** You can't invent endings. Instead, list three ways your subject could have responded to what happened to them, and ask them which they considered. The rejected options often reveal more than the one they chose.

**Check:** Does each ending change what the earlier scenes mean? If two of them say the same thing, cut one.
