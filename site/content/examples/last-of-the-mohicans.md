## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*The Last of the Mohicans* (1992) was directed by Michael Mann, who wrote the screenplay with Christopher Crowe, adapting James Fenimore Cooper's 1826 novel and Philip Dunne's screenplay for the 1936 film. In 1757, during the French and Indian War, Hawkeye (Daniel Day-Lewis), a white man raised by the Mohican Chingachgook (Russell Means) alongside his son Uncas (Eric Schweig), rescues Cora and Alice Munro (Madeleine Stowe and Jodhi May), daughters of a British colonel, from an ambush led by the Huron Magua (Wes Studi). He gets them to their father's besieged fort, falls in love with Cora, and pursues Magua after the fort's surrender ends in a massacre. The score is by Trevor Jones and Randy Edelman, and its main theme comes from "The Gael", a tune by the Scottish songwriter Dougie MacLean.

## Why it works

### The hero introduced in motion

The film opens with three men running through a forest. We don't know who they are or what they're chasing until Hawkeye stops, aims and brings down an elk. Then Chingachgook kneels and speaks to the animal, telling it they are sorry to kill it and honouring its speed and courage.

*Introducing a character* says a first action is a thesis. In one minute, this one gives us skill, speed, a bond between three men, and a relationship to the land that is respectful rather than owning. When Hawkeye tells a British officer, a little later, that he's nobody's scout and certainly not in any militia, we already know why: these men answer to their family and the forest, not to an empire. It's Indiana Jones's mystery-then-competence pattern, done without a word of exposition.

### A romance with a war for its barrier

Pamela Regis's account of romance in *Genre as a promise* lists a meeting, an attraction, a barrier, a point of ritual death, and a recognition. Mann builds a full romance inside a war film, and the war supplies every one of those elements. Cora and Hawkeye meet in the aftermath of an ambush, and their first exchanges are prickly: she is a British officer's daughter, courted by Major Duncan Heyward (Steven Waddington), who has asked her to marry him, and Hawkeye is blunt about what the British don't understand. When they reach a friend's cabin and find the family massacred, Hawkeye refuses to stop and bury them, so as not to alert the war party; Cora is appalled, then comes to understand.

The barriers are social (class, Heyward, her father's authority) and then legal: after Hawkeye helps the colonial militiamen slip away to defend their homes, Colonel Munro has him arrested for sedition and condemned to hang. Cora backs his account, and Heyward, out of jealousy, lies about the massacre at the cabin. The romance and the politics are one story: Cora's growing loyalty to Hawkeye is also her move from the British world to the frontier, which is the film's idea of the birth of American identity.

### The waterfall farewell

After the surrender of Fort William Henry and the massacre that follows, about three-fifths of the way in, the survivors flee by canoe and hide in a cave behind a waterfall. Magua's men find them. Three men can't fight off the whole war party, and the only way to survive is to leave the women and come back for them. Hawkeye's farewell is shouted over the roar of the falls:

> No matter how long it takes, no matter how far, I will find you.
> — Hawkeye (Daniel Day-Lewis), *The Last of the Mohicans*

Then he jumps through the waterfall. It's the romance's point of ritual death, and it inverts the usual heroics: the hero's choice is to leave. The scene works because the line is a promise, not a statement of feeling (*Dialogue reveals character*), and because the rest of the film becomes its fulfilment. Everything after is Hawkeye keeping his word.

### Magua: a villain with a case

Wes Studi's Magua is the film's antagonist and, by its own account, a man with a grievance. When Montcalm, the French commander, asks him why he hates the British colonel, Magua explains that Munro's war cost him his children, his freedom (he was enslaved by the Mohawk, Britain's allies) and his wife. He means to wipe out Munro's line in return. During the massacre after the surrender, he kills Munro and cuts out his heart, and tells him first that he will kill his daughters too.

*The cast as a system* asks what each character stands for. Hawkeye and Magua are both men whose lives were made by other people's wars; one has chosen a family and the forest, the other revenge. *Want, need and wound* warns against over-tidy backstory, and here the wound is told in a few lines and never used to excuse him. But the sachem's verdict in the Huron village late in the film makes the point that the film's Hurons do not all share Magua's way: Magua is judged by his own people, and storms off in fury. Studi plays him with total stillness, and critics widely singled out the performance.

### The final sequence: music instead of words

The film's last movement, roughly the final tenth, is close to wordless. After the Huron village, Magua takes Alice up a mountain path. Uncas races ahead, fights Magua and is killed, his body falling from the cliff. Alice stands at the edge; Magua beckons her back; she looks at him, then steps off. Chingachgook and Hawkeye arrive, and while Hawkeye holds the remaining Hurons at gunpoint, Chingachgook kills Magua.

Almost none of this has dialogue. The cue that drives it (called "Promentory" on the soundtrack album) builds on the tune of "The Gael", a repeated Scottish fiddle figure that grows by layering over a relentless pulse. *Music and silence* calls this empathetic music, music that feels with the scene, and it's the purest case: the sequence has been cut to the music, so it plays like a ballet of pursuit. Then, at the end, Chingachgook stands on the mountaintop and prays to the Great Spirit for his son, and calls himself the last of the Mohicans. The title, held back the whole film, lands with full weight.

### How the adaptation changed Cooper

Mann's version, by most accounts, draws as much on Dunne's 1936 screenplay as on Cooper's novel, and it rearranges the book's couples. In Cooper, it's Uncas who is drawn to Cora, Hawkeye (Natty Bumppo, renamed Nathaniel Poe in the film) has no romance at all, Cora and Uncas both die, and Alice survives to marry Heyward. Cooper's Cora also has a mother of partly African descent; the film drops it. Mann gave the central love story to Hawkeye and Cora, killed Alice and Heyward, and kept Uncas's love for Alice as a quiet, almost silent subplot. The novel's slow, much-mocked prose (Mark Twain's "Fenimore Cooper's Literary Offenses", 1895, is the classic attack) became a fast, visual action film. *Same skeleton, three media* explains the move: on screen the main tools are images and action, so Mann kept Cooper's skeleton (the escort, the siege, the massacre, the captivity, the pursuit) and rebuilt the flesh around what a camera can show.

## The shape

*Christopher Booker*'s Overcoming the Monster fits the pursuit, with a romance braided through it. The film's turning points line up with the stages fairly closely.

| Stage | What happens | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| Anticipation and Call | The elk hunt; the militia question; the ambush by Magua | Opening to about a fifth |
| Dream | The journey; the cabin; Cora and Hawkeye begin to fall in love | A fifth to a third |
| Frustration | The siege; Hawkeye's arrest; the surrender | About a third to half |
| Nightmare | The massacre; the waterfall; capture; the Huron village | Half to five-sixths |
| Thrilling Escape and Death of the Monster | The cliff path: Uncas and Alice die, Chingachgook kills Magua | Final tenth |

Unlike Booker's model, the monster's death doesn't bring a restored community. It ends with a father mourning the end of his people.

## What people said

**The makers:** Michael Sragow, writing for the Library of America, traces Mann's interest to seeing the 1936 film as a boy, and reports that Mann considered Cooper's novel a cartoon of frontier life. Day-Lewis prepared for months, learning to hunt, build canoes and load and fire a flintlock on the run. The shoot, mainly in the mountains of North Carolina, was known for Mann's perfectionism; several composers, an editor and the costume designer left the production, and the score ended up credited to both Jones and Edelman.

**The critics:** Roger Ebert, in the *Chicago Sun-Times* (1992), gave it three stars and thought it an improvement on Cooper's book, though he found it more of a matinee fantasy than it admitted: it is

> not as authentic and uncompromised as it claims to be
> — Roger Ebert, *Chicago Sun-Times* (1992)

Desson Howe in *The Washington Post* (1992) mocked it as glam-opera, while his colleague Rita Kempley admired the scenery and the pent-up passion. The film won the Academy Award for Best Sound.

**On its Native characters:** Mann cast Native actors in the major Native roles, among them Means, the Oglala Lakota activist, in his first film role; Means also pressed for better pay and lodging for the film's Native extras. Studi is Cherokee. But writers on the film have noted the limits. Sean Axmaker, writing for TCM (2010), points out that Uncas's story, and his romance with Alice, is left to the margins: this is a story about the white man adopted into Native life. The film divides its Native characters into noble allies and a vengeful war party, a split that critics of Hollywood's Indians have long objected to. And the title repeats Cooper's premise that the Mohicans died out. The historian Joyce Appleby, according to the AFI Catalog, objected to exactly that: Mohican descendants were alive in 1992, and the Stockbridge-Munsee Community of Mohicans remains a federally recognised nation in Wisconsin today.

## Watch for

- **The elk hunt** (first minutes): the running camera, and Chingachgook's words to the animal.
- **The ambush** (about a sixth in): Magua's switch from guide to enemy, and how the Mohicans' arrival turns the fight.
- **The burned cabin** (about a quarter in): Hawkeye's refusal to bury the dead, and Cora's response.
- **The courier run** (during the siege): Hawkeye and the Mohicans passing rifles back and forth to cover a runner, a wordless routine of pure craft.
- **Magua and Montcalm** (about half in): the scene where Magua explains his grievance.
- **The waterfall** (about three-fifths in): the promise, shouted over the water.
- **The Huron village** (about four-fifths in): Hawkeye walking unarmed through the gauntlet, and his last shot from a distance.
- **The cliff path** (final tenth): watch once with the sound off, then once with only the music.

## Try it

Write a climax with no dialogue, built to a piece of music. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Pick a sequence from your draft with a chase, a search or a race against time.
2. Choose one piece of instrumental music you know well. Note its sections: where it builds, where it repeats, where it peaks.
3. Write the sequence beat by beat to match the music, one action per musical phrase. Cut every line of dialogue. If you need a word, allow only one, and make it a name.

- **Stage:** Use live music or a recorded cue, and block the action so each musical build is a new entrance or exit.
- **Screen:** Write "MUSIC CONTINUES" as a slug line and keep each shot to a single sentence.
- **Journalism:** For an audio or video piece, cut a sequence of actuality (footsteps, doors, a crowd) to a single cue, with no narration, then decide honestly whether the music is telling the audience how to feel about real people.

**Check:** If you read only the action lines aloud, in time to the music, does the story still make sense?
