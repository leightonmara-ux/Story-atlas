## At a glance

*Spoilers: this case study discusses the whole film, including the ending.*

*The Square* (2017) was written and directed by Ruben Östlund, three years after *Force Majeure*, and won the Palme d'Or at Cannes that year. Christian (Claes Bang) is the chief curator of a contemporary art museum in Stockholm, a divorced father of two girls, charming, liberal and well practised at saying the right thing. His museum is about to open *The Square*, an installation that is simply a lit square set into the cobbles outside, with a plaque declaring it a sanctuary of trust and caring where everyone has equal rights and obligations. The same week, Christian's phone and wallet are stolen in the street, and his attempt to get them back, together with a marketing campaign for the new work, slowly wrecks his life.

## Why it works

### An episodic satire held together by a theme

*The Square* runs about two and a half hours and doesn't move like a conventional plot. It moves in episodes: a disastrous interview, a street con, a night drive to a housing block, an artist's talk, a one-night stand, a gala dinner, a press conference. Many scenes are self-contained, almost short films.

What holds them together is a single question, asked in a different key each time: what do we owe a stranger who needs help, and what do we do instead? This is the structure *Bertolt Brecht* calls episodic, each scene for itself, joined by an idea rather than by tight cause and effect. Like Brecht, Östlund wants us asking why rather than what next, and his estrangement is quieter: a distant camera, and situations stretched until familiar etiquette looks strange.

### A reflex, a lie, and a boy who won't go away

The film's A story starts with a piece of street theatre. Crossing a busy square on his way to work, Christian hears a woman shouting for help as a man seems to chase her. He and another passer-by step in, he feels briefly heroic, and a few minutes later he discovers that his phone, wallet and cufflinks are gone. It was a con, and his kindness was the bait.

He traces the phone to a large apartment block on the outskirts of the city and, egged on by a young colleague, drafts a threatening anonymous letter accusing the thief and has a copy put through every door in the building. It works: his things are returned. But a boy who lives there, whose parents now believe he's a thief because of the letter, tracks Christian down and demands an apology. Christian brushes him off, then, when the boy comes to his apartment, shoves him, and the boy falls down the stairwell, out of sight. We hear him crying for help offscreen, and Christian does not go down.

This strand is a ladder of escalating moral failure, each rung small. *Robert McKee* asks that every scene turn a value, and here each one turns Christian's decency a notch further down. He never makes a big villainous choice; he just keeps choosing comfort, one step at a time. Late in the film, he searches through bags of garbage in the rain to find the boy's phone number, then records a long video apology on his phone. It is sincere and too late.

### The B story: how to sell a sanctuary

The museum needs publicity for *The Square*, and two young agency men pitch a viral video. Christian, distracted by his own troubles, signs it off without really looking. The video shows a small, blond beggar girl walking into the square and being blown up. It goes viral, there is an outcry, and Christian ends up facing hostile journalists at a press conference where some attack the video as tasteless and others demand that he defend free expression.

*Television structure* explains that the best B stories rhyme with the A story, so the whole adds up to more than two plots. That's exactly what happens. In the A story, Christian takes revenge on a whole building of poor people he has never met. In the B story, his museum turns a poor child into a shock image to sell a message about trust. Both strands are about using strangers as material. *Janet Malcolm & John Hersey* raises the same problem from the journalist's side: real people as raw material for someone else's story.

### The dinner where nobody moves

The film's most famous scene comes about two-thirds of the way in, at the museum's gala dinner for its wealthy patrons. The guests are told that a performance is about to begin and that they will be in the presence of a wild animal; if they stay still, it may leave them alone. Oleg, the performance artist, played by the movement specialist and stunt performer Terry Notary, enters as an ape: shirtless, on crutch-like arm extensions, hooting, sniffing, leaping onto tables.

First Oleg singles out a guest and humiliates him until he leaves. Then he goes further, pulling a woman from her seat by her hair and dragging her across the floor. For a long, agonising stretch nobody moves, because moving would mean admitting this has stopped being art. Finally one man gets up, then others, and they set upon Oleg with a violence that is shocking in its own way.

It's the film's question condensed: the bystander effect performed live, in evening dress. *Comedy structure* describes a game of the scene, a single unusual premise heightened step by step. Here the game is simple (a man pretends to be an animal, and the rich must pretend it's fine) and Östlund heightens it past the point where comedy can survive. Östlund has said he wanted the scene premiered at Cannes so that an audience in black tie would watch an audience in black tie being controlled.

### The camera as an uneasy witness

Like *Force Majeure*, the film is shot mostly in long, composed takes with little cutting to close-ups, and sound often carries what the frame withholds. The clearest case is the stairwell: the boy's cries come from below and outside the frame, and we stay with Christian as he listens and decides. *Sound inside and outside the story* explains how offscreen sound widens the world; here it also forces a moral position on us, since we, like Christian, hear and do nothing.

Sound is also a joke engine. An artist's public talk (Dominic West as a famous artist, Julian) is repeatedly interrupted by a man in the audience who has Tourette's syndrome and shouts obscenities. Everyone strains to be tolerant, excruciatingly. Later, Christian's quarrel with the journalist Anne (Elisabeth Moss), after their one-night stand, plays against the loud clatter of a gallery installation of stacked chairs that nobody mentions.

### Who is a person, and who is scenery

Beggars sit outside shops and in the background of Stockholm's streets throughout. Christian ignores most of them, and once, when a woman asks him for a sandwich, buys it and hands it over curtly. These figures barely speak, and that's deliberate: the film is about who gets to be a full character and who stays scenery.

*Who gets to be the subject* asks who gets to be seen and from whose point of view. Östlund turns that into the satire. The museum preaches equality; the video makes a poor child an image; the letter makes a whole building suspects. The boy, insisting on being treated as a person owed an apology, is the one character who refuses to stay in the background.

## The shape

*The Square* has an A story and a B story that converge, more like an extended television episode than a three-act feature, with episodes in between that belong to neither.

| Strand | What happens | Roughly where |
|---|---|---|
| Set-up | The interview with Anne; the street con; *The Square* introduced | Opening to about a sixth |
| A story rises | The phone is traced; the letter; the things are returned; the boy appears | About a sixth to two-fifths |
| B story rises | The agency pitch; the video is approved without being watched | About a third to a half |
| Convergence | The boy at Christian's door and the stairwell; the gala dinner with Oleg | About three-fifths to three-quarters |
| Fall | The video goes viral; the press conference; Christian's resignation | About the last fifth |
| Coda | Christian takes his daughters to the apartment block to apologise, but the family has moved; a cheerleading competition | Final minutes |

The coda refuses catharsis. Christian finally does the right thing, in person, and there's nobody to receive it. Around it, the film gives us his daughters' cheerleading team: a group moving in practised, trusting unison, perhaps the only working community the film can find.

## What people said

**Ruben Östlund** has said in interviews that the film grew from a real installation called *The Square*, which he and the producer Kalle Boman created in Sweden in 2015, and from his long interest in the bystander effect: how people in a crowd fail to help, each assuming someone else will. He said the dinner performance drew on the Russian artist Oleg Kulik, who performed as a dog and attacked visitors at a Stockholm exhibition; he also cited the punk performer GG Allin as an influence. **Terry Notary** has said he based Oleg partly on a Russian acrobat he knew from his days with Cirque du Soleil. The film was nominated for the Academy Award for best foreign-language film.

**The critics** agreed it was ambitious and often hilarious, and argued about whether it was too much. Justin Chang, on NPR (2017), called it a sharp satire and found its ideas coming together around Christian's arc. Peter Bradshaw, in *The Guardian* (2017), described a sprawling, daringly surreal satire that turns the museum into a strange, Ballardian city-state, and judged it essential despite its flaws.

The dissent is about length and lecture. Giovanni Marchini Camia, reviewing it for *Sight & Sound* on its UK release (2018), found it a laboured lecture on inequality, much less than the sum of its best sections, a succession of vignettes only loosely tied to the main story.

## Watch for

- **The interview** (first few minutes): Christian trying to explain a piece of baffling art-speech, and how charm carries him through.
- **The con** (about a twentieth in): who the film makes us watch, and when you notice the theft.
- **The letter** (about a fifth in): the drive to the apartment block, and who actually goes into the building.
- **The video pitch** (about a third in): how little attention Christian pays.
- **The artist's talk** (around halfway): the audience's strained tolerance, and Christian's.
- **Oleg's dinner** (about two-thirds in): when you stop laughing, and who is the first to stand.
- **The stairwell and the garbage** (late): the boy's cries offscreen; then Christian on his knees in the rain.

## Try it

Write a scene in which a crowd fails to act, and each person's reason is perfectly reasonable. Twenty to thirty minutes.

1. Put five or more people in a polite setting: a dinner, a lecture, a waiting room, an office meeting.
2. Introduce one disruption that escalates in three steps. Each step should be a little worse than the last.
3. Give each bystander a private reason not to move (manners, doubt, fear, the hope someone else will). Show those reasons through behaviour, not explanation. End the scene the moment one person stands up.

- **Stage:** Put the audience in the role of the guests: seat the disruption downstage, so the real audience's own stillness becomes part of the scene.
- **Screen:** Write it as one long, wide take, and note where a viewer will expect a cut that never comes.
- **Journalism:** Reconstruct a real public incident where bystanders didn't intervene. Interview witnesses about what they were thinking in the moment, and structure the piece around those reasons rather than around blame.

**Check:** At each step, could a reasonable reader imagine staying still too? If not, you've written villains, not a crowd.
