## Who and when

Joseph Campbell was an American scholar of comparative mythology who taught for decades at Sarah Lawrence College. In 1949 he published *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*, which argued that the hero myths of the world, from the Buddha to Moses to Native American and Polynesian tales, share one underlying pattern. He called it the **monomyth**, a word he borrowed from James Joyce's *Finnegans Wake*.

Campbell was not writing a manual for storytellers. He drew on Freud and above all Jung, and saw the hero's adventure as a picture of the psychological journey every person makes. But George Lucas has said the book helped him shape *Star Wars*, and when the two became friends, Campbell's ideas reached a huge popular audience, especially through *The Power of Myth*, his television conversations with Bill Moyers, broadcast in 1988, the year after his death.

Christopher Vogler turned the monomyth into a screenwriting tool. In 1985, working as a story analyst at Disney, he wrote a seven-page memo, "A Practical Guide to *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*". It was passed around Hollywood, and he expanded it into a book, *The Writer's Journey* (1992), which he has revised several times since.

So this lesson is about two different things: a scholar's theory of myth, and a working writer's adaptation of it.

## The big ideas

### Campbell: separation, initiation, return

Campbell's nuclear unit is a three-part movement he adapted from the anthropology of rites of passage: **separation** from the everyday world, **initiation** through trials in a strange one, and **return** home transformed. His own summary has the hero venture forth from the world of common day into "a region of supernatural wonder", win a decisive victory, and come back with the power to bestow boons on others.

Inside those three phases he lists seventeen stages, from the **call to adventure** and **refusal of the call**, through **supernatural aid**, the **crossing of the first threshold** and the **belly of the whale**, to the **road of trials**, the **meeting with the goddess**, **atonement with the father**, **apotheosis** and the **ultimate boon**, and finally the **refusal of the return**, the **magic flight**, **rescue from without**, the **crossing of the return threshold**, becoming **master of the two worlds**, and **freedom to live**.

He was clear that no single myth contains every stage. Some stress one part, some another; some stages are doubled or dropped.

### What the journey means

For Campbell the outward adventure stands for an inward one. The dragon is the hero's fear, the descent into the underworld a confrontation with what's hidden in the self, the boon a new understanding brought back to the community. That's why he thought the pattern recurred: it reflects how human beings grow up and change.

Whether that's true is debated, but it's the key to using the pattern well. The physical journey should mean something for the character.

### Vogler's twelve stages

Vogler compressed and renamed Campbell's stages for film, and laid them over the three acts that Hollywood was already using:

{{diagram:campbell-vogler}}

**Act One**

1. **Ordinary World:** the hero's normal life, before the story begins.
2. **Call to Adventure:** a problem, challenge or opportunity arrives.
3. **Refusal of the Call:** fear or doubt makes the hero hesitate.
4. **Meeting with the Mentor:** someone gives advice, training or a gift.
5. **Crossing the First Threshold:** the hero commits and enters the special world.

**Act Two**

6. **Tests, Allies, Enemies:** the hero learns the rules of the new world.
7. **Approach to the Inmost Cave:** preparations for the central danger.
8. **Ordeal:** a crisis in the middle of the story, a brush with death.
9. **Reward (Seizing the Sword):** the hero survives and takes the prize.

**Act Three**

10. **The Road Back:** consequences follow; the hero recommits to finishing.
11. **Resurrection:** a final, most dangerous test, in which the hero is purified and transformed.
12. **Return with the Elixir:** home again, carrying something that changes the ordinary world.

Notice where the Ordeal falls: in the middle, like *Freytag's* climax. The hero's journey has two peaks, a central ordeal and a final resurrection.

### Archetypes as masks

Vogler also adapted Campbell's (and Jung's) archetypes into eight recurring character functions: **Hero**, **Mentor**, **Threshold Guardian**, **Herald**, **Shapeshifter**, **Shadow**, **Ally** and **Trickster**.

His most useful point is that these are **functions, not fixed people**. A character can wear different masks at different moments: an ally becomes a shapeshifter by betraying the hero, a threshold guardian later becomes a mentor. It's an idea close to *Propp's* spheres of action.

### A form, not a formula

Vogler repeatedly warns against using the stages as a checklist. The order can be rearranged, stages can be dropped or repeated, and the journey can be emotional rather than physical: a hero's special world might be a new job, a marriage or grief. He presents the pattern as a set of questions to ask of a story, not a template to fill in.

## Use it

- **Screen:** Vogler's natural home. *Star Wars*, *The Matrix* and *The Lion King* are the standard examples, and the stages are useful for spotting what's missing from a quest story: Where is the refusal? Who is the mentor? What's the elixir?
- **Stage:** Less often used, since most plays happen in one or two locations. But the inner version applies: a character called out of their ordinary world, tested, and returning changed. Many coming-of-age plays fit.
- **Journalism:** Profiles and personal essays often have the shape of a departure and return: someone leaves, is tested, and comes back with something to tell. Be careful not to force real lives into it, and don't invent a mentor or a resurrection that the reporting doesn't support.

## The pushback

- **Is it really universal?** Folklorists and anthropologists have argued that Campbell chose the myths that fit and smoothed over the ones that didn't, and that the differences between cultures matter as much as the similarities.
- **Whose journey?** The pattern centers a lone, usually male, hero. Maureen Murdock wrote *The Heroine's Journey* (1990) in response, and other writers have built models around community rather than individual triumph.
- **Formula fatigue.** As the pattern spread through Hollywood, critics blamed it for making blockbusters predictable. Vogler himself says it was never meant to be applied mechanically.
- **The *Star Wars* story is complicated.** Lucas has credited Campbell, but how much the book shaped his early drafts, as opposed to later revisions and later retellings, is debated.
- **Campbell described myths; he didn't write screenplays.** Using a scholar's psychological reading of religion as a plotting tool is a leap that Vogler, not Campbell, made.

## Read next

- Joseph Campbell, *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949). The prologue and the chapter on departure are the most useful for writers.
- Christopher Vogler, *The Writer's Journey: Mythic Structure for Writers* (any recent edition).
- Maureen Murdock, *The Heroine's Journey* (1990).
- Joseph Campbell with Bill Moyers, *The Power of Myth* (1988), the book of the television series.

## Try it

Lay your work-in-progress, or a quest film you know well such as *Star Wars*, against Vogler's twelve stages.

1. For each stage, write the scene that fills it, or "missing". Don't force a fit.
2. For each gap, ask whether it was a choice. A missing Refusal can mean a hero with no doubt to overcome. A missing Elixir can mean an ending that changes nothing back home.
3. Write one line saying what the outer journey means inwardly: what does the dragon stand for, and what does the boon change?
4. Find one character who wears two masks, such as an ally who turns shapeshifter.

- **Stage:** use the inner version. Where does your character cross the threshold into a strange world, even if they never leave the room?
- **Screen:** check that the Ordeal falls near the middle and the Resurrection near the end: two peaks, not one.
- **Journalism:** map a profile only against what your reporting supports. Leave a stage blank rather than invent a mentor or a resurrection.

**Check:** Can you say what the hero brings home, and who in the ordinary world it changes?

## Check yourself

Q: What are the three phases of Campbell's monomyth?
A: Separation (departure), initiation and return.

Q: Where did Vogler's version of the hero's journey begin?
A: As a seven-page memo he wrote in 1985 as a story analyst at Disney, later expanded into *The Writer's Journey* (1992).

Q: Where does the Ordeal fall in Vogler's twelve stages, and what follows it?
A: In the middle of the story. It's followed by the Reward, then the Road Back, the Resurrection and the Return with the Elixir.

Q: What does Vogler mean when he calls archetypes masks?
A: They're functions a character performs at a given moment, not fixed identities. One character can be an ally and later a shapeshifter.

Q: Give one major criticism of the monomyth.
A: That it isn't truly universal: Campbell selected the myths that fit, and the pattern centers a lone male hero.
