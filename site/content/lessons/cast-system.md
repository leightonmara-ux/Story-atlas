## Where it comes from

Writers often build characters one at a time. Audiences never meet them that way: they meet them against each other, and a character's meaning comes largely from the contrast.

The idea is very old. Greek tragedy was performed by at most three speaking actors, who shared all the roles between them; *Aristotle*'s *Poetics* credits Aeschylus with adding the second actor and Sophocles the third. The **protagonist** was literally the first actor, and the cast was a system of parts long before it was a list of people.

The word **foil** comes from the jeweller's trade: a thin leaf of metal set behind a gem to make it shine. Shakespeare puns on it in *Hamlet*, when Hamlet tells Laertes before their duel, "I'll be your foil, Laertes."

Modern teachers have turned the instinct into method. *Lajos Egri* (1946) told playwrights to **orchestrate** their characters for contrast. Robert McKee's *Story* (1997) states a **principle of antagonism**. Michael Hauge's *Writing Screenplays That Sell* (1988) names four primary roles: hero, nemesis, reflection and romance. And *John Truby*'s *The Anatomy of Story* (2007) gives the fullest version, the **character web**.

## The big ideas

### The foil

A **foil** is a character whose likeness to another makes the differences show. The trick is the likeness. Two characters in the same situation, making different choices, reveal each other.

*Hamlet* is built on this. Three young men lose their fathers: Hamlet, Laertes and Fortinbras. Laertes, hearing his father is dead, storms back to Denmark and is ready to cut the king's throat in a church; Fortinbras marches an army off to fight for a worthless patch of ground. Hamlet, with the strongest cause of all, broods and delays. Without the other two, his hesitation would be a trait. With them, it's the subject of the play.

### Protagonist and antagonist

The protagonist drives the story; the antagonist opposes them. The useful refinement is that they should be locked to the **same goal**, or to goals that rule each other out. Truby insists the best opponent wants the same thing as the hero, and Egri's **unity of opposites** is the bond that stops either side walking away.

### A hero is only as strong as the opposition

McKee's principle of antagonism says a protagonist, and the story, can only be as compelling as the forces against them. A weak opponent makes an easy victory, and an easy victory means nothing.

*The Dark Knight* (2008) is the popular example: the Joker's challenge isn't physical but moral, and each of his schemes forces Batman to decide which of his own rules he'll break.

### The web and four-corner opposition

Truby's **character web** (see *John Truby*) asks you to design the cast as a set of contrasts, each character a different answer to the hero's moral problem. His **four-corner opposition** puts a hero, a main opponent and two secondary opponents in conflict with each other and with everyone else, each standing for a different set of values. The test is simple: if two characters stand for the same thing, one of them may not be needed.

### The supporting roles

Some recurring functions do a lot of the work. Vogler's archetypes (see *Joseph Campbell & Christopher Vogler*) catalogue them as functions, not fixed people.

- **The mentor** guides the hero. The word comes from Mentor in Homer's *Odyssey*, whose shape the goddess Athena takes to advise Odysseus's son Telemachus. Its modern sense owes much to Fénelon's popular novel *The Adventures of Telemachus* (1699). A mentor usually has to leave or fail, so the hero can stand alone.
- **The confidant** is the person the protagonist can tell the truth to, which lets the audience hear it too. French classical tragedy made it a fixed role: in Racine's *Phèdre* (1677), Phèdre's nurse Oenone draws out her secret love and then pushes her toward the false accusation that destroys everyone. Horatio is Hamlet's confidant.
- **The reflection** is Hauge's term for the character who shares the hero's outer goal or situation and so helps reveal their inner conflict: the partner, the sidekick, the friend in the same trouble.

### The ensemble

In an **ensemble**, no one character carries the story; the meaning lies in the pattern. *Anton Chekhov* is the model on stage: in *Three Sisters* each sister wants a different version of escape, and none gets it.

Television has made the ensemble its home. *The Wire* (2002–2008) sets the Baltimore police and the drug trade side by side as mirror-image institutions, each with its bosses, its loyal soldiers and its idealists who get crushed, and adds a new institution most seasons: the docks, city politics, the schools, the newspaper. In the first season, D'Angelo Barksdale teaches two young dealers chess in a housing-project courtyard, explaining each piece as a role in the drug trade. The scene is the show describing its own cast system.

*Succession* (2018–2023) runs on variations of one want. Logan Roy's children all want his company and his approval, and each pursues it with a different weapon: Kendall's ambition, Shiv's politics, Roman's jokes. 

### The cast list as a tool

On stage, the cast list is a design decision and a budget. Every actor costs money, so many new plays have small casts, and the limit can sharpen a play: each character must earn their place.

**Doubling**, one actor playing two roles, can also make meaning. In J. M. Barrie's *Peter Pan* (1904), the same actor traditionally plays the stern Mr Darling and Captain Hook, so the nursery's father returns as the Neverland's villain. Caryl Churchill's *Cloud Nine* (1979) deliberately casts across gender and race to make its point about how roles are assigned.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Write the cast list before the first scene. For each name, write one line on what they stand for and whom they contrast with. Then look for doubling that could mean something, not just save money.
- **Screen:** Test your antagonist against your hero's weakness. Are they the person best placed to attack it? If your lead has a best friend, decide whether they're a confidant (who hears the truth) or a reflection (who shares the goal), and cut what doesn't do either job.
- **Journalism:** Features have casts too. J. Anthony Lukas's *Common Ground* (1985) tells the story of Boston's busing crisis through three families, one Black, one Irish-American and one white middle-class, chosen to show the city from three sides. That's casting, and it's powerful. The danger is the reverse: reducing real people to types (the victim, the villain, the wise elder) because the structure needs one. Report people as individuals, and let them contradict the role.

## The pushback

- **Function over person.** Thinking of characters as foils and functions can produce people who exist only to make the hero look good. Critics have long pointed this out about the "best friend" and "mentor" roles often given to women and characters of color.
- **Opposition isn't everything.** Not every cast needs a villain. *Kishōtenketsu* and much of Chekhov work by juxtaposition rather than conflict.
- **Too neat.** A cast designed as a perfect grid of values can feel like an argument in costume.
- **In journalism, casting is choosing.** Picking three families to "represent" a city makes a claim about the city. Readers deserve to know how and why they were chosen.

## Try it

Take the cast of your work-in-progress, or of a film or play you know well.

1. Write every named character on a card, with one line for what they want and one for the value they stand for.
2. Lay the cards out. Put the hero in the middle and arrange the others by contrast. Any two cards that say the same thing? Merge or cut one.
3. Find the hero's foil, someone in a similar situation who chooses differently. If there isn't one, write a scene that creates one.
4. Rate the antagonist: can they attack the hero's specific weakness? If not, strengthen them.

- **Stage:** fit the cast to six actors, doubling where it adds meaning.
- **Screen:** check each supporting role is doing a job (mentor, confidant, reflection, opponent) and that no two do the same one.
- **Journalism:** list the people in your feature and the "role" each plays. Then find one fact about each that breaks the role.

**Check:** Can you say what each character shows about the hero that no one else could?

## Check yourself

Q: What makes a foil work?
A: Likeness. A character in a similar situation who makes different choices shows up what's distinctive in the other.

Q: How do Laertes and Fortinbras work as foils to Hamlet?
A: All three have lost fathers. Laertes and Fortinbras act swiftly and violently; Hamlet delays, so his hesitation becomes the subject of the play.

Q: What is McKee's principle of antagonism?
A: A protagonist and story can only be as compelling as the forces of antagonism make them. A weak opponent means an easy, meaningless victory.

Q: What is the difference between a confidant and a reflection character?
A: The confidant is someone the protagonist tells the truth to, so the audience hears it. The reflection (Hauge's term) shares the hero's goal or situation, which helps reveal their inner conflict.

Q: What's the danger of "casting" a feature story?
A: Reducing real people to types, the victim or the villain, because the structure needs them, instead of reporting them as individuals.
