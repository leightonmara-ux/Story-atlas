## Who and when

Joachim Trier (born 1974) is a Norwegian director, born in Copenhagen and raised in Oslo. His grandfather Erik Løchen was a film director, and Trier trained at the National Film and Television School in England. Every one of his features was written with Eskil Vogt (also born 1974), a friend since their teens, who studied at La Fémis in Paris and directs films of his own, including *Blind* (2014) and *The Innocents* (2021).

The films: *Reprise* (2006), about two young Oslo writers; *Oslo, August 31st* (2011), freely adapted from Pierre Drieu La Rochelle's novel *Le Feu follet* (1931); the English-language *Louder Than Bombs* (2015); *Thelma* (2017), a supernatural coming-of-age story; *The Worst Person in the World* (2021); and *Sentimental Value* (2025), about two sisters and their filmmaker father. *Reprise*, *Oslo, August 31st* and *The Worst Person in the World* are often called the Oslo trilogy. *Worst Person* won Renate Reinsve Best Actress at Cannes and earned the pair an Oscar nomination for original screenplay. *Sentimental Value* won the Grand Prix at Cannes in 2025 and the Oscar for best international feature in 2026, Norway's first.

## The big ideas

### Characters first, plot late

Trier and Vogt describe a slow, talk-heavy partnership. In *Screen Daily*'s 2022 feature on the pair, they said they spend more time talking about life than about scenes, and that stories and characters come out of those conversations. In the Academy's *A.frame* (2022) they said they work mostly in the same room. On John August's *Scriptnotes* podcast (2026), Trier said they spend about a year in a room together before there's a draft, and that they deliberately don't build a plot and then illustrate it: the material and the characters come first.

That method has a cost and a payoff. The cost is time and uncertainty. The payoff is that the plot, when it arrives, grows out of people we already understand. *Sentimental Value* began, Trier told August, as the daughter's story. Partway through, the writers realized it also needed the father's point of view, and it became what he called a more polyphonic film, with several centres of feeling. *Point of view and voice* explains polyphony; here it's a working decision about whose film this is.

### The art form of memory

Trier's films keep asking how a life looks from later. A 2016 conversation with him on the Criterion Collection's website is titled after his own description of cinema as "the art form of memory", and you can see the idea in his openings.

- ***Oslo, August 31st*** begins before its story does: old footage and home movies of Oslo, over anonymous voices each recalling the city, each beginning "I remember". Trier has said he recorded these by asking Oslo people to start with those words. Then we meet Anders (Anders Danielsen Lie), a recovering heroin addict on a day's leave from rehab for a job interview, and the film follows him through one day and night in the city he grew up in. The opening tells us how to watch: this city is made of memories, and Anders is walking through his.
- ***Thelma*** opens with a child and her father hunting on a frozen lake. He raises his rifle and points it at the back of her head, then lowers it. The meaning arrives much later, when the film reveals what Thelma did as a small child. The past is placed first and explained last.
- ***Sentimental Value*** opens with a school essay that the young Nora wrote from the point of view of the family house, illustrated with moments from a century of its life. In a 2025 RogerEbert.com interview, Trier said he saw the house as a constant, a structure that lasts longer than any human life.

The lesson for writers is that memory isn't just flashback. It's a frame: a voice, an image or a place that tells the audience how far away from events we're standing. *Story vs. telling* gives you the terms (order, duration, frequency) for planning that distance.

### Voice-over that adds, not explains

Screenwriting manuals warn against voice-over. Trier uses it a lot, on one condition. In a 2016 *Seventh Row* interview about *Louder Than Bombs*, he said narration is a cop-out when it simply tells us what the film isn't showing; it earns its place when it contrasts with the image and gives us something the pictures can't.

*Reprise* shows how. Two friends post their first manuscripts into a mailbox. A narrator then races through their imagined futures: publication, fame, crisis. Then he admits this is only one way it could go, and the film returns to the mailbox to start again. The voice-over isn't explaining the story; it's playing with it, and with the young men's dreams of themselves. *The Worst Person in the World* uses a calm, slightly ironic woman's voice that knows Julie better than she knows herself (the *Worst Person* case study in Examples looks at it closely). In both films the narrator has an attitude, and that gap between voice and image is where the comedy and the sadness live.

### Chapters and elastic time

*The Worst Person in the World* is told in a prologue, twelve titled chapters and an epilogue, covering about four years. The chapters vary hugely in length, and time jumps between them without apology. Chapter cards do two jobs: they make the gaps honest, and they give the film a wry, novelistic voice before a word is spoken.

Inside that structure sits the frozen-Oslo sequence. Julie flicks a light switch in her kitchen and the world stops; she runs through a city of people caught mid-stride to spend a day with a man who isn't her partner. Vogt told *Seventh Row* (2022) that this image was one of their first ideas, before there was a story. It's what *Story vs. telling* calls a pause, made literal: the clock stops because Julie wants consequences to wait.

### The city as a character

Oslo isn't backdrop in these films; it's what the characters measure themselves against. In *Oslo, August 31st*, each place Anders visits (a friend's flat, a café, a party, a swimming pool at dawn) is a version of the life he might have had. In one café scene he simply listens to strangers' conversations around him, a whole city of plans going on without him. Trier told *Interview* magazine that Drieu La Rochelle's novel was a starting point more than something they adapted closely; Louis Malle had already filmed it in France in 1963. Moving it to Oslo made it Trier's own.

### Introduce people on their worst day

On *Scriptnotes*, August asked whether to introduce a character on an ordinary day or an extreme one. Trier chose extreme. *Sentimental Value* introduces Nora (Reinsve), a stage actress, on an opening night when stage fright grips her so hard she tries to flee, and her colleagues have to get her on stage almost by force. It's funny and alarming, and it tells us at once what the film will be about: a woman whose art is bound up with her fear. *Introducing a character* has the general method; Trier's version is to start where the character is least in control.

### Bergman nearby

Trier is often compared to *Ingmar Bergman*, and he has spoken of Bergman's way of mixing naturalism with dreams. Critics made the comparison above all for *Sentimental Value*: a family, a house, an artist father and two sisters. The difference is tone. Trier's families argue with more jokes and more hope.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Treat the set as a memory the play returns to. A house, a café or a street corner can carry decades if the characters keep measuring themselves against it. A narrator who comments rather than explains suits the stage too (see *Mood on stage*).
- **Screen:** Draft voice-over last, and only where it contradicts, undercuts or widens the picture. Try chapter cards on a draft that sprawls: they force you to choose your ellipses. Introduce your lead at the moment they're least in control.
- **Journalism:** A long feature can borrow the "I remember" device: let several people's memories of a place open the piece before you narrow to one person. Chapter headings in a long read work like Trier's cards, marking jumps in time honestly.

## The pushback

- **Comfort.** Critics have found the films comfortable in both senses. *The New Republic* called *Sentimental Value* an upper-middle-brow crowd-pleaser, and Justin Chang (NPR, 2025) found moments that felt coy and complacent, as if the film let its characters off too easily. The central characters are mostly educated, creative Oslo people.
- **Sentiment.** Peter Bradshaw (*The Guardian*, 2025) found *Sentimental Value* baggy and sentimental in ways it may not intend.
- **Narration as a crutch.** Voice-over that sounds like a clever novelist can smooth over what a scene should have dramatized. Trier's own rule sets the test.
- **Slow method, rare luxury.** A year of talk before a draft is easier for an established, publicly funded European team than for a writer working to a studio deadline.

## Try it

Take a project you're drafting and give it a frame of memory.

1. Choose one place that matters in the story: a house, a street, a café. Write a half-page opening in which several voices (not your protagonist) remember it, each beginning "I remember".
2. Write one line of voice-over for an existing scene that disagrees with what we see. If the picture shows a happy dinner, the narrator might mention who isn't there.
3. Divide the draft into chapters and give each a title. Note what happens in the gaps between them.

- **Stage:** let a narrator or chorus speak the "I remember" lines while the set stays empty.
- **Screen:** script the opening as archive or home-movie images under the voices, before the first scene.
- **Journalism:** open a feature with three short memories of a place from three sources, then narrow to your subject.

**Check:** Does your voice-over tell us something the images can't, or does it just repeat them?

## Check yourself

Q: How do Trier and Vogt begin a script?
A: By talking, often about life rather than scenes, for a long time, letting characters and material come first and arriving at plot late.

Q: What is the "I remember" opening of *Oslo, August 31st*, and what does it do?
A: Old footage of Oslo under anonymous voices each recalling the city; it frames the film as memory and tells us Anders is walking through a city made of his past.

Q: When does Trier think voice-over earns its place?
A: When it contrasts with the image and adds something the pictures can't show, not when it explains what the film isn't showing.

Q: What do the chapter cards in *The Worst Person in the World* do?
A: They make the jumps in time honest and give the film a wry, novelistic voice; the frozen-Oslo sequence inside them turns a narrative pause into an image.

Q: Why does *Sentimental Value* introduce Nora during an attack of stage fright?
A: Trier prefers introducing a character on an extreme day: the scene shows at once that her art and her fear are bound together.
