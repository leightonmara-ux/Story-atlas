## Who and when

Dwight V. Swain taught for more than twenty years in the Professional Writing Program at the University of Oklahoma. Before and alongside that he wrote pulp fiction, science fiction and westerns for the magazines, and scripts for industrial and educational films. He knew the market from the inside: writers who didn't hold a reader's attention didn't get paid.

*Techniques of the Selling Writer* came out in 1965, with a revised edition from the University of Oklahoma Press in 1981. The title sounds like a get-rich guide, but the book is a patient, practical manual on how fiction moves a reader from one moment to the next. He later applied the same thinking to film in *Film Scriptwriting: A Practical Manual* (1976).

Swain wrote mainly for prose writers, so why is he in the screen track? Because his two big tools, the **scene and sequel** and the **motivation-reaction unit**, work at the smallest scale of any theory in this course, and screenwriters, novelists and genre writers have borrowed them ever since.

## The big ideas

### Story is feeling

Swain starts from the reader. People don't read fiction for information; they read to *feel* something. Everything else in the book is a way of producing that feeling reliably, line by line, so that the reader never has a reason to put the story down.

### Two kinds of unit: scene and sequel

Swain divides a story into two alternating kinds of unit. A **scene** is where things happen. A **sequel** is where a character responds to what happened and decides what to do next.

**The scene** has three parts:

1. **Goal:** the point-of-view character wants something specific, here and now. Not "happiness", but "get the sheriff to release my brother tonight".
2. **Conflict:** someone or something opposes the goal. The scene is the struggle between the two.
3. **Disaster:** the scene ends badly for the character. They fail, or they succeed in a way that makes things worse, or a new problem appears.

The disaster is the hook. If the character simply got what they wanted, the reader would have no reason to turn the page. A scene that ends in disaster leaves a question hanging.

**The sequel** also has three parts:

1. **Reaction:** the character's emotional response to the disaster. Shock, grief, anger, before they can think straight.
2. **Dilemma:** as the feeling settles, they face their options, and none of them is good.
3. **Decision:** they choose one. That choice becomes the goal of the next scene.

And so the chain runs on: scene, sequel, scene, sequel. Each disaster forces a reaction, each decision launches a new goal. Cause and effect is built into the pattern itself.

### Why the sequel matters

Many writers handle scenes well and skip the sequels, cutting straight from one action beat to the next. Swain's point is that the sequel does three jobs a scene can't:

- It lets the reader **feel** the disaster along with the character.
- It **motivates** the next scene, so the new goal feels earned rather than arbitrary.
- It controls **pace**. A long sequel slows the story to let it breathe; a sequel cut to a single line ("She ran.") keeps it racing.

A sequel can be a page of agonized thought or a glance. What matters is that the character visibly processes what happened before acting again.

### Motivation-reaction units

Swain then zooms in further, to the level of sentences. The smallest building block is the **motivation-reaction unit**: something happens outside the character (the **motivating stimulus**), and the character responds (the **reaction**).

The rule is order. The stimulus comes first, in its own sentence or paragraph, written objectively, so the reader sees it as the character does. Then the reaction, written from inside the character.

Within the reaction, Swain gives a natural sequence: **feeling** first, then reflexive **action**, then deliberate action and **speech**. A shot rings out. She flinches. Then she dives for the door. Only then does she shout. Reverse the order ("She dove for the door, startled, when a shot rang out") and the prose feels slightly wrong, because it doesn't match how people experience things.

This sounds fussy, but it's the source of the "page-turning" quality in a great deal of genre fiction: the reader is always just ahead of the character's reaction, never confused about what caused what.

## Use it

- **Screen:** The scene-and-sequel rhythm is visible in most thrillers and action films: a set piece that goes wrong, then a quieter scene where the heroes regroup, argue and decide. If your second act is all set pieces and feels exhausting, you may be missing sequels. If it sags, the sequels may be too long. And the motivation-reaction order is what film editors call cutting on the reaction: show the cause, then the face.
- **Stage:** The disaster ending is a good test for any scene, and the dilemma is where actors find their most playable moments. A whole act of Chekhov can feel like a long sequel; knowing that helps you choose it on purpose.
- **Journalism:** Narrative reporters can't invent disasters, but they can structure around real ones, and they can reconstruct the sequel: what the person felt, what options they saw, what they decided. Those are interview questions.

## The pushback

- **It was built for pulp.** Swain's examples come from commercial genre fiction, and the "disaster" ending can feel mechanical in quieter literary work, where scenes may end in ambiguity or a small shift of understanding.
- **Conflict everywhere, again.** Like McKee, Swain assumes every scene runs on opposition. Writers drawn to *Kishōtenketsu* or Ursula K. Le Guin's critique of conflict will find this too narrow.
- **Read it strictly and it becomes a metronome.** Scene, sequel, scene, sequel can produce a monotonous rhythm. Swain himself treats the pattern as flexible: sequels can be compressed to almost nothing, or several scenes can run before a pause.
- **Dated in places.** The book's examples and some of its assumptions about readers and characters show their age.

## Read next

- Dwight V. Swain, *Techniques of the Selling Writer* (1965; rev. ed. 1981). The scene and sequel chapters are the heart of it.
- Jack M. Bickham, *Scene & Structure* (1993). Bickham also taught in Oklahoma's writing program, and his book is the most popular restatement of scene and sequel.
- Compare *Robert McKee*: his scene must turn a value; Swain's scene must end in disaster. They're close cousins.

## Try it

Take one scene from your draft.

1. Label its goal, conflict and disaster. If it ends in plain success, rewrite the last beat so the success makes things worse or opens a new problem.
2. Write the sequel that follows: reaction, dilemma, decision. Make the decision the goal of the next scene. Write it twice, once as a single line and once as a paragraph, and choose the length that gives the pace you want.
3. Find a moment of sudden action and check the motivation-reaction order: the stimulus first, in its own sentence, then feeling, reflex, deliberate action and speech. Fix any reversals.

- **Stage:** find the dilemma in your scene and give the actor a moment to weigh the options on stage.
- **Screen:** in an action sequence, find where the characters regroup and decide. Then check your cuts: cause first, then the face.
- **Journalism:** turn the sequel into interview questions: what did you feel, what options did you see, what did you decide? Write the sequel only from the answers.

**Check:** Does each decision launch the next goal?

## Check yourself

Q: What are the three parts of a Swain scene?
A: Goal, conflict and disaster. The point-of-view character wants something specific, meets opposition, and the scene ends badly for them.

Q: What are the three parts of a sequel, and how does it connect to the next scene?
A: Reaction, dilemma and decision. The decision becomes the goal of the next scene.

Q: Why should a scene end in disaster rather than success?
A: A disaster leaves a question open and forces a new response, which pulls the reader forward. Plain success gives them a reason to stop.

Q: In a motivation-reaction unit, what comes first, and in what order does the reaction unfold?
A: The external stimulus comes first. Then the reaction: feeling, then reflexive action, then deliberate action and speech.

Q: A thriller's middle feels breathless and confusing, all chases and fights. What might Swain suggest?
A: Add or lengthen sequels, so characters react, weigh their options and decide. That motivates the next action and gives the reader room to feel.
