## Who and when

Lajos Egri was a Hungarian-born playwright and teacher who emigrated to the United States and ran his own school of writing in New York. His book first appeared in 1942 as *How to Write a Play*. A revised edition in 1946 took the title it has kept ever since: *The Art of Dramatic Writing*, subtitled *Its Basis in the Creative Interpretation of Human Motives*.

That subtitle is the clue to the whole book. Egri thought most writing manuals got things backward, by teaching plot first and fitting characters in afterward. For him, plays grow out of people, and good plays grow out of a clear idea about people. He wrote for the stage, but the book has been read by generations of screenwriters and novelists.

He picks a direct fight with *Aristotle* along the way, and he writes with the zeal of someone who thinks he has found the answer.

## The big ideas

### The premise

Egri's first demand is that every good play has a **premise**: a short statement of what the play proves. His examples come from Shakespeare. *Macbeth*: ruthless ambition leads to its own destruction. *Romeo and Juliet*: great love defies even death.

A premise, he argues, has three parts:

- a **character** (or a trait): *ruthless ambition*
- a **conflict**: *leads to*
- a **resolution**: *its own destruction*

The premise is not a theme like "ambition" or "love". It's a whole sentence with a cause and an outcome, which the action of the play must demonstrate. Every scene should push toward proving it, and anything that doesn't is a candidate for cutting. Without a premise, Egri says, characters wander, and so does the audience.

Notice how close this is to *Robert McKee's* controlling idea, a value plus its cause. Egri got there half a century earlier.

### Character comes first

Where Aristotle ranked plot above character, Egri reverses the order. Plot, for him, is what characters do because of who they are. Invent the right people, put them in the right situation, and the plot will largely write itself. Invent a plot and push people through it, and they'll feel like puppets.

### The bone structure

To know a character that well, Egri gives every one of them a **three-dimensional bone structure**:

- **Physiology:** sex, age, appearance, health, posture, any defects. The body shapes how someone sees the world and how the world sees them.
- **Sociology:** class, job, education, home life, religion, politics, friends, amusements.
- **Psychology:** the product of the other two. Ambitions, frustrations, temperament, attitudes, complexes, talents.

The point isn't to fill in a form. It's that behavior has causes, and if you know the causes, you'll know what someone would do under pressure.

### Growth through conflict

Egri insists that characters must **grow**, meaning change, under the pressure of conflict. He borrows the language of the dialectic: everything is in constant change, and change comes through the struggle of opposites.

He also insists the change be **traced step by step**. A character moves from one pole to its opposite, say from love to hate, or from cowardice to courage, but must pass through every stage between. He calls the steps **transitions**.

### Four kinds of conflict

From this comes Egri's most practical diagnostic. Conflict, he says, can be:

- **Static:** it doesn't move. Characters argue in circles, or a character can't make a decision, and the play stalls.
- **Jumping:** it moves, but leaps from one pole to another without the transitions in between. A meek character suddenly becomes violent, and the audience doesn't believe it.
- **Rising:** the good kind. It climbs steadily, each step following from the last, because the characters are strong-willed enough to keep acting.
- **Foreshadowing:** the early hints that tell us conflict is coming.

If a draft feels dull, look for static conflict. If it feels false, look for jumping conflict.

### The unity of opposites

Strong conflict needs strong opponents who **cannot simply walk away**. Egri calls the bond that keeps them locked together the **unity of opposites**. It might be love, blood, money, duty or a shared trap. If either side could just leave, the conflict collapses. A strong bond is why marriage, family and workplace dramas are so durable.

He also asks writers to **orchestrate** the cast: to choose characters who contrast sharply, so their clashes are vivid.

### Point of attack

Where should a play begin? Egri's answer: at the point where something is at stake, ideally where a character must make a decision that will set the conflict going. Start as close to the crisis as you can, not with a long stretch of calm before anything matters.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Egri's native ground. Use the four kinds of conflict as a checklist for a dragging act, and test your central relationship against the unity of opposites: why can't they leave?
- **Screen:** Writing a premise sentence for a script forces you to decide what the story says. The three dimensions are a quick way to make secondary characters behave like real people.
- **Journalism:** You can't choose a premise in advance and make the facts prove it; that's how reporting goes wrong. But Egri's bone structure is an excellent reporting checklist for a profile, and his unity of opposites names what makes some real conflicts gripping: two sides who can't walk away.

## The pushback

- **Didactic premises.** "Proving" a thesis can make drama feel like a sermon. Many writers prefer to discover what a play means rather than set out to demonstrate it, and plays built to prove a point can make every character a mouthpiece.
- **Not every character must grow.** Comedy, satire and much modern drama feature people who don't change, and that's often the point. Chekhov's characters frequently end where they began.
- **His Aristotle is a straw man.** Aristotle never said characters didn't matter, only that we learn who people are from what they do. The two positions are closer than Egri lets on.
- **Psychology of its time.** The bone structure's talk of complexes and its assumptions about class and bodies reflect the 1940s.

## Read next

- Lajos Egri, *The Art of Dramatic Writing* (1946). Still in print.
- Lajos Egri, *The Art of Creative Writing*, a later book that applies the same ideas to fiction.
- *Robert McKee*, for the controlling idea and the claim that character is revealed by choice under pressure.

## Check yourself

Q: What are the three parts of an Egri premise? Use "ruthless ambition leads to its own destruction".
A: Character (ruthless ambition), conflict (leads to) and resolution (its own destruction).

Q: What are the three dimensions of Egri's bone structure?
A: Physiology, sociology and psychology.

Q: A meek character suddenly turns violent with no steps in between. What kind of conflict is this?
A: Jumping conflict. The character has leapt between poles without the transitions that would make the change believable.

Q: What is the unity of opposites?
A: The bond that locks protagonist and antagonist together so neither can simply walk away, which keeps the conflict going.

Q: How does Egri disagree with Aristotle?
A: He puts character first. Plot grows from who the characters are, rather than characters being fitted to a plot.
