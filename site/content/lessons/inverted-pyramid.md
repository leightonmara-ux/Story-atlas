## Where it comes from

The inverted pyramid is the shape of a hard news story: the most important facts first, then the next most important, down to background and detail at the bottom. It's called inverted because the broad, heavy part sits on top and the story narrows as it goes.

It is a 19th-century American invention, and it is still the default structure for breaking news around the world. Wire services, newspapers, news websites and press releases all use it.

### The telegraph story, and why historians doubt it

You'll often hear this origin story: during the American Civil War, reporters sent dispatches by telegraph, and because the line might be cut at any moment, they put the most important fact first. The form then spread because it was efficient.

It's a neat story, but historians are skeptical. Studies of 19th-century newspapers found that many stories were still told in chronological order long after the telegraph arrived, and well after the war. The historian David T. Z. Mindich, in *Just the Facts* (1998), pointed instead to the terse official dispatches of Secretary of War Edwin Stanton, including his reports of Lincoln's assassination in 1865, as an early model. Other researchers date the summary lead's spread to the last decades of the century, and tie it less to technology than to changing ideas about what readers need and what "objective" news should look like.

The fair summary: the telegraph probably helped, but it didn't simply cause the form. The inverted pyramid grew up gradually, alongside the rise of wire services, mass-circulation papers and the ideal of the neutral reporter.

## The big ideas

### Most important first

{{diagram:inverted-pyramid}}

Where almost every other structure in this course orders events by **time** or by **tension**, the inverted pyramid orders facts by **importance**. The climax, if there is one, comes first. There's no suspense, no rising action, and no withheld information. The writer's job is to judge, honestly and quickly, what matters most to the reader right now.

That judgment is the real skill. Two reporters with the same notebook will often agree on the facts but disagree on the lede, and the lede decides everything that follows.

### The five Ws and H

The traditional checklist for a news story is **who, what, when, where, why and how**. A complete story answers all six somewhere. The top of the story answers the ones that matter most for *this* event.

For a fire, "what" and "where" usually lead: a warehouse burned on the east side. For an arrest, "who" may lead. For a court ruling, "what" and "why" carry the weight. The checklist isn't a template to fill in order; it's a way to make sure nothing essential is missing.

### The summary lede

The first sentence, the **lede**, is a summary of the whole story in one sentence, often kept under about 35 words. It should make sense on its own. If a reader sees only that sentence, in a headline feed or a notification, they should know what happened.

A good summary lede is concrete, leads with the news rather than the background, and uses an active verb. Compare:

- "A meeting of the city council was held on Tuesday evening to discuss the budget."
- "The city council voted Tuesday to close three public libraries to cover a $4 million shortfall."

The first tells you a meeting happened. The second tells you what happened.

### Then: the second paragraph and the rest

After the lede usually comes a paragraph that supports or expands it: the key detail, or the source. Then a strong quotation, then context and background, then secondary facts, then reactions and minor details. Each paragraph is a little less essential than the one before.

### Cutting from the bottom

The great practical advantage of the form is that an editor can cut from the end without rewriting. On a print page with a fixed hole, that was vital. It still matters: syndicated stories run at different lengths in different outlets.

It also respects the reader. Most readers don't finish news stories. The inverted pyramid makes sure that wherever they stop, they've already had the most important part.

### It doesn't follow the arc

Compare this with *The shared core*. A story, in the dramatic sense, builds toward a turn. The inverted pyramid gives away the turn in the first sentence. That's why it doesn't line up with any moment of the arc in this app's concordance. It isn't a weak version of dramatic structure, it's a different tool for a different job.

## Use it

- **Journalism:** Use it for breaking news, short updates, anything time-sensitive, and anything a reader might scan. When in doubt about a news story, write the summary lede first; if you can't, you probably don't yet know what your story is.
- **Screen and stage:** Not a dramatic structure, but a useful discipline. Writing a one-sentence "lede" for a script, or for each scene, is a quick test of whether you know what's important. Loglines are a cousin of the summary lede.
- **Any writer:** Emails, reports and pitches benefit from the same principle. Put the news, the request or the decision first.

## When it's the wrong choice

The inverted pyramid is strong at *informing* and weak at *engaging*. Because it gives away the outcome at once, it gives readers no reason to keep going, and it can't build feeling or suspense. For features and narratives, writers turn to other shapes: the feature formula (see *The feature formula*), or full narrative structure (see *Jon Franklin* and *Jack Hart*).

Many news stories today are hybrids: a summary lede, then a short anecdote, then the pyramid. Online, some editors argue that "most important first" matters more than ever, because readers skim on phones; others argue that a wall of facts loses readers who want a reason to care.

## The pushback

- **It flattens events.** Ordering by importance can strip out sequence and cause. Readers learn *what* happened but not always *how* one thing led to another.
- **It hides judgment.** The form looks neutral, but deciding what is "most important" is a choice, and the facts at the bottom (often context, or the people affected) are the ones that get cut.
- **It's dull.** Critics of newswriting have long argued that formulaic ledes make every story sound the same, and that a rigid pyramid discourages good writing.
- **The origin myth.** The tidy telegraph story is itself a reminder to check the history you're told about your own craft.

## Try it

Take a news story you're working on, or a recent event you know the facts of.

1. Go through the five Ws and H, and decide which two matter most for *this* event.
2. Write a summary lede of under about 35 words that leads with them: concrete, self-contained, with an active verb.
3. Rank the rest of your facts by importance and write a short paragraph for each, most important first.
4. Cut the last two paragraphs. If anything essential went with them, it was in the wrong place; move it up.

- **Stage:** write a one-sentence lede for each scene of your play, saying plainly what happens in it. Any scene you can't summarize needs work.
- **Screen:** write your logline as a summary lede, in under 35 words.
- **Journalism:** read your lede alone, as if it were a notification, and see whether it tells the reader what happened.

**Check:** If a reader stopped after any paragraph, would they already have the most important facts?

## Check yourself

Q: How does the inverted pyramid order information?
A: By importance, not by time or tension: the most important facts first, then progressively less important ones.

Q: What is the popular telegraph explanation, and why is it doubted?
A: That Civil War reporters put key facts first in case the line was cut. Historians found chronological stories persisted long after the telegraph arrived, and they point to other causes, such as official dispatches, wire services and the ideal of objectivity.

Q: What should a summary lede do?
A: Tell the reader what happened in one concrete, self-contained sentence, leading with the news rather than the background.

Q: Why can editors cut an inverted-pyramid story from the bottom?
A: Each paragraph is less essential than the one before, so removing the end loses the least important material without rewriting.

Q: When is the inverted pyramid the wrong choice?
A: When the goal is to engage, build feeling or tell a story that rewards reading to the end, as in features and narratives.
