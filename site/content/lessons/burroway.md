## Who and when

Janet Burroway is an American novelist, playwright and teacher who taught for many years at Florida State University. In 1982 she published *Writing Fiction: A Guide to Narrative Craft*, a textbook for creative writing classes. It became one of the most widely used books of its kind in the English-speaking world, and it has been revised again and again. Later editions were written with Elizabeth Stuckey-French and Ned Stuckey-French; the tenth appeared from the University of Chicago Press in 2019.

That history explains what kind of book it is. Burroway isn't a guru with a patented system. She is a working writer gathering the best of what's been said about fiction, from *Aristotle* and *Forster* onward, testing it against stories, and turning it into practice for a workshop. The book pairs its chapters with short stories and exercises, and it has trained a great many of the writers now teaching.

You won't find a twelve-step diagram here. What you'll find is the craft vocabulary that most fiction workshops share, set out clearly, and a few memorable formulations that have become part of how writers talk.

## The big ideas

### Only trouble is interesting

Burroway's best-known line comes from her discussion of plot:

> In literature only trouble is interesting.
> — Janet Burroway, Writing Fiction

Her point is not that fiction must be violent or melodramatic. It's that a smooth day, a happy marriage or a picnic that goes well isn't yet a story. In life we avoid trouble; in fiction we go looking for it. The trouble can be very quiet: a misunderstanding at a dinner table, a decision put off. But something has to be at stake and in doubt.

### Conflict, crisis, resolution

Burroway teaches the basic shape of a story as **conflict, crisis and resolution**. Two forces pull against each other; the struggle builds; at the **crisis** it comes to a head and one force prevails; the **resolution** is the changed situation that follows.

She stresses that the crisis is usually best **shown, not reported**: written as a scene, in the moment, so the reader experiences the turn rather than hearing about it afterward. A story that summarizes its most important moment throws away its biggest payoff.

She also stresses that a story records a **change**. Something must be different at the end. In literary fiction, the change is often internal, a shift in how a character sees things, but it has to be earned by what came before.

### Story and plot

Burroway builds on Forster's distinction. **Story** is the events in the order they happened; **plot** is the deliberate arrangement of those events to create meaning, suspense and emotion. Since the plot is chosen, a writer can start anywhere, hold things back and reveal them when they'll matter most. (*Story vs. telling* explores this in depth.)

### Another lens: connection and disconnection

Later editions of *Writing Fiction* also acknowledge that the conflict model doesn't fit every story. Alongside it they offer another way of seeing: a story as a pattern of **connection and disconnection** between characters, people moving toward each other and apart. Many quiet, domestic stories that seem to have no "fight" make sense in these terms. It's an important softening of the strict conflict-first view, and it links to the *Counterpoints* track.

### Showing and telling

Burroway gives one of the clearest accounts of the workshop's most repeated advice. Fiction works through **significant detail**: details that are **concrete** (they appeal to the senses) and **specific** (this dog, not a dog), and that also carry meaning, suggesting an idea or a judgment without stating it.

A line like "he was a cruel man" tells. Showing would be the moment he lets a door swing shut in a child's face. The reader reaches the judgment themselves and believes it more because of that.

But she doesn't treat "show, don't tell" as absolute. Summary and direct statement have their uses: to move quickly through time, to give background, to let a narrator think aloud. The skill is choosing which to use where, and keeping the most important moments in scene.

### Scene and summary

Closely linked is the balance of **scene** (moment-by-moment, with dialogue and action, slow) and **summary** (compressed, covering time quickly). A story made only of scenes is exhausting; one made only of summary keeps the reader at arm's length. Writers move between them, using summary to get to the next scene and scenes for the moments that turn.

### Revealing character

Burroway sets out the ways a writer can reveal character, broadly: what people look like, what they say, what they do, and what they think, together with what the narrator or other characters tell us about them. The most vivid characters, she argues, come through contradiction and through action. People are complicated, and a character who wants two incompatible things at once is alive on the page.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Conflict, crisis, resolution is the same shape playwrights work with, and her advice to show the crisis in scene is simply how theatre works. Her treatment of dialogue, which should do more than one job at a time, transfers directly.
- **Screen:** Film can only show, so "significant detail" is a screenwriter's daily tool: the prop, the gesture, the image that tells us who someone is without a line of dialogue.
- **Journalism:** Narrative nonfiction uses exactly these tools. Report for significant detail, the specific, concrete thing that carries meaning. Decide what goes in scene and what in summary. And find the trouble: the problem, the risk, the moment of doubt that makes a true story worth following.

## The pushback

- **A synthesis, not a theory.** The book's strength, its balanced survey of craft, is also why it offers few ideas that are wholly its own. Writers looking for a bold system will look elsewhere.
- **The workshop consensus.** Critics of the creative writing workshop, such as Matthew Salesses in *Craft in the Real World* (2021), argue that the "standard" craft advice taught by books like this reflects particular cultural assumptions, for example about conflict, character and what counts as a satisfying ending.
- **Show, don't tell, overused.** Even with Burroway's caveats, the rule is often applied mechanically in workshops, producing prose crowded with details and empty of reflection.
- **Conflict-first.** Even in its softened form, the model still puts trouble at the center, which writers in other traditions, such as those behind Kishōtenketsu, would question.

## Try it

Take a story or scene you're working on.

1. Name the trouble in one sentence: what is at stake, and in doubt? If you can't, the draft may not be a story yet.
2. Find your crisis, the moment one force prevails. Is it written in scene or in summary? If it's summarized, rewrite it as a scene, moment by moment, so the reader lives through the turn.
3. In that scene, find a line that tells a judgment ("she was furious", "he was a cruel man") and replace it with a significant detail: concrete, specific, and carrying the judgment without stating it.

- **Stage:** your crisis is already in scene, so check the dialogue instead. Make one line do two jobs at once.
- **Screen:** replace one line of dialogue with a prop or gesture that tells us who someone is.
- **Journalism:** go through your notes for the significant details. Decide what goes in scene and what in summary, and put in scene only what you witnessed or can reconstruct reliably.

**Check:** Would a reader reach your judgment of a character without your stating it?

## Check yourself

Q: What does Burroway mean by "only trouble is interesting"?
A: In fiction, something must be at stake and in doubt. Smooth, untroubled events aren't yet a story, though the trouble can be quiet.

Q: What are the three parts of the basic story shape Burroway teaches?
A: Conflict, crisis and resolution.

Q: Why should the crisis usually be written as a scene?
A: So the reader experiences the turn as it happens, rather than being told about the most important moment afterward.

Q: What makes a detail "significant"?
A: It's concrete and specific, and it also suggests an idea or judgment without stating it.

Q: What alternative to the conflict model do later editions of *Writing Fiction* offer?
A: Seeing a story as a pattern of connection and disconnection between characters.
