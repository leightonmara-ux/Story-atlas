## Who and when

John McPhee has written for *The New Yorker* since the 1960s, on subjects as unpromising-sounding as oranges, geology, freight trains, shad fishing and the merchant marine. He won the Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction in 1999 for *Annals of the Former World*, his long work on the geology of North America. For decades he has also taught a famous nonfiction writing seminar at Princeton University, where many well-known journalists learned the trade.

*Draft No. 4: On the Writing Process* (2017) collects eight essays about how he works, most of them first published in *The New Yorker*. The one that matters most here is called "Structure". McPhee thinks about structure more explicitly than almost any other nonfiction writer, and he draws it, literally, as diagrams.

## The big ideas

### Structure comes before writing

McPhee's rule of thumb is that you should know the structure of a piece before you write the first sentence. Not the sentences, not the details, but the shape: where it begins, how the parts are arranged, and where it ends.

He's frank about how hard that can be. In "Structure" he tells the story of being stuck on an early piece, *The Pine Barrens* (about a wild stretch of New Jersey), with a mass of material and no way to order it. He spent about two weeks lying on his back on a picnic table in his yard, unable to see how it went together. The answer turned out to be a person: Fred Brown, a resident of the Barrens whom McPhee had met, was connected to most of the topics he needed to cover. Brown became the thread the whole piece could hang on.

The lesson: structure often comes from finding the right organizing element *in the material*, not from a template.

### Structure from the material

This is McPhee's central conviction. The shape of a piece shouldn't be imposed on the material from outside; it should grow out of what you've gathered. Every subject suggests its own structure, and the writer's job is to find it.

That makes him a useful contrast to many screen theorists in this course, who start with a template (three acts, a beat sheet) and fit the story to it. McPhee would say: look at what you have first.

### Diagrams

McPhee draws the structure of his pieces as simple shapes before he writes. "Structure" reproduces several of these drawings. Two examples are well known:

- **"Travels in Georgia"** (1973) follows a trip McPhee took through Georgia with two naturalists who, among other things, collected animals killed on the road. He didn't begin at the beginning. He opened partway through the journey with a vivid scene, then looped back to the start, caught up with the opening scene, and continued to the end. His drawing shows the line of the story doubling back on itself once before carrying on.
- **Encounters with the Archdruid** (1971) is built on three journeys. David Brower, the famous environmentalist, travels with three adversaries in turn: a mineral engineer, a resort developer and the commissioner who built dams across the West. The structure is a sequence of three parallel encounters, each a kind of debate on location.

The point isn't any particular shape. It's that drawing the shape forces you to see the whole and to make deliberate choices.

### Chronology and theme

One recurring choice McPhee discusses is between **chronological** and **thematic** order. Chronology is the natural default and often the best one: things happen in order, and readers follow cause and effect easily. But chronology can bury your best material, or take too long to start. Thematic structure, grouping material by subject, can be clearer but loses forward motion. Often the answer is a combination: a chronological spine with thematic sections hung on it, or a chronology that starts in the middle, as in "Travels in Georgia".

### Sorting the material

McPhee describes his working method in detail. He typed up all his notes, coded each passage by topic, and physically sorted the pieces into piles or folders, one per section of the planned structure. Later he did the same on a computer, with software written for him by a Princeton programmer. Then he wrote one section at a time, with only that section's material in front of him.

The method sounds mechanical, but it's a way of freeing the writer. Once the structure is decided and the material sorted, each day's writing is a manageable job rather than an overwhelming one.

### Strong but quiet

For all the planning, McPhee's finished pieces wear their structure lightly. Most readers never notice the scaffolding; they simply find the piece easy to follow and hard to put down. The diagrams are for the writer, not the reader.

### Where the habit started

McPhee credits a high-school English teacher, Mrs. McKee, who made her students submit a structural outline with every composition. Decades of writing began with that habit: shape first.

## Use it

- **Journalism:** After reporting, before drafting, draw your piece. A line for chronology, loops for flashbacks, branches for digressions. If you can't draw it, you don't know it yet. Then sort your notes into the sections of the drawing.
- **Screen:** Editors of documentaries do exactly this with footage. McPhee's "start in the middle, loop back, catch up" structure is one of the most common shapes in film, from cold opens to flash-forward prologues.
- **Stage:** The idea of letting the material suggest the structure is a useful counterweight to template thinking. A play built from interviews or real events, especially, may want a shape no textbook gives you.

## The pushback

- **Not everyone can plan first.** Many writers discover structure by drafting. For them, McPhee's insistence on structure before writing may be paralyzing rather than freeing.
- **It's a method for long, reported pieces.** The diagrams and sorting system fit book-length work and long magazine features. They're less useful for a daily news story or a short essay.
- **Little about character and conflict.** McPhee writes about structure as arrangement. Compared with *Jon Franklin* or *Jack Hart*, he has less to say about drama: complications, resolutions, the people who drive them. Some of his pieces have very little conflict at all, which is either a limitation or a demonstration that arrangement alone can hold a reader.
- **Hard to imitate.** His structures look obvious once you've seen them and are very hard to find in your own material.
