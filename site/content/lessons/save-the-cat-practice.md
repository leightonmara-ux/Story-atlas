## Who and when

This is the hands-on companion to *Blake Snyder*, which explains the beat sheet, the ten genres and the best-known rules. Here we put them to work, using Snyder's three books: *Save the Cat!* (2005), *Save the Cat! Goes to the Movies* (2007), which sorts and breaks down films in each genre, and *Save the Cat! Strikes Back* (2009), written after years of teaching the method.

The film breakdown is ours, not Snyder's. We use *Die Hard* (1988), a film Snyder treated as the classic **Dude with a Problem**: an ordinary person dragged into extraordinary danger. It's easy to find, and its beats are unusually clear.

## The big ideas

### Die Hard, beat by beat

The figure in brackets is Snyder's target as a share of the running time (his page numbers divided by 110). Real films drift, so time it yourself.

1. **Opening Image** (first minute): New York cop John McClane on a plane landing in Los Angeles, gripping the armrest. A passenger tells him to make fists with his toes on the carpet to unwind. A tense man, alone, out of place.
2. **Theme Stated** (5%): In the limo, McClane tells the driver, Argyle, that his wife Holly took a big job in LA and he stayed behind, expecting her to come back. Can he let go of that pride?
3. **Set-Up** (0–10%): The office Christmas party. Holly uses her maiden name at work; her boss Takagi is warm; her colleague Ellis is a creep. John and Holly quarrel within minutes, and he's left barefoot in her washroom.
4. **Catalyst** (11%): Hans Gruber's armed men seize the building. McClane, shoeless, slips into the stairwell.
5. **Debate** (11–23%): He hides and watches Gruber shoot Takagi. He argues with himself, pulls a fire alarm, and hears the gang call off the fire trucks.
6. **Break into Two** (23%): He kills one of the gang and sends the body down in the lift with a mocking message. He has chosen to fight.
7. **B Story** (27%): On a captured radio he reaches Sergeant Al Powell, an LAPD officer outside. Their friendship, entirely by voice, is the film's heart.
8. **Fun and Games** (27–50%): A barefoot cop against a gang in an unfinished skyscraper: air ducts, lift shafts, radio taunts, a body dropped on Powell's car.
9. **Midpoint** (50%): The police storm the building and fail; McClane drops explosives down a lift shaft to stop the gang's rockets. A victory, but the stakes rise: the authorities and the press now become part of his problem.
10. **Bad Guys Close In** (50–68%): Ellis tries to bargain with Gruber and is shot. Gruber, meeting McClane face to face, passes himself off as a hostage. A TV report will soon reveal that Holly is McClane's wife.
11. **All Is Lost** (68%): Gruber's men shoot out the glass around him. He escapes with his feet cut to pieces, and Gruber has the detonators back.
12. **Dark Night of the Soul** (68–77%): In a washroom, pulling glass from his feet, McClane asks Powell to tell Holly he should have backed her, and he's sorry. A "whiff of death", and the theme said aloud.
13. **Break into Three** (77%): The hostages are being taken to a roof wired to explode, and Gruber has Holly. The fight and the marriage are now the same fight.
14. **Finale** (77–99%): The roof, the leap on a fire hose, the gun taped to his back. Gruber falls, clutching Holly's wrist, until McClane unclasps her watch, a gift from her company. Outside, Powell, who told McClane he hasn't drawn his gun since shooting a boy by mistake, shoots the last gunman.
15. **Final Image** (last minute): John and Holly together in Argyle's limo, driving off to "Let It Snow". The man alone has become half of a couple.

Notice how much the B story does. Powell gives McClane someone to confess to, and his own wound pays off at the end.

### The five-point finale

In *Strikes Back*, Snyder went back to the Finale beat and broke it into five steps:

1. **Gathering the Team:** the hero rallies allies and tools, and makes a plan.
2. **Executing the Plan:** the plan goes ahead, and seems to work.
3. **The High Tower Surprise:** the hero reaches the tower and the princess isn't there. A twist breaks the plan, like a second Catalyst.
4. **Dig, Deep Down:** with the plan gone, the hero has to find something inside: nerve, faith, the lesson of the B story. A second, shorter Debate.
5. **The Execution of the New Plan:** the hero acts on what they found.

*Die Hard* fits loosely, since McClane's "team" is a voice on a radio. His plan, getting the hostages off the roof, works until an FBI helicopter fires on him and the roof blows (the surprise). Wounded and nearly out of bullets, he digs deep, and the new plan is the taped gun.

### The other rules of thumb

*Blake Snyder* covers Save the Cat, the Pope in the Pool, Double Mumbo Jumbo, Laying Pipe and the Covenant of the Arc. These are the rest that writers use most. The examples are ours.

- **Is it primal?** The hero's drive should be one a caveman would understand: survive, protect your family, find a mate. McClane wants to save his wife and stay alive. If your hero wants a promotion, ask what primal need it stands for.
- **Black Vet** (also called **Too Much Marzipan**): don't pile one clever idea on another. A ghost story where the ghost is also a time traveller has one hook too many.
- **Watch Out for That Glacier!:** danger must be close and coming fast. A threat that creeps in from far away, like a glacier, builds no suspense. If your menace is slow (an illness, a lawsuit), give it a near deadline.
- **Keep the Press Out:** once news crews arrive, the story stops being private and starts to feel less real, unless it's about the press. Snyder credited the rule to Steven Spielberg. *Die Hard* bends it on purpose: its TV reporter is one more antagonist.
- **The Hero Leads:** the hero must drive the action. If other characters keep telling yours what to do, something's wrong.
- **Make the Bad Guy Badder:** a hero is only as big as the opposition. Gruber is smarter and better armed than McClane at every turn.

### A diagnosis, not a template

The beat sheet's best use is on a draft that already exists. Write the story first, then map it against the fifteen beats and read the gaps:

- **No Catalyst by 15%?** The opening is probably all set-up.
- **Fun and Games that aren't fun?** You may not know your premise's promise. What would the trailer show?
- **A sagging middle?** If nothing raises the stakes halfway, the second half has nothing new to push against.
- **An unearned ending?** Look for a Dark Night, and a Break into Three where the B story hands the hero the answer.
- **Opening and Final Images that match?** Then the hero hasn't changed, or you haven't shown it.

A missing beat isn't automatically a mistake. It's a question: is the gap deliberate, and does it serve the story?

## Use it

- **Screen:** Break down a produced film in your genre (*Goes to the Movies* covers many), then your own draft. If your Act Three is a single fight, try the five-point finale.
- **Stage:** Ignore the percentages. But the five-point finale suits many final scenes (a plan, a surprise, an inner reckoning, a new choice), and "Is it primal?" is a sharp test of a character's objective (see *Stanislavski*).
- **Journalism:** Be careful: you can't invent a Midpoint. But some narrative features really do follow a person through a plan, a surprise and a reckoning, and "Watch Out for That Glacier" is good advice for a lede: start near the danger.

## The pushback

- **Retrofitting is easy.** Almost any film fits fifteen beats if you're generous about what counts. Our *Die Hard* mapping involves judgment calls, and others place the Midpoint or Theme Stated elsewhere.
- **Rules from one market.** These rules grew out of studio comedies and action films. Is It Primal and Keep the Press Out suit spectacle better than quiet drama.
- **A finale checklist.** Five points for every ending adds to the sameness critics already complain about (see *Blake Snyder*).
- **Genres overlap.** Snyder's placements are debatable, and his followers disagree about some films.

## Try it

Map your own draft, or a film in your genre, against the beat sheet before changing a word.

1. Note the minute (or page) of each of the fifteen beats you can find. Leave blanks where you can't.
2. Divide each by the total length and compare with Snyder's targets. Circle anything more than 10% off.
3. For each blank or circled beat, write one sentence: deliberate, or a problem?
4. Check your Act Three against the five-point finale. Where's the High Tower Surprise?

- **Stage:** skip step 2 and look only for the turns, especially the midpoint and the dark night.
- **Screen:** do it for a produced film first, then your script, and compare the drift.
- **Journalism:** map a published narrative feature, and note which beats the facts supplied.

**Check:** For each gap, can you say whether it's a choice or a mistake?

## Check yourself

Q: What are the five steps of Snyder's five-point finale?
A: Gathering the Team, Executing the Plan, the High Tower Surprise, Dig Deep Down, and the Execution of the New Plan.

Q: What does "Watch Out for That Glacier!" warn against?
A: A threat that's far away or slow. Danger should be close and closing in fast.

Q: What is "Black Vet", or "Too Much Marzipan"?
A: Piling one clever idea onto another. A story needs one hook, not two.

Q: In *Die Hard*, what does the B story give the A story?
A: Powell gives McClane someone to confess to, which states the theme, and Powell's own wound pays off when he saves McClane.

Q: How should you use the beat sheet on a draft?
A: As a diagnosis: map what's there, then ask whether each missing or misplaced beat is a choice or a problem.
