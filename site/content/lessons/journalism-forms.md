## Where it comes from

Journalism's forms weren't designed by theorists. They grew in newsrooms, were named by editors and passed on at the copy desk. Some have known birthdays. *The New Yorker* launched its "Profiles" department in 1925 and gave the form its name. *Playboy* made the long magazine Q&A famous with its first Playboy Interview, Alex Haley's conversation with Miles Davis (1962). Studs Terkel's *Hard Times* (1970) and *Working* (1974) made oral history a popular form. Others, like the trend piece, simply accumulated.

What unites them is a **promise to the reader**. Each form tells readers, in its first lines, what kind of reading they're about to do and what they'll have at the end. Pick the wrong form and you break the promise: an investigation that buries its finding, a profile with no person in it.

| Form | What it promises | Typical structure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hard news | What happened, fast | Inverted pyramid |
| Feature | Why a situation matters | Anecdote, nut graf, body, kicker |
| Profile | To know a person | Scene, why now, life, testimony, return |
| Investigation | A wrong exposed, with proof | Finding, evidence, case, response, stakes |
| Explainer | To understand | Question, short answer, layers of context |
| Obituary | A life summed up | News of death, what they did, the life, survivors |
| Day in the life | What it's like | The clock |
| Trend piece | Something new is happening | Examples, then the evidence it's real |
| Narrative | An experience | The arc |
| Oral history, Q&A | The voices themselves | Edited speech |

## The big ideas

### Hard news and the feature

Both have their own lessons. Hard news promises the most important facts first, so a reader can stop at any paragraph; see *The inverted pyramid*. The feature promises a person, a situation and why it matters, through the anecdote, nut graf, body and kicker of *The feature formula*. Most other forms are variations on one of these two.

### The profile

A profile promises to make a person known: not a résumé, but how they think and act. The usual shape opens on the subject doing something revealing, explains why we're reading about them now, moves back through their life, brings in other people's testimony, and returns to a final scene.

The classic lesson in observing is Gay Talese's "Frank Sinatra Has a Cold" (*Esquire*, 1966). Sinatra wouldn't talk to him, so Talese built the piece from watching Sinatra and interviewing the people around him. It opens on the singer at a bar, holding a bourbon, silent, with a cold that has put his whole entourage on edge. Profiles raise hard questions about access and fairness: see *Janet Malcolm & John Hersey* and *Who gets to be the subject*.

### The investigation

The investigation promises that something hidden and wrong has been found, and proved. Its structure inverts the mystery: **the finding comes first, then the proof.** A typical first story opens on the core finding, often with a phrase like "a review of records shows", then gives the evidence and how it was gathered, a human case that shows the harm, the response of those accused, and why it matters beyond this case.

*The Boston Globe*'s Spotlight team opened its 2002 series on clergy abuse with the case of John Geoghan, a priest accused by more than 130 people, and the finding that church leaders had known of the accusations and moved him from parish to parish anyway. Readers got the scandal in the first paragraphs; the rest of the story made it undeniable. Mark Lee Hunter's UNESCO manual *Story-Based Inquiry* (2009) teaches investigators to start from a hypothesis and test it, which helps keep the finding in view as you report.

### The explainer

The explainer promises understanding. It starts from the reader's question (what is this, why is it happening, why should I care?), answers it in a sentence or two, then adds layers: how we got here, the competing views, what we don't know. Its enemy is the writer's urge to tell everything. Its test: could a reader who stops after the third paragraph explain the issue to a friend?

### The obituary

The news obituary has fixed parts: name and identity in the first sentence ("who did what"), the death, its date and place, the age, the cause where known and the source for it; then the life, mostly in order; and finally the survivors. *The New York Times* style ends the first paragraph with a short sentence of age: "He was 87." Big papers keep **advance obituaries** on file for famous people; Alden Whitman, the Times's chief obituary writer from the mid-1960s, made a practice of interviewing subjects for their own.

The art lies beyond the formula. Hugh Massingberd's obituaries at *The Daily Telegraph* from 1986 revived the witty, frank British obituary. *The Economist* runs one each week on its last page, unsigned, often about someone readers have never heard of, written as an essay on a life rather than a record (Ann Wroe has written them since 2003). In 2018 the Times began "Overlooked", obituaries for women and others it had never covered.

### The day in the life

This form promises to put you inside someone's routine: a nurse's shift, a farmer's harvest. The clock is the structure, with time stamps as headers or scene breaks. Its risk is monotony; the best ones pick a day when something happens, or let small events build. A cousin is the **tick-tock**, a minute-by-minute reconstruction of a big event.

### The trend piece

The trend piece promises that something is new and spreading. Newsrooms joke that "three makes a trend": three anecdotes, and you have a story. That's the pitfall. Anecdotes prove only that something happens, not that it's growing. The media critic Jack Shafer spent years mocking "bogus trend stories" at *Slate*. The famous warning is *Newsweek*'s 1986 cover story claiming a 40-year-old single woman was more likely to be killed by a terrorist than to marry; the statistic didn't hold up, and the magazine retracted it in 2006. A good trend piece needs data showing change over time, and admits how much remains uncertain.

### The narrative long-form piece

Narrative promises an experience: a protagonist, a complication, a resolution, told in scenes. Its methods are covered by *Jon Franklin*, *Jack Hart* and *Tom Wolfe & the New Journalism*. Hart's useful point: if your material lacks a resolution, you may have an explanatory narrative or a profile, not a story.

### Oral history and the Q&A

Both promise the **voice itself**. In oral history, the editor's art is invisible but total: choosing which speakers to include, cutting, and sequencing voices so they answer and contradict each other. Studs Terkel's *Working* lets people describe their jobs at length; Svetlana Alexievich, awarded the Nobel Prize in 2015, built *Voices from Chernobyl* (1997) from such testimony.

The Q&A keeps the questions visible. The *Paris Review* "Writers at Work" interviews (from 1953) and the Playboy Interview made it an art. Its structure is a short introduction, then the exchange, which is usually condensed and reordered. Saying "edited for length and clarity" is the convention; not changing what anyone meant is the obligation.

## Use it

- **Journalism:** Before reporting, write the form's promise in one sentence ("By the end you'll know why the river flooded"). Check the first paragraph keeps it.
- **Stage:** Oral history and the Q&A are close cousins of verbatim theatre (see *Stage forms*); the obituary and profile are monologue material.
- **Screen:** Documentary borrows all these: the profile doc, the investigative doc, the day-in-the-life observational film (see *Bill Nichols*).

## The pushback

- **Forms harden into templates.** A reporter can cram material into the familiar shape instead of finding what it needs.
- **The promise can be faked.** Explainers can carry opinion as explanation; trend pieces can sell a hunch as news.
- **Obituaries reflect who mattered to editors.** "Overlooked" exists because the record left out so many.

## Try it
Take one piece of reporting you have, or a news event from this week, and pitch it in four forms.
1. Write the first paragraph of it as an investigation (the finding first), an explainer (the reader's question first), a profile (a person doing something) and a trend piece (the pattern).
2. Under each, write the promise in one sentence, and list the evidence you'd still need to keep it. For the trend piece, name the data, not just the anecdotes.
3. Choose the form your material can actually support.

- **Stage:** Turn the profile version into a two-minute monologue in the subject's voice.
- **Screen:** Outline the investigative version as a documentary's first five minutes.
- **Journalism:** Write a 150-word obituary of a public figure who died in the past year, in the standard order, then compare with how a major paper did it.

**Check:** Could a reader say, after the first paragraph, what kind of story this is and what they'll have at the end?

## Check yourself

Q: How does an investigation's structure differ from a mystery's?
A: It puts the finding first, then the proof: evidence and method, a human case, the response of those accused, and the wider stakes.

Q: What are the standard parts of a news obituary?
A: Name and what they were known for, the death with date, place, age and cause, then the life in rough order, then the survivors.

Q: What's wrong with "three makes a trend"?
A: Three anecdotes prove something happens, not that it's growing. A real trend needs evidence of change over time.

Q: What does an explainer promise, and how is it built?
A: Understanding: it starts from the reader's question, answers it briefly, then adds layers of context.

Q: Where does the art lie in an oral history?
A: In the editing: which voices to include, what to cut, and how to sequence voices so they answer and contradict each other.
