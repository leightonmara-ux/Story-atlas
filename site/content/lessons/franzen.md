## Who and when

Jonathan Franzen (born 1959) is an American novelist who grew up in Webster Groves, a suburb of St. Louis. His first two novels, *The Twenty-Seventh City* (1988) and *Strong Motion* (1992), were noticed but didn't sell. *The Corrections* (2001), a family novel set around a father's decline, won the National Book Award and made him famous. *Freedom* (2010), *Purity* (2015) and *Crossroads* (2021) followed.

He is also a prolific essayist, and that's why he's here. Across three collections, *How to Be Alone* (2002), *Farther Away* (2012) and *The End of the End of the World* (2018), he keeps returning to the same questions: what is a novel for, what does a writer owe a reader, and how do you concentrate long enough to write one? His answers are sharp, often contrary, and useful well beyond the novel.

## The big ideas

### Contract or Status?

In "Mr. Difficult", published in *The New Yorker* in 2002 and subtitled "William Gaddis and the problem of hard-to-read books", Franzen describes two ways of thinking about what fiction is.

In the **Status model**, a novel is a work of art whose value doesn't depend on how many people enjoy it. The writer is an artist, possibly a genius. If the book is hard, that may be a sign of its seriousness, and if readers can't follow it, that's their problem.

In the **Contract model**, a novel is an agreement between writer and reader. The reader gives time, attention and trust; the writer promises something worth having in return: pleasure, connection, a sense of being less alone. Difficulty is allowed, but it has to pay. A book that makes the reader work hard for nothing has broken the deal.

Franzen admits he holds both views. He says he grew up reading in the Status spirit, admiring the great difficult modernists, and became a Contract writer. His test case is William Gaddis (1922–1998). He respected the ambition of Gaddis's *The Recognitions* (1955), but found *J R* (1975), a long novel told almost entirely in unattributed dialogue, exhausting, and asked what the reader gets back for all that labor.

For writers, the useful part isn't the verdict on Gaddis. It's the question: *what am I asking of my audience, and what am I giving back?*

### Why bother?

In 1996, *Harper's* published Franzen's long essay "Perchance to Dream" (the title was an editor's choice, echoing *Hamlet*). He revised and shortened it as "Why Bother?" for *How to Be Alone*.

The original was a lament. Franzen had wanted to write the big "social novel" that engages with the whole culture, and had come to believe that television and electronic media had made the novel culturally marginal. Many readers took the essay as a promise that his next book would revive the social novel, and judged *The Corrections* against it.

The revised version arrives somewhere gentler. Drawing on the linguistic anthropologist Shirley Brice Heath's research on who goes on reading serious fiction, Franzen concludes that there was never a mass audience to lose. Readers of serious novels are a scattered community, often people who felt a bit apart as children and found company in books. The novel doesn't need to change society to matter. It needs to keep faith with those readers, and the writer is freed to write for the book's own sake rather than to save the culture.

### Ten rules

In 2010, *The Guardian* asked writers for their rules for fiction, inspired by Elmore Leonard's well-known list. Franzen's ten are short and pointed, and a version of the list appears in *The End of the End of the World*. The first sets the tone:

> The reader is a friend, not an adversary, not a spectator.
— Jonathan Franzen, *The Guardian* (2010)

A friend, so you don't trick, bully or lecture them. Not a spectator, so you don't perform at them; you bring them in. That one line is the Contract model in miniature.

The others, mostly paraphrased:

- Fiction that isn't a personal adventure into the frightening or the unknown isn't worth writing, except for money.
- Don't use "then" as a conjunction to dodge too many "ands". It's a lazy fix.
- Write in the third person unless a really distinctive first-person voice insists.
- When information is free for everyone, piling up research for a novel counts for less.
- You see more by sitting still than by chasing after things.
- Interesting verbs are seldom very interesting.
- You have to love before you can be relentless, usually read as: care for your characters before you're hard on them.

### Autobiography by invention

The sixth rule is the one writers quote most:

> The most purely autobiographical fiction requires pure invention.
— Jonathan Franzen, *The Guardian* (2010)

He follows it by saying that nobody ever wrote a more autobiographical story than Kafka's *The Metamorphosis*. Kafka never woke up as an insect, but the story is a precise picture of how he felt inside his family.

Franzen develops the point in essays collected in *Farther Away*. Fiction that merely transcribes your life tends to be lifeless; the autobiography that matters is the emotional struggle the book records, and the change the writer has to go through to finish it. Put it with the second rule: if writing the book didn't frighten or change you, it probably won't move anyone else.

### Concentration and the internet

His eighth rule doubts that anyone with an internet connection at their workplace is writing good fiction. A 2010 *Time* profile reported that he had disabled his laptop's internet connection, plugging the Ethernet port with glue. In a 2011 commencement speech at Kenyon College, adapted for *The New York Times* as "Liking Is for Cowards. Go for What Hurts.", he set the easy "liking" of technology against the risk and pain of real love. Behind both is a craft claim: the deep, sustained attention a long story needs is fragile, and a writer has to protect it.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Theatre audiences can't reread or skip, so the Contract is literal: they've paid and they're sitting there. Ask what each difficult stretch of your play gives back. "Not a spectator" is a useful challenge in a form built on watching: how does your play make the audience participate in working out what's going on?
- **Screen:** "You have to love before you can be relentless" is good advice for writing unlikeable leads (compare *Blake Snyder*'s save-the-cat moment). And the Metamorphosis rule suits genre: a monster, a heist or a time loop can carry your own experience more truly than a memoir-like script.
- **Journalism:** Journalism already runs on the Contract model; a reader who feels tricked stops trusting the outlet. "You see more sitting still than chasing after" is a reporter's rule too: stay longer in one place rather than collecting more quotes. And the research rule is a warning: in an age of free information, the value is in what you saw and understood, not in how much you looked up.

## The pushback

- **A false choice?** Many critics say Contract and Status aren't the only two options. The novelist Ben Marcus answered Franzen in *Harper's* in 2005, defending experimental fiction and arguing that difficulty can be its own reward and its own kind of deal with readers. Others point out that Franzen's own long, allusive novels hardly fit a pure Contract model.
- **Public quarrels.** In 2001, after Oprah Winfrey chose *The Corrections* for her book club, Franzen spoke uneasily about the choice in interviews, and she withdrew her invitation to appear on her show. Many saw him as a snob; he later said he regretted how he'd put it.
- **Who gets attention.** When *Freedom* was greeted with lavish coverage in 2010, the novelists Jennifer Weiner and Jodi Picoult objected that reviewers favored white male literary writers; the complaint became known as "Franzenfreude". It was aimed at the press more than at his books.
- **Grumpiness about technology.** His criticism of the internet and social media is often called nostalgic, and the rules have been mocked as easy to say for a writer with time and money. Supporters reply that he's describing what helps him concentrate, not legislating.

## Try it

Take a difficult passage in your work-in-progress: a long explanation, an unusual structure, a stretch where the audience has to work.

1. Write down exactly what you're asking of the reader or viewer there: patience, memory, tolerance of confusion.
2. Write down what they get back, and when.
3. If the second list is empty or vague, cut, clarify, or move the reward closer.
4. Then find one place where you're performing *at* the audience, and rewrite it so they're working something out alongside you.

- **Stage:** do it for a scene where the audience doesn't yet know what's going on. When do they find out, and is it worth the wait?
- **Screen:** do it for a nonlinear opening or a dense exposition scene.
- **Journalism:** do it for a slow lede or a long background section. Is the reader paid back by the nut graf?

**Check:** Could you tell a reader, honestly, what they get for every demand you make?

## Check yourself

Q: What's the difference between Franzen's Contract and Status models?
A: In the Status model a novel is art whose value doesn't depend on readers' pleasure, and difficulty may signal seriousness. In the Contract model it's an agreement: the writer must give the reader something worth the time and effort.

Q: What did Franzen conclude about readers in "Why Bother?"?
A: That there was never a mass audience to lose: readers of serious fiction are a scattered community, and the novel matters by keeping faith with them, not by changing society.

Q: What does "the most purely autobiographical fiction requires pure invention" mean?
A: Transcribing your life rarely works. Invented stories, like Kafka's *The Metamorphosis*, can carry the author's emotional truth more exactly.

Q: Why does Franzen say the reader is "not a spectator"?
A: Because the writer shouldn't perform at the reader; the reader should be drawn in as a participant and a friend.

Q: What's the strongest objection to the Contract/Status framing?
A: That it's a false choice: difficult, experimental work can offer its own rewards and its own deal with readers, as Ben Marcus argued.
