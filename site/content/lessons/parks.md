## Who and when

Suzan-Lori Parks is an American playwright who studied at Mount Holyoke College, where James Baldwin, teaching a writing class, encouraged her to write plays. Her early works, such as *Imperceptible Mutabilities in the Third Kingdom* (1989), which won an Obie, *The Death of the Last Black Man in the Whole Entire World* (1990) and *The America Play* (1994), made her one of the most original voices in American theatre.

In 1995 she collected three of these plays in *The America Play and Other Works*, together with essays on her craft. The most famous is "Elements of Style" (printed there as "from Elements of Style"), a mix of manifesto, glossary and advice that explains the unusual look of her scripts.

In 2002 *Topdog/Underdog* won the Pulitzer Prize for Drama; she was the first African American woman to win it. Her other work includes *Venus* (1996), *In the Blood* (1999), *365 Days/365 Plays* (writing a play every day for a year) and *Father Comes Home from the Wars* (2014).

## The big ideas

### Rep & Rev

Parks's central idea is **Rep & Rev**: **repetition and revision**. She takes it from jazz, where a musician states a phrase and returns to it again and again, changing it a little each time. The meaning lives in the variations.

In her plays, words, phrases, gestures and whole scenes come back, slightly altered. Characters repeat a line and it means something different the third time. The audience feels the play less as a line climbing to a climax and more as a pattern that deepens: each return adds weight. Parks contrasts this with the conventional drama in which events march from A to B to C to a resolution.

Many scholars connect Rep & Rev with Henry Louis Gates Jr.'s account of "signifyin(g)" in African American literature, repetition with a difference, and with the call-and-response and riffing of Black music. Parks presents it as an aesthetic of her own, rooted in those traditions.

### Why repeat?

Repetition is often treated as a flaw in a script. Parks argues the opposite. Rep & Rev lets a play:

- **Build like music**, with meaning accumulating rather than being revealed once.
- **Work like memory and history**, which are themselves made of retellings, each one revising the last.
- **Make language physical.** Actors have to find what's different in each repetition, so the words become events.

History matters here. Parks has written about using theatre to make history: to dig up and stage what the written record left out, especially Black American lives. Rep & Rev suits that project, because history in her plays is something told over and over, never fixed.

### A notation for the stage

"Elements of Style" also explains her unusual script notation, which she treats something like a musical score:

- **(Rest):** a pause. Take a little time, a breath, a moment of transition. It's a beat the actor has to fill.
- **The spell:** the names of two or more characters stacked on the page and repeated with no dialogue, something like LINCOLN, BOOTH, LINCOLN, BOOTH, each on its own line.

A spell, in Parks's description, is a longer and more heightened rest: a moment where the characters' surface falls away and they simply exist with each other. There are no words, but a great deal happens.

She also uses non-standard spellings to capture speech, footnotes in scripts, and passages meant to be read differently from how they look. The point throughout is that the script is a score for performance, not a novel in dialogue.

### Topdog/Underdog

Two brothers, named **Lincoln** and **Booth** by their father as a joke, share a cramped room. Lincoln, once a hustler at three-card monte, now works in an arcade dressed as Abraham Lincoln, where customers pay to pretend to shoot him. Booth wants to learn the card game and become the master hustler his brother used to be.

From the moment you hear the names, you know how it might end, and the play uses that knowledge. Card patter, stories about their parents and arguments over a small inheritance come back again and again, revised each time. *Topdog/Underdog* is one of her most conventionally built plays, with two characters, one room and rising tension, yet it is still made of repetitions. It also revises an earlier work: *The America Play* features the Foundling Father, a Black man who resembles Lincoln and lets people re-enact the assassination.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Find a line that matters and bring it back three times in different circumstances. Make each return change its meaning. Try a spell where your characters most need to say something and can't.
- **Screen:** Rep & Rev is everywhere in film and television that rewards rewatching: recurring images, a phrase that returns in a new light, a scene replayed from a different point of view. Write the variation, not just the echo.
- **Journalism:** In narrative nonfiction and audio, a refrain (a phrase a source repeats, an image you return to) can hold a long piece together. Each return should add new information or a new angle; otherwise it's padding.

## The pushback

- **Difficult on the page.** Readers and some critics have found her early plays opaque. The notation can look like an obstacle until you hear it performed.
- **Hard to imitate.** Copying the surface, such as rests and odd spellings, without the underlying music produces mannerism, not Rep & Rev.
- **Not only non-linear.** Parks herself moves between modes. *Topdog/Underdog* shows that Rep & Rev can live inside a tight, linear drama, so it's a tool rather than a rejection of story.
- **Readings vary.** Scholars disagree about how far her work should be read through jazz, history or politics. She has resisted being pinned to any one message.

- Then read *Paula Vogel* and *Sarah Ruhl* for other writers questioning the arc.

## Check yourself

Q: What does Rep & Rev stand for, and where does Parks take it from?
A: Repetition and revision. She takes it from jazz, where a phrase returns again and again, changed slightly each time.

Q: How does a Rep & Rev play feel different from a conventional arc?
A: Meaning accumulates through returns and variations, rather than building in a straight line from event to event toward a climax.

Q: What is a spell in Parks's notation?
A: Characters' names repeated with no dialogue: a heightened, extended rest where something passes between them without words.

Q: What does (Rest) tell an actor?
A: To take a pause, a little time, a breath or transition, which the actor has to fill.

Q: How does *Topdog/Underdog* use repetition while still being a tightly built two-hander?
A: Card patter, stories and arguments return again and again in revised forms within a linear, escalating plot, and the brothers' names set up an ending the audience can't stop anticipating.
