## Where it comes from

Audiences make up their minds about people fast, in fiction as in life. The first thing a character does, says, or has said about them becomes the lens for everything after. Writers in every medium have learned to use that first impression on purpose.

There's no single theorist here. The craft comes from practice: the conventions of screenplay format, the habits of playwrights from Shakespeare and Molière onward, and the magazine profile writers of the twentieth century. The best teachers are the famous entrances themselves, so this lesson leans on examples you can watch, read and study.

## The big ideas

### The first action is a thesis

The most useful rule is simple: **introduce a character doing something only they would do**. Not doing something neutral, like walking into a room, but acting in a way that sums up who they are. Their first action is a thesis statement the rest of the story will test.

The first impression doesn't have to be the whole truth. Often the best introductions show one side clearly so the story can reveal the other later, or show who someone is *now* so we can measure how far they change.

### The screenplay introduction

Screenplays have a convention for this. The first time a character appears, their name is written in CAPITALS, often with their age, and followed by a short description. Readers in the industry look hard at these lines, because they show whether the writer knows who this person is.

A good introduction line does three things:

- **It gives the essence, not an inventory.** Hair colour and height matter less than the one thing that defines them. Leave room for casting.
- **It shows attitude.** How they carry themselves, what they're doing, how they treat the people around them.
- **It's quickly followed by a defining action.** The description promises; the first action delivers.

### Indiana Jones: mystery, then competence

*Raiders of the Lost Ark* (1981) opens in a South American jungle in 1936. A man in a battered fedora and leather jacket leads a group of guides, but the camera keeps him in shadow or shows him from behind. We don't see his face. Then one of the guides draws a gun on him. A crack of the bullwhip knocks the gun from the man's hand, and only then does Harrison Ford step into the light.

Within the first few minutes we know he's dangerous, calm and very good at what he does. The temple sequence that follows, with its traps, the golden idol and the rolling boulder, confirms it. Then the film shows its second card. We cut to a college lecture hall, where the same man, in glasses and a tweed jacket, is a slightly awkward professor. Two contrasting introductions give us the whole character: adventurer and academic.

### Hannibal Lecter: talked about first, then revealed

*The Silence of the Lambs* (1991), from Thomas Harris's 1988 novel, builds its villain's entrance mostly out of other people's talk. Before Clarice Starling meets Lecter, her boss and then the hospital director warn her. Don't get close to the glass. Don't hand him anything except soft paper. The director shows her a photo of a nurse Lecter attacked.

Then Clarice walks a long, dim corridor past other cells and their shouting occupants. At the end, Lecter is standing perfectly still in the middle of his cell, behind a wall of glass rather than bars, neat and composed, as if he'd been expecting her. He is courteous. After all the warnings, his calm is more frightening than any snarl. The build-up created a monster in our heads, and the actual man is something stranger.

### The delayed entrance

The stage has long known the power of keeping a character offstage. Molière's *Tartuffe* (final version 1669) spends its first two acts with a household arguing about Tartuffe: the father worships him as a holy man, nearly everyone else calls him a fraud. He doesn't appear until Act 3. When he does, his first line is to call offstage to his servant to put away his hair shirt and scourge, a loud show of piety for anyone listening. Moments later he hands the maid a handkerchief to cover her bosom. By the time he enters, we're desperate to judge him, and he shows us both his hypocrisy and his lust in two gestures.

*The Third Man* (1949), written by Graham Greene and directed by Carol Reed, takes the delayed entrance further. Holly Martins arrives in postwar Vienna to visit his friend Harry Lime and is told Lime is dead. For about an hour the film is all talk about Harry: what he was like, how he died, who was there. Then, at night, a cat rubs against the shoes of a man in a dark doorway. A light snaps on in a window across the street, and Orson Welles's face appears, with a small, knowing smile. The audience has built a Harry Lime in their heads, and the real one has been alive all along.

Samuel Beckett's *Waiting for Godot* (1953) takes the idea to its end: the title character is discussed throughout and never appears.

### Others' talk as introduction

The Lecter, Tartuffe and Lime examples share a technique: **let other characters introduce someone before they arrive**. It builds expectation, it can mislead, and it lets the writer set up a contrast between reputation and reality. The gap between what we were told and what we see is often the point.

### The quiet introduction

Not every introduction is a flourish. In *Hamlet*, the prince first appears in Act 1, scene 2, in mourning black among a court celebrating his uncle's new marriage. He stays silent while the king does business, and his first line is a bitter aside, a pun on his uncle's claim to be both kin and kind. One costume and one muttered line set him against the whole room.

*The Godfather* (1972) introduces Michael Corleone at his sister's wedding, sitting apart with his girlfriend in his army uniform, explaining his family's crimes to her and insisting he's not like them. It's a modest introduction, and it's a setup: the whole film measures how far he travels from it.

## Use it

- **Stage:** The entrance is one of theatre's great tools. Decide whether the audience should meet a character cold, or hear about them first. A delayed entrance needs the talk beforehand to argue, so the audience arrives with a question. Give the first line work to do.
- **Screen:** Write the introduction line as essence plus attitude, in a sentence or two. Then check the character's first action: could anyone else in the story do it? If so, it isn't defining them.
- **Journalism:** The profile lede is a character introduction. Start with the subject doing something characteristic, in a scene, rather than with their résumé. Gay Talese's "Frank Sinatra Has a Cold" (*Esquire*, 1966) opens with Sinatra silent in a bar, a drink in one hand and a cigarette in the other, in a sour mood. Talese never got his interview; he built the portrait from watching Sinatra and talking to the people around him, a reporting version of introducing someone through others' talk.

## The pushback

- **First impressions can be too neat.** A character who perfectly embodies their first action from then on is predictable. The best introductions leave room to surprise, as Forster's round characters do (see *E. M. Forster*).
- **Introduction lines become showing off.** Some screenplay introductions are so jokey or writerly that they read like the author performing. They should serve the reader's picture of the person.
- **Delayed entrances can disappoint.** If the build-up is enormous, the character has to live up to it. Lecter and Lime do; many don't.
- **In journalism, a scene lede can distort.** A single vivid moment chosen to open a profile can define a real person unfairly. Make sure it's typical, or say that it isn't.

## Try it

Take your protagonist and one other important character from your draft, or two characters from a film you know.

1. Write each one's first action in a sentence. Ask: could anyone else in the story do this? If yes, replace it with something only they would do.
2. Write a two-line introduction for each: essence and attitude, no inventory.
3. For one of them, try a delayed entrance. Write a short scene where others talk about them first, disagreeing, so the audience arrives with a question.
4. Decide what the first impression hides, and note the later scene where it's revealed.

- **Stage:** Write the delayed character's first line so it confirms or overturns everything said about them.
- **Screen:** Do it within a page: introduction line, then a defining action.
- **Journalism:** Rewrite a profile lede to open on your subject doing something characteristic, with no résumé in the first paragraph.

**Check:** If the reader saw only this first moment, could they guess who this person is, and still be surprised later?

## Check yourself

Q: What's the basic rule for a character's first appearance?
A: Show them doing something only they would do, an action that sums up who they are.

Q: What makes a good screenplay introduction line?
A: The essence rather than an inventory of looks, the character's attitude, and a defining action soon after.

Q: How does *The Silence of the Lambs* build Lecter's entrance?
A: Mostly through warnings from other characters, then a long walk to his cell, where his calm, courteous stillness turns out to be more frightening than the build-up.

Q: When does Tartuffe first appear, and what does the delay achieve?
A: Not until Act 3. The household's arguments about him make the audience eager to judge him, and his first gestures expose him at once.

Q: How can a profile lede work like a character introduction?
A: By opening on the subject doing something characteristic in a scene, rather than with their résumé.
