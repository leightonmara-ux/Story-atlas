## Who and when

Charles Baxter is an American novelist, short-story writer and teacher, best known for the novel *The Feast of Love* (2000) and for decades of teaching in MFA programs. He's a writer's writer: quiet, precise, interested in the moral life of ordinary Midwestern people.

***Burning Down the House: Essays on Fiction*** (1997) collects his craft essays, many of which began as lectures to writing students. It's become one of the standard books on fiction writing, not because it offers a formula but because it diagnoses habits in contemporary fiction and asks what they say about the culture. Two essays in particular push against the conventional story: "Dysfunctional Narratives" and "Against Epiphanies".

## The big ideas

### "Mistakes were made"

"Dysfunctional Narratives" opens the book. Baxter starts not with fiction but with politics: the phrase "mistakes were made", associated with Richard Nixon and the Watergate era and repeated by public figures since. The sentence is grammatically clever. It admits a mistake while hiding who made it.

Baxter argues that this habit of **deniability**, of events without agents, had seeped into the culture, and from there into stories.

### The dysfunctional narrative

His term isn't about stories *about* dysfunctional families. It describes a way of building a story. In a dysfunctional narrative:

- **Things happen to the protagonist** rather than being done by them. The central character is acted upon.
- **Responsibility is somewhere else.** The cause of the character's situation lies in the past, in a parent, in a system, in an injury. The present-tense story mostly displays the damage.
- **The climax is a recognition of grievance**, an accusation or a revelation of what was done, rather than a choice or an action.
- **Nobody makes a mistake** that they then have to own.

The result, Baxter suggests, is fiction that feels static and oddly airless, however painful its material. When all causes lie outside the character, the story can't move, because the character can't.

### The interesting mistake

Baxter's alternative is not heroics. It's **agency**, including the agency to get things wrong. Characters who make choices, make mistakes, and then have to live with and acknowledge them are, in his view, the life of fiction. Owning a mistake is how a character becomes someone, and how a reader learns who they are.

This connects to an old idea. Aristotle's *hamartia* is an error a character makes, not something done to them (see *Aristotle*). Baxter is, in part, defending the moral seriousness of that kind of story against a culture of excuse.

### What he isn't saying

It's easy to misread the essay as "stop writing about victims". Baxter's concern is narrower. He doesn't deny that real harm happens or that it deserves stories. His target is a **narrative habit** in which the character's agency disappears and the story becomes an exhibit of injury. A story can be about terrible harm and still give its people choices.

### Against epiphanies

In "Against Epiphanies" Baxter takes on the most familiar ending in the modern short story: the sudden moment of insight, the device James Joyce named and made famous, in which a character (and reader) suddenly *sees*.

Baxter questions whether the epiphany had become a reflex. Too many stories, he suggests, end with an insight laid on in the final paragraph to supply meaning, as if understanding were the same as change. He's also wary of the epiphany as a cultural habit: the conviction that a single flash of revelation can explain a life, which in the real world is just as often wrong as right. He encourages endings that stay with action, consequence or mystery instead of announcing what it all meant.

### Other tools in the book

The collection is also full of practical craft. "Rhyming Action", for example, looks at how events in a story echo each other, so that a later action recalls an earlier one and the repetition creates meaning. Baxter's later book ***The Art of Subtext*** (2007) continues this line: what's left unsaid is often where a story lives.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Ask of every major character: what do they *do*, and what do they get wrong? A play built on characters reporting old injuries to each other is Baxter's dysfunctional narrative in its purest form. Give them a present-tense choice.
- **Screen:** Screenwriting teachers say "make your protagonist active". Baxter gives the reason a moral dimension: passivity isn't just dull, it's a way of evading responsibility. Watch for climaxes that are confessions rather than decisions.
- **Journalism:** The passive voice that hides agents ("shots were fired", "mistakes were made") is exactly what Baxter starts from. In narrative nonfiction, look for the people who made decisions, and resist structures in which institutions simply happen to the people in your story.

## The pushback

- **Trauma deserves form.** Critics argue that some experiences genuinely are things done to people, and that insisting on agency can blame victims or flatten survivors' stories. The debate continues; Parul Sehgal's essay "The Case Against the Trauma Plot" (*The New Yorker*, 2021) revived a related argument.
- **A cultural diagnosis is hard to prove.** Baxter's link between political deniability and literary passivity is suggestive, not demonstrated.
- **Epiphanies still work.** Many great stories end on a moment of insight. Baxter's objection is to the reflexive, unearned version, and readers sometimes quote him as if he opposed all of them.
- **Not a structural system.** Like Le Guin, Baxter diagnoses rather than prescribes. He won't give you a beat sheet.

## Read next

- Charles Baxter, *The Art of Subtext: Beyond Plot* (2007).

- James Joyce, *Dubliners* (1914), the stories that made the epiphany a model, to read alongside Baxter's critique.

## Try it

Take a scene from your work-in-progress where things happen *to* your protagonist, or where the climax is a confession or the recognition of an old harm.

1. Write one sentence naming who is responsible for the situation. If it's someone else, or the past, you may have a dysfunctional narrative.
2. Rewrite the scene so the protagonist makes a choice in the present, one that turns out to be a mistake.
3. Write a short next scene in which they have to live with it and own it.
4. If the piece ends on an epiphany, replace the final paragraph with an action or a consequence, and let the reader find the meaning.

- **Stage:** replace a speech that reports an old injury with a choice made in front of us.
- **Screen:** turn a climax that's a confession into a decision.
- **Journalism:** find each passive construction that hides who acted ("mistakes were made") and, where your reporting supports it, name the person who decided.

**Check:** Does your protagonist now do something they have to answer for?

## Check yourself

Q: What does Baxter mean by a "dysfunctional narrative"?
A: A way of building a story in which the protagonist is acted upon, responsibility lies elsewhere, and the story displays damage rather than showing choices. It's about structure, not subject matter.

Q: Why does he begin with "mistakes were made"?
A: The phrase admits error while hiding who made it. Baxter sees the same evasion of agency in much contemporary fiction.

Q: What does Baxter value instead?
A: Characters with agency who make choices, including mistakes, and then have to own them.

Q: What is his objection to epiphanies?
A: That the sudden-insight ending has become a reflex, supplying meaning in a last paragraph as if understanding were the same as change.

Q: What's a fair criticism of "Dysfunctional Narratives"?
A: Insisting on agency can seem to blame victims or deny that some experiences really are things done to people. And his cultural diagnosis is suggestive rather than proven.
