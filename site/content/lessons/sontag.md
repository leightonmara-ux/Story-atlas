## Who and when

Susan Sontag (1933–2004) was an American essayist, novelist and critic, and for forty years one of the most visible intellectuals in the United States. She wrote about film, theatre, photography, illness, war and fascist aesthetics, usually against whatever the educated consensus of the moment happened to be.

"**Against Interpretation**" is a short essay first published in 1964 and collected in *Against Interpretation and Other Essays* (1966). It made her name, along with "**Notes on Camp**" (also 1964), which treated a sensibility of exaggeration and artifice as worth taking seriously. She didn't coin "camp"; the word was already in use, and she credits a brief sketch of it in Christopher Isherwood's novel *The World in the Evening* (1954). Her later books ***On Photography*** (1977) and ***Regarding the Pain of Others*** (2003) are among the most important writing on how images of real events, including suffering, work on the people who look at them.

## The big ideas

### Interpretation as translation

Sontag's target isn't thinking about art. It's a particular habit: treating a work as a code whose real meaning lies somewhere behind it. The interpreter looks at the story and says "X is really Y": the tank is really a symbol, the hero's journey is really about the writer's father, the play is really about capitalism. Content becomes the essence, and form becomes mere packaging.

She traces the modern version of this habit to Marx and Freud, whose doctrines she describes as elaborate systems of interpretation, which taught readers that the surface always hides a truer, latent meaning. Applied to art, she argues, that habit becomes a way of taming it: once a work has been translated into a meaning, it can be filed away and no longer has to be felt.

### The examples she chose

Sontag points at specific cases. Interpreters had turned Kafka into an allegory machine, of social critique, of psychoanalysis, of religion. And she cites Elia Kazan's published notes on directing *A Streetcar Named Desire*, in which he decided the play was about the decline of Western civilization, with Stanley standing for a sensual, vulgar barbarism. Kazan's reading may be interesting, she suggests, but a production built on it is staging the interpretation, not the play.

### Show how it is what it is

Her alternative is a criticism that pays attention to form, to the experience of the work in time, to its surface. The critic's job, she writes, is to show *how* a work is what it is, even *that* it is what it is, rather than what it means. She values what she calls transparency: experiencing the thing itself, as it is, without hurrying past it to a message. The essay ends with its most famous line:

> In place of a hermeneutics we need an erotics of art.
> — Susan Sontag, "Against Interpretation" (1964)

"Erotics" here means sensuous, bodily response: seeing, hearing and feeling more, before (or instead of) decoding.

### A myth worth avoiding

The essay is often summarized as "Sontag said art has no meaning" or "never analyze anything." That's not quite it. She objected to interpretation that *replaces* the work with a paraphrase, especially in a culture she thought was overloaded with ideas and starved of sensation. She went on to write a great deal of careful analysis herself.

### Photography and the pain of others

In *On Photography* (1977) Sontag argued that photographs give the feeling of possessing the world while making it into something to be consumed. She also argued that repeated exposure to images of atrocity tends to blunt their force: the first shock fades, and the images become familiar.

In *Regarding the Pain of Others* (2003), written in the shadow of the wars of the 1990s and of September 11, she returned to that argument and partly took it back. She questioned the easy claim that audiences simply become numb, and argued that images of suffering still matter, even if they can't explain anything on their own: a photograph can haunt, but understanding has to come from narrative and argument. That later book also takes on the comfortable distance of the viewer, who can feel sympathetic without asking what connects them to the suffering shown.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Sontag's Kazan example is a warning for directors and dramaturgs. A concept production that translates a play into a single meaning can flatten what made it alive. Ask what the play *does* to an audience minute by minute before deciding what it's "about".
- **Screen:** Her essay sits in tension with theme-first craft advice. *Lajos Egri* wants a premise you could prove like a proposition; *Robert McKee* wants a controlling idea that the climax expresses. Sontag would say a film reduced to its controlling idea has been interpreted to death before it's shot. You don't have to choose sides, but notice when a theme statement is helping you build scenes and when it's replacing the experience with a slogan.
- **Journalism:** *On Photography* and *Regarding the Pain of Others* are essential reading for anyone choosing images of war, disaster or crime. Her later view suggests a practical rule: an image of suffering can command attention, but it needs words around it, context, cause, who is responsible, or it becomes spectacle.

## The pushback

- **Interpretation is unavoidable.** Critics answered that you can't experience a work without some interpretation: even noticing a form is a way of reading it. Sontag's "against" is better read as a polemic about emphasis than a program anyone could follow strictly.
- **The erotics was never spelled out.** The closing line is memorable precisely because it's undeveloped. What an "erotics of art" looks like in practice is left to the reader.
- **She changed her mind.** Sontag was candid that her views on photography shifted, and readers of *On Photography* should read the later book too. Some critics thought even the revised view underestimated what images can do politically.
- **Taste.** Her examples favor European modernism and avant-garde film, and some readers find the essay's confidence about what "we" need now more fashionable than argued.

## Try it

Take a short piece you love, a scene, a story or a feature, and describe it for 15 minutes without saying what it means. Only record what happens to you as you meet it: what you notice first, where your attention speeds up or snags, what you see, hear and feel, how long each moment lasts. Then take a piece of your own and do the same.

- **Stage:** Watch or read one scene of your play and note only what an audience would see and hear, beat by beat. Cut any stage direction that explains a meaning instead of showing a thing.
- **Screen:** Describe a sequence shot by shot, sounds included. Where does your script tell the reader what an image signifies?
- **Journalism:** Choose a news photograph of suffering. Write the caption it needs: who, where, why, who is responsible. Then ask what the image does that the caption can't.

**Check:** If someone stripped every theme statement from your notes, would the piece still give them an experience?

## Check yourself

Q: What kind of interpretation is Sontag against?
A: Interpretation that translates a work into a hidden meaning ("X is really Y"), treating content as the essence and form as packaging, so the work no longer has to be experienced.

Q: What does she propose instead?
A: Criticism that attends to form and to the experience of the work, showing how it is what it is rather than what it means. In her closing words, an erotics of art instead of a hermeneutics.

Q: Why does she cite Kazan's notes on *A Streetcar Named Desire*?
A: As an example of a director deciding what a play "really means" (Stanley as a kind of barbarism) and then staging that interpretation rather than the play.

Q: How did her view of images of suffering change between *On Photography* and *Regarding the Pain of Others*?
A: In 1977 she argued repeated exposure blunts their force. In 2003 she questioned the claim that viewers simply go numb, while insisting images need narrative and context to produce understanding.

Q: Did Sontag say art has no meaning?
A: No. She objected to interpretation that replaces the work with a paraphrase, not to thinking about art.
