## Where it comes from

Comedy has a famous hole at its origin. In the *Poetics*, Aristotle promises to discuss comedy, and the second book where he presumably did so is lost. (Umberto Eco built a murder mystery, *The Name of the Rose*, 1980, around a monastery hiding the last copy.) A short medieval Greek text, the *Tractatus Coislinianus*, has been argued to summarize it, most fully by Richard Janko in *Aristotle on Comedy* (1984), but that claim is disputed. What survives in the *Poetics* itself is a line or two: comedy imitates people worse than average, and the ridiculous is a kind of mistake or ugliness that causes no pain or harm.

So comedy theory has been assembled from other places. Three are especially useful to writers.

**Henri Bergson** (1859–1941), the French philosopher, published ***Laughter*** (*Le Rire*) in 1900, an essay on what makes things comic. The standard English translation, by Cloudesley Brereton and Fred Rothwell, appeared in 1911.

**Improvisational theatre**, developed in Chicago from the 1950s onward and later in groups such as the Upright Citizens Brigade, worked out practical rules for building comic scenes. Matt Besser, Ian Roberts and Matt Walsh set down the UCB's version in ***The Upright Citizens Brigade Comedy Improvisation Manual*** (2013).

**Sitcom and farce** writers, from Georges Feydeau in 1890s Paris to today's writers' rooms, built a craft of comic structure that nobody wrote down as a single theory but everybody learns.

## The big ideas

### The mechanical and the living

Bergson's central claim: we laugh when a living person behaves like a machine. His French formula is *du mécanique plaqué sur du vivant*, usually rendered as "something mechanical encrusted on the living." Life, for Bergson, is supple, alert and always adapting. When someone stops adapting, when habit, absent-mindedness or obsession makes them carry on as if by clockwork, they become funny.

His simplest example is a man running along the street who stumbles and falls. We laugh not at the fall but at the **rigidity**: his body kept going when it should have changed course. The absent-minded professor, the bureaucrat applying a rule to a situation it doesn't fit, the character who can only respond one way: all rigid.

Bergson adds that laughter needs a kind of momentary detachment. If we feel too much for the person who falls, we stop laughing. And laughter has a social job: it corrects rigidity by mocking it.

### Bergson's three devices

Looking at comic plots, Bergson picks out three structural tricks, all versions of the mechanical:

- **Repetition.** The same situation recurs, or a phrase returns in a new context. Life doesn't repeat itself exactly; machines do. This is the root of the running gag and the callback.
- **Inversion.** The roles flip: the robber is robbed, the prosecutor ends up in the dock, the child lectures the parent. The situation reverses as neatly as a mechanism turned around.
- **Reciprocal interference of series.** One situation belongs to two independent chains of events at once and can be read in two completely different ways. This is the engine of the misunderstanding: two characters have a whole conversation, each meaning something else.

### Yes, and

Improv's first rule is "**yes, and**": accept what your scene partner establishes, then add to it. It comes out of the improv tradition broadly, not one inventor. For writers, it's a lesson in commitment. A comic premise has to be accepted fully by the world of the scene. If everyone onstage stops to point out how absurd it is, the comedy collapses.

### The game of the scene

The UCB's key idea is the **game**: the first unusual thing in a scene, which then becomes a pattern the performers play out. A scene starts in **base reality**, an ordinary situation; something odd happens; and the performers identify that oddity and build on it.

They do this two ways. **Heightening** makes the unusual thing bigger each time. **Exploring** asks why it's happening. UCB teachers sum up the exploring question as "If this is true, what else is true?" The scene doesn't need a plot in the usual sense. It needs a pattern that escalates. Many sketch comedies are a single game played out and heightened three or four times.

### The sitcom's status quo

The classic sitcom is built to come back to where it started. Each episode disturbs the situation, usually through a character's flaw (vanity, greed, cowardice, a scheme) and then restores it, so next week can begin the same way. The flaw is the engine (see *Television structure*): because the characters never learn, they will make the same mistake in a new form next week.

The writers of *Seinfeld* made a rule of this, famously summed up as "no hugging, no learning." Many sitcoms soften it with a moment of warmth before the reset. But the shape, disturbance and return, is why comic characters can stay rigid for years, which is exactly Bergson's point.

### Farce

Farce is comedy as machinery. Georges Feydeau, whose plays include *A Flea in Her Ear* (1907), built plots of lies, mistaken identities, and characters who must not meet, all colliding in a single location with many doors. A principle often attributed to him is that when two characters must under no circumstances meet, the playwright should bring them together as soon as possible.

The mechanics are precise: every lie creates the need for another; every entrance threatens exposure; the pace rises until the system breaks. Michael Frayn's *Noises Off* (1982) and *Fawlty Towers* (1975, 1979) are modern masterclasses. Farce is also where Bergson's "reciprocal interference" lives, as one character's innocent remark lands as a confession in another's ears.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Farce needs a controlled space, doors, and a clock. Map who knows what in every scene, because the comedy lives in the gaps. Rehearse the timing as you would choreography.
- **Screen:** For sketches and sitcom scenes, name the game in a sentence, then heighten it at least three times. In sitcom, give each character a flaw strong enough to generate a new mistake every week.
- **Journalism:** Humor writing and comic features use the same devices: repetition and callback, the rigid official who keeps applying the rule, the reveal that inverts a situation. Played straight, the absurd detail does the work; the writer shouldn't nudge.

## The pushback

- **Bergson doesn't explain all laughter.** He set aside wordplay, joy and laughter between friends. Other theories cover different ground: the superiority theory (Hobbes's "sudden glory"), the incongruity theory (Kant, Schopenhauer), Freud's relief theory, and more recent work like Peter McGraw and Caleb Warren's "benign violation" (2010).
- **Laughter as punishment.** Bergson's idea that laughter corrects people who don't fit in has an uncomfortable side. Comedy that mocks rigidity can slide into mocking difference.
- **Rules make formula.** "Yes, and" and the game are teaching tools, not laws. Some of the best comedy comes from a character who refuses, or from scenes with no clean game.
- **Sitcoms change.** Many modern comedies let characters grow and carry arcs across a season, trading the strict reset for something closer to drama.

## Check yourself

Q: What makes something comic, for Bergson?
A: Rigidity: a living person behaving mechanically, as if by habit or clockwork, when life calls for alertness and adaptation.

Q: Name Bergson's three devices for comic plots.
A: Repetition, inversion, and the reciprocal interference of series, where one situation belongs to two independent chains of events and can be read two ways.

Q: What is the "game" of a scene in UCB improv?
A: The first unusual thing in an otherwise ordinary scene, which the performers then heighten and explore as a pattern.

Q: Why do classic sitcom characters stay the same?
A: Each episode disturbs and then restores the status quo, and the characters' flaws are the engine that generates next week's story. If they learned, the engine would stop.

Q: What's a farce principle often attributed to Feydeau?
A: When two characters must not meet, bring them together as soon as possible.
