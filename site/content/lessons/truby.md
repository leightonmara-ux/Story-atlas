## Who and when

John Truby is a story consultant and teacher who spent decades running a screenwriting class and advising film and television productions before he put his method in a book. *The Anatomy of Story: 22 Steps to Becoming a Master Storyteller* came out in 2007. It's written mainly for screenwriters, but Truby draws examples from novels, myths and plays as freely as from films, and novelists use it widely too.

The book is long and demanding. Where *Syd Field* gives you a simple shape and *Blake Snyder* a page-by-page checklist, Truby wants you to understand why a story grows the way it does, from the inside out.

## The big ideas

### Organic, not mechanical

Truby's central argument is against **three-act structure**. He calls it mechanical: a shape imposed on the story from outside, with acts and plot points that tell you *where* things happen but nothing about *what* should happen or *why*. In his view it pushes writers to think in terms of external events, and it leaves a vast, shapeless middle.

His alternative is **organic structure**. A story, he argues, should grow from a single seed, its **premise**, the way a plant grows from a seed. The structure is how a character changes, or fails to, and every event should be an expression of that change. Structure isn't something you pour the story into. It's what the story is.

### The seven key steps

Underneath every good story, Truby says, are at least seven steps. They are the minimum, and he presents them as grounded in how people actually act and change:

1. **Weakness and need.** The hero has a weakness that is holding them back, and a need: what they must fulfil to have a better life. The need usually means overcoming the weakness.
2. **Desire.** What the hero wants, a specific goal in the world. Desire drives the plot; need drives the change. The hero usually doesn't know their need at the start.
3. **Opponent.** The character who wants to stop the hero getting what they desire. Ideally the opponent competes with the hero for the *same* goal.
4. **Plan.** The guidelines or strategy the hero uses to defeat the opponent and reach the goal.
5. **Battle.** The final conflict between hero and opponent, which decides who gets the goal.
6. **Self-revelation.** Through the battle, the hero learns something fundamental about who they are, and sees past the weakness that was holding them back.
7. **New equilibrium.** Everything returns to normal, but the hero is now at a higher (or, in a tragedy, lower) level.

Truby then expands these seven into twenty-two, adding steps such as the **ghost** (an event from the past that still haunts the hero), the **apparent defeat** and the **visit to death**. But the seven are the nucleus, and he insists that you work them out before anything else.

### Desire versus need

The most practical idea in the book is the gap between desire and need. The hero chases something outside themselves (win the case, get the girl, catch the killer) while what they actually lack is inside (learn to trust, stop lying, stop using people). The plot is the pursuit of desire. The meaning is what the pursuit teaches about need.

Truby also splits need into **psychological** need (a flaw that hurts the hero) and **moral** need (a flaw that hurts others). The strongest stories, he argues, give the hero both.

### The moral argument

Truby thinks every story makes an argument about how to live, whether the writer intends it or not. The danger is preaching: a character who announces the theme. His answer is that the argument should be made through **structure**: through the hero's actions, their moral decisions, and the consequences those decisions bring.

Usually the hero acts immorally at times in pursuit of their desire, because they're losing. The opponent should represent a competing set of values, not just evil. The argument comes to a head in the battle and the self-revelation, where the audience sees which way of living holds up. Truby calls the ideal shape a moral argument the audience *feels* rather than hears.

### The web of characters

Truby's other major idea is that you shouldn't create characters one at a time. Each character is defined by how they **contrast** with the others, and above all with the hero. He calls this the **character web**.

His tool for it is **four-corner opposition**: instead of one hero against one opponent, imagine a hero, a main opponent and two secondary opponents, each in conflict with all of the others, and each representing a different set of values. The conflict then runs in many directions at once, and each character's values sharpen everyone else's.

Truby also treats minor characters as variations on the theme. Each one tries a different answer to the moral problem the hero faces, so the whole cast together makes the moral argument.

### The opponent is the key

If Truby had a single rule it might be this: the opponent is the most important character after the hero, and usually the one writers neglect. A great opponent is the person best able to attack the hero's weakness, wants the same thing the hero wants, and has values the audience can understand.

## Use it

- **Screen:** Run your outline through the seven steps. If you can't name the hero's need separately from their desire, or the opponent isn't fighting for the same goal, the story will feel thin however good the set pieces are.
- **Stage:** The character web suits ensemble plays especially well. Chekhov's and Ibsen's casts work like four-corner opposition, with several characters pursuing competing values in one room.
- **Journalism:** You can't invent a self-revelation for a real person, but you can listen for one. Ask your subject what they wanted at the start, what they learned, and who stood in their way. Those answers often map straight onto desire, need and opponent.

## The pushback

- **A system against systems.** Truby attacks three-act structure as mechanical, then offers seven steps and twenty-two more. Critics note that his model can be applied just as mechanically, and some readers find it harder to apply, not easier.
- **The moral frame isn't universal.** Self-revelation and moral growth suit some stories and not others. Counterpoint thinkers such as *Ursula K. Le Guin* and the tradition of *Kishōtenketsu* question whether a story must be organized around a hero's change at all.
- **The three-act straw man.** Defenders of three-act thinking reply that Truby attacks its crudest version. Writers like *Robert McKee* already counted acts by reversals and insisted on character change.
- **Long and dense.** The book is packed with lists, techniques and analyses. Many writers use it best as a reference for a specific problem, like a weak opponent, rather than reading straight through.

## Read next

- John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story: 22 Steps to Becoming a Master Storyteller* (2007).
- John Truby, *The Anatomy of Genres* (2022), which applies the approach to individual genres.
- *Robert McKee* and *Syd Field*, to compare with the models Truby argues against.

## Check yourself

Q: Name Truby's seven key steps in order.
A: Weakness and need, desire, opponent, plan, battle, self-revelation, new equilibrium.

Q: What's the difference between desire and need?
A: Desire is the hero's goal in the world, which drives the plot. Need is what they must overcome inside to have a better life, which drives their change. The hero usually doesn't know their need at the start.

Q: Why does Truby object to three-act structure?
A: He thinks it's mechanical: it says where events happen but not what or why, and it imposes a shape from outside instead of growing it from the character's change.

Q: What is four-corner opposition?
A: A hero, a main opponent and two secondary opponents, each in conflict with all the others and each standing for different values.

Q: How should a story make its moral argument?
A: Through structure, the hero's actions and moral choices and their consequences, rather than through characters stating the theme.
