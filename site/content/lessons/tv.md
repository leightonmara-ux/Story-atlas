## Where it comes from

Television structure wasn't designed by a theorist. It grew out of business conditions, and most of its vocabulary still shows it.

The serial came first, on radio. In the 1930s, daytime radio dramas sponsored by soap manufacturers, the original **soap operas**, kept listeners coming back with stories that never ended. Early American television borrowed from radio and the stage: in the 1950s, live **anthology dramas** presented a new self-contained play each week, written by playwrights such as Paddy Chayefsky (*Marty*, 1953) and Rod Serling.

Then came the series as we know it: the same characters, week after week, paid for by advertising. Two pressures shaped it. The commercial breaks had to fall somewhere, and the audience had to be held through them. And for decades, episodes needed to stand alone, so viewers could join anywhere and reruns could air in any order.

The rest of the history is a slow loosening. *Hill Street Blues* (1981) wove multiple storylines that ran across episodes. Cable dramas like *The Sopranos* (1999) dropped the commercial breaks and leaned into long arcs. Streaming services, releasing whole seasons at once, made serialization the norm for prestige drama.

The standard working text is Pamela Douglas's *Writing the TV Drama Series*, by a longtime writer and USC professor, revised several times (the fourth edition appeared in 2018) to keep up with these changes.

## The big ideas

### Teasers and cold opens

Most American series open with a short scene before the titles. In hour-long drama it's traditionally called the **teaser**; in comedy, the **cold open**. Its job is to grab the viewer before they reach for the remote. A drama teaser usually poses the episode's problem: a body is found, a patient collapses, a stranger knocks on the door. A comedy's cold open may just be a self-contained joke.

Many comedies also end with a **tag**, a short scene after the story is resolved.

### Act breaks and act-outs

On commercial television, the episode is divided into **acts** separated by advertising. Network hour dramas long used four acts plus a teaser; as networks added breaks, five and six became common. Half-hour comedies traditionally had two acts.

The moment just before each break is the **act-out**, and writers treat it as a small cliffhanger: a reveal, a reversal, a question. The viewer has to want to come back after the ads. This is why network drama has its distinctive pulse, with a turn every ten minutes or so, and why the biggest turn usually falls at the end of the act before the last.

Shows made without breaks, for cable or streaming, don't need act-outs, but many writers still outline in acts. The habit of building toward regular turns survives the commercials that created it.

### A, B and C stories

An episode rarely tells one story. Most interweave several:

- The **A story** carries the episode, gets the most scenes and usually belongs to the lead.
- The **B story** is a secondary plot, often for other characters.
- A **C story**, or **runner**, is a small thread, sometimes just a running joke across a few short scenes.

Cutting between storylines lets each one skip its dull stretches, and gives an ensemble cast something to do. The best episodes make the stories rhyme: the B story echoes or complicates the theme of the A story, so the episode adds up to more than three plots.

### The engine

The deepest question in series television is what keeps generating stories. Writers call it the series' **engine**. A film tells one story and ends; a series needs a situation that produces new stories for years.

Some engines are external. In a **procedural**, the story walks through the door each week: a new case for the detectives, a new patient for the doctors, a new client for the lawyers. Others are internal: a relationship that can't resolve, a lie that must be kept, a family that can't escape each other. The danger is resolving the engine. If the couple gets together or the secret comes out, the series may lose the thing that drove it.

### Episodic and serialized

An **episodic** show closes its main story each week, and characters stay mostly the same. A **serialized** show carries its stories across episodes, and characters can change profoundly. Most shows now sit somewhere between: a closed case or problem of the week, wrapped around serialized arcs. *The X-Files* made the split explicit, alternating standalone "monster of the week" episodes with ones advancing its ongoing "mythology".

### Season arcs

In serialized television, a season is a story in its own right, often with its own antagonist, midpoint and finale. *Buffy the Vampire Slayer* popularized the term "Big Bad" for the season-long villain. The same shape repeats at every scale (*John Yorke* calls this fractal): scene, episode, season, series.

### The pilot problem

The pilot is the hardest episode to write. It has to introduce the world and characters, make the engine visible, tell a satisfying story of its own, and convince a buyer that episode 2, and episode 50, will be just as good.

Writers often distinguish two approaches. A **premise pilot** shows the situation beginning: the new job, the move, the crime that starts everything. A **non-premise pilot** drops us into a world already running, like a typical episode. The first has a built-in story but may not resemble the series that follows; the second shows the series but must explain itself without a natural starting point.

## Use it

- **Screen:** Before writing a pilot, name your engine in a sentence and list a dozen episode ideas it generates. If you can't, you may have a film, not a series. Outline every episode with its act-outs marked, even for streaming.
- **Stage:** The act-out has a theater cousin: the curtain line before the interval, which has to bring the audience back. Interwoven A and B plots are as old as Shakespeare's double plots.
- **Journalism:** Serialized narrative journalism, from newspaper series to podcasts like *Serial* (2014), uses the same tools: an episode-ending reveal, a question that carries to next week, and a season-long question that may not resolve.

## The pushback

- **Commerce, not art.** Act-outs exist to sell advertising. Critics argue the manufactured cliffhanger every ten minutes distorts storytelling, and many writers welcomed its disappearance on cable and streaming.
- **The ten-hour movie.** Serialization has its own failure: seasons that feel like a stretched film, with saggy middle episodes that don't stand alone. Some critics have called for a return to the satisfying single episode.
- **An American model.** The A/B/C pattern and long seasons reflect American network practice. British series, for instance, often run six episodes, sometimes written by a single writer, and are structured more like novels.
- **Static by design.** An engine that must never resolve can trap characters in place. The most admired dramas often let change break the engine and build a new one.

## Check yourself

Q: What is an act-out, and why does it exist?
A: The turn or cliffhanger just before a commercial break. It exists to keep viewers watching through the ads.

Q: What's the difference between an A story and a C story?
A: The A story carries the episode and gets the most scenes. A C story, or runner, is a small thread, sometimes just a recurring joke.

Q: What is a series' "engine"?
A: The situation that keeps generating new stories, such as a case that walks through the door each week or a relationship that can't resolve.

Q: How do episodic and serialized storytelling differ?
A: Episodic shows close their main story each episode and keep characters mostly stable. Serialized shows carry stories across episodes and let characters change.

Q: What's the difference between a premise pilot and a non-premise pilot?
A: A premise pilot shows the series' situation beginning. A non-premise pilot drops the viewer into a world already running, like a typical episode.
