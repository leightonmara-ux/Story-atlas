## Why start here

Story theories seem to multiply endlessly: 12 stages, 15 beats, 8 steps, 22 steps. But most of them are different maps of the same territory. Learn the territory first and every map gets easier to read, and easier to argue with.

Here are six things nearly every theorist agrees on, from Aristotle to Pixar.

## 1. Desire meets resistance

Someone wants something, and something stands in the way. The names change: Aristotle talks about an *action*, Stanislavski about a character's *objective*, McKee about an *object of desire*, Jon Franklin about a *complication*. The idea doesn't.

Without a want, a story has no direction. Without an obstacle, it has no story: the character simply gets what they want and we stop caring.

## 2. Cause, not just sequence

Events should happen *because of* each other, not merely *after* each other. Aristotle made the point first. E. M. Forster gave it its most famous form in *Aspects of the Novel* (1927):

> "The king died and then the queen died" is a story. "The king died, and then the queen died of grief" is a plot.
> — E. M. Forster

The *South Park* creators Trey Parker and Matt Stone put it as a working test: if the beats of your outline are joined by "and then", you're in trouble. They should be joined by "therefore" or "but".

## 3. Escalation

Each complication should cost more than the last. Freytag drew it as a rising line, McKee calls it *progressive complications*, and screenwriters speak of "raising the stakes". A story that repeats the same level of pressure feels like it's going in circles, even if things keep happening.

## 4. A question held open

Audiences keep reading or watching because something unanswered pulls them forward: *Will she get out? Who did it? What happened to this town?* Hitchcock's famous example, from his interviews with François Truffaut: a bomb that suddenly goes off under a table gives you a few seconds of surprise. Show the audience the bomb first, and you get several minutes of suspense.

In journalism the same job is done by the *nut graf*, the paragraph near the top that tells readers what the story is about and why it's worth their time.

## 5. Change

Something must be different at the end. It can be:

- **A character:** they learn, break, harden or transform (the "arc").
- **A situation:** Aristotle's *change of fortune*, from good to bad or bad to good.
- **The audience's understanding:** common in journalism, mysteries and series television, where the hero may not change at all but we see the world differently.

## 6. Setup and payoff

What you plant early, you use later, and what you use later must have been planted. The best-known version is Chekhov's advice that a loaded gun shown on stage must eventually go off. (It appears in several versions in his letters and in friends' memories of him.) The reverse holds too: a payoff with no setup feels like a cheat.

## Where the agreement ends

The disagreements are where the real learning is, and later lessons take them one at a time:

- **How many acts?** Three (Syd Field), five (Freytag, following the classical play), or "as many as the story needs" (McKee).
- **Is conflict required?** Kishōtenketsu and Ursula K. Le Guin say no.
- **Must the hero change?** Many say yes, but plenty of great stories have a fixed hero and a changing world.
- **Rules or descriptions?** Aristotle described the plays he admired. Blake Snyder prescribes beats by page number. McKee insists his book is "about principles, not rules".

## Use it

Run any draft, whether play, script or feature, through six questions:

1. Who wants what, and what's in the way?
2. Read your outline aloud. Are the beats joined by "therefore" and "but", or by "and then"?
3. Does each complication cost more than the last?
4. What question is the audience holding at each point?
5. What is different at the end, and for whom?
6. Is everything you pay off planted, and does everything you plant pay off?

## Check yourself

Q: What is Forster's difference between a story and a plot?
A: A story is events in time order ("the king died and then the queen died"). A plot adds causation ("the queen died of grief"). Most theorists want plot.

Q: What's the "therefore / but" test?
A: Join the beats of your outline with words. If they connect with "and then", the events are just a sequence. "Therefore" and "but" show cause and complication.

Q: Name three kinds of change a story can deliver.
A: A change in a character, a change in their situation (fortune), or a change in the audience's understanding.

Q: According to Hitchcock, how do you turn surprise into suspense?
A: Give the audience information the characters don't have. Show them the bomb under the table before it goes off.
