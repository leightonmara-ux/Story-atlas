## Where it comes from

Drama has always been people talking, but most of what writers are taught about dialogue is recent. It comes from three directions.

The first is the rehearsal room. *Konstantin Stanislavski*'s work on Chekhov at the Moscow Art Theatre taught actors to look for what a character is *doing* with a line, and the word **subtext** came into wide use through it.

The second is the playwrights. Harold Pinter's plays of the late 1950s and 1960s, such as *The Birthday Party* (1958) and *The Homecoming* (1965), made silence a part of the script, and David Mamet's made speech a weapon.

The third is the screenwriting handbook, which has always warned against **on-the-nose** dialogue. The fullest single book on the subject is Robert McKee's *Dialogue: The Art of Verbal Action for Page, Stage, and Screen* (2016). Its subtitle is the lesson in a phrase.

## The big ideas

### Dialogue is action

People rarely talk to pass on information. They talk to get something: to seduce, to wound, to wriggle out, to win. That's the Stanislavski idea, carried into writing by *David Mamet* and McKee: every line is a move in a contest, aimed at the other person. If you can't say what a line is *doing*, it probably isn't doing anything.

In Mamet's *Glengarry Glen Ross* (1984), the salesmen are always selling, even to each other: flattering, bullying, lying, pleading.

### Subtext and what people avoid

The real scene is usually underneath the words (see *Anton Chekhov*). Two things push meaning under the surface: people protect themselves, and they often don't know what they feel.

The richest source of subtext is **what a character avoids saying**. In *Annie Hall* (1977), early in their romance, Alvy and Annie stand on her balcony with drinks making nervous small talk about photography, while subtitles show what each is really thinking: whether they sound shallow, whether the other likes them.

### On the nose

The opposite failure is dialogue that is **on the nose**: characters stating exactly what they feel and mean ("I'm angry because you never listened to me"). See the glossary entry. On-the-nose lines are inert because they leave the actor and the audience nothing to do. The fix is rarely to make the line cleverer. It's to give the character a reason not to say it, and something else to say instead.

### Voice and idiolect

Each person has an **idiolect**, a linguists' term (usually credited to Bernard Bloch, 1948) for the speech habits peculiar to one speaker: vocabulary, rhythm, sentence length, pet phrases, what they never say. A good test is to cover the character names on a page of your script. Can you still tell who's speaking?

Voice comes from the whole person: class, region, job, age and temperament (*Lajos Egri*'s three dimensions again). But voice is also choice under pressure. A character who usually jokes and suddenly speaks plainly has told us something.

### The speech in a crisis

Most good dialogue is short, but there's a place for the long speech: the moment pressure cracks a character open.

In *On the Waterfront* (1954), late in the film, the brothers Terry and Charley sit in the back of a taxi. Charley has been sent to bring Terry into line, and pulls a gun. Terry, instead of fighting, talks about the night Charley made him throw a fight for a gambling bet. The famous lines ("I coulda been a contender") aren't a summary of his feelings. They're an accusation, and they win: Charley lets him go, and it costs him his life.

*A Few Good Men* (1992) builds its climax on the opposite move. On the witness stand, Kaffee provokes Colonel Jessup until Jessup's contempt for him spills into a speech defending what men like him do, and in the heat of it he admits giving the order at the center of the case. The speech reveals character because the character can't stop himself making it.

### Overlap and rhythm

Real people interrupt and talk over each other. Howard Hawks's *His Girl Friday* (1940) was famous for rapid, overlapping exchanges. Robert Altman went further: on *California Split* (1974) and *Nashville* (1975) his sound team recorded actors on separate tracks, using radio microphones, so several conversations could run at once and be balanced in the mix. The result feels eavesdropped rather than performed.

Aaron Sorkin works the other way, with talk so rhythmic it's close to music: rapid exchanges, repeated words passed back and forth, long sentences landing on a hard final beat. In *The West Wing* (1999–2006), the "walk and talk", characters striding through corridors mid-argument, keeps that rhythm moving on screen. The opening scene of *The Social Network* (2010), a breakup in a crowded bar, shows both his strength and the risk: everyone is brilliant, so everyone can sound alike.

### Pinter's pauses

Pinter's scripts mark **pauses** and **silences** precisely. In his 1962 talk "Writing for the Theatre", he described two kinds of silence: one where nothing is said, and one where a torrent of language is used to keep the real thing hidden. Peter Hall, who directed many of his premieres, described how actors came to treat the three dots, "Pause" and "Silence" as rising degrees of pressure. In *The Homecoming*, the pauses are where power changes hands. Don't copy the marks; copy the idea that what isn't said can be the loudest thing in the room.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Read each scene aloud and mark what every line does to the other person. Use pauses sparingly and only where something shifts; an actor will treat every one as an event.
- **Screen:** Run the silent-film test from *David Mamet*. Then cover the names and check voices. Save the long speech for the moment the character cannot hold it in.
- **Journalism:** Quote for what only that person could say: opinion, emotion, voice. Paraphrase facts and figures. Place the quote after the reader has the context to feel it, and don't let it repeat the sentence before. On **cleaning up quotes**, newsrooms differ. Many style guides, the AP's among them, tell reporters not to alter quotations; when you must cut, use ellipses or paraphrase. In *Masson v. New Yorker Magazine* (1991), the US Supreme Court held that altering a quote is not libel unless it materially changes the meaning, but that's a legal floor, not an ethical standard (see *Janet Malcolm & John Hersey*). And be even-handed: tidying an official's grammar while printing a poor or immigrant source's every slip is a choice about whose dignity matters.

## The pushback

- **Subtext can become coyness.** Characters who never say anything directly can be as false as ones who say everything. In real crises, people sometimes do say exactly what they mean, and it lands hardest of all.
- **Style can swallow character.** Mamet-speak and Sorkin rhythms are so distinctive that critics complain every character sounds like the writer.
- **Realism isn't the goal.** Transcribed real speech is mostly dull and repetitive. Good dialogue is selected and shaped, which is why naturalistic-sounding scripts are some of the most worked-over.

## Try it

Take a two-person scene from your draft, ideally the one where the most is at stake.

1. In the margin beside each line, write what it does to the other person as a verb: *flatters*, *dodges*, *threatens*.
2. Find every line where a character states a feeling outright. Rewrite it so they talk about something else and the feeling leaks through.
3. Cover the character names. If you can't tell who's speaking, give each one a habit: a rhythm, a word, a thing they never say.
4. Find one moment where a silence would say more than a line, and cut the line.

- **Stage:** mark pauses only where power shifts, then read it aloud with a partner.
- **Screen:** cut the scene's dialogue by a third and see what the faces and actions now have to carry.
- **Journalism:** list every quote in your draft, mark each *voice* or *fact*, and paraphrase the facts. Check every quote you've tidied against your recording.

**Check:** Does every line do something to someone, and does the most important thing go unsaid?

## Check yourself

Q: What does it mean to say "dialogue is action"?
A: Every line is something a character does to another person (persuades, wounds, dodges) in pursuit of what they want, not just a way of passing on information.

Q: What is on-the-nose dialogue, and how do you fix it?
A: Dialogue in which characters say exactly what they think and feel. Give the character a reason not to say it, and something else to talk about instead.

Q: What is an idiolect?
A: The speech habits peculiar to one person: their words, rhythms and phrases. A strong cast should be recognizable with the names covered.

Q: How did Robert Altman achieve his overlapping dialogue?
A: His sound team recorded actors on separate tracks, with radio microphones, so several conversations could run at once and be balanced later in the mix.

Q: What did the Supreme Court decide in *Masson v. New Yorker Magazine*, and why isn't that the end of the matter?
A: That altering a quote isn't libel unless it materially changes the meaning. That's a legal minimum, not an ethical one; many newsrooms forbid altering quotes at all.
