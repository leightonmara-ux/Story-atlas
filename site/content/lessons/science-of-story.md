## Where it comes from

Since the 2000s, a wave of popular books has promised that science, especially evolutionary psychology and neuroscience, can explain why stories work and how to write better ones. Some of this research is solid. Some of it has been stretched far past what the studies showed. A writer who wants to use it needs to know the difference.

The main books:

- **Brian Boyd**, a literary scholar best known for his biography of Nabokov, wrote ***On the Origin of Stories: Evolution, Cognition, and Fiction*** (2009), a serious, long argument that storytelling is an evolved behavior.
- **Jonathan Gottschall**, an English scholar, wrote ***The Storytelling Animal: How Stories Make Us Human*** (2012), a lively popular version of similar ideas.
- **Lisa Cron**, a story consultant, wrote ***Wired for Story*** (2012), a craft book that uses brain science to back its advice.
- **Will Storr**, a British journalist, wrote ***The Science of Storytelling*** (2019), which draws on psychology to explain character and plot.

Behind the books sits a range of research: on narrative persuasion, on brains processing stories, and on hormones.

## The big ideas

### Well supported: stories are a human universal

Every known human culture tells stories. Children start making them up spontaneously. Nobody seriously disputes that storytelling is deeply human. What's debated is *why*.

Boyd argues that fiction is an **adaptation**, a kind of cognitive play. Like animal play, it lets us rehearse social situations safely, and it trains attention: storytellers compete for it, and audiences reward those who hold it. Gottschall uses a memorable image: stories as a **flight simulator** for social life, letting us practice dealing with problems we haven't faced yet. Storr emphasizes that the brain builds a model of the world and of other people, and that stories engage us when a character's model is flawed and gets tested.

These are reasonable hypotheses. But it's very hard to prove that a behavior evolved *for* a purpose rather than arising as a by-product of other abilities, such as language, imagination and interest in other people. Some scientists favor the by-product view. Evolutionary stories about storytelling are themselves stories; they're plausible, not proven.

### Well supported: narrative transportation

The most solid experimental work is on **narrative transportation**, a concept developed by the psychologists **Melanie Green** and **Timothy Brock** in a 2000 paper. Transportation is the experience of being absorbed in a story: attention, emotion and imagery all focused on the narrative world, so you partly lose track of your own.

Green and Brock built a questionnaire to measure it and found that readers who were more transported came away holding beliefs more consistent with the story, and were less likely to spot "false notes" in it. Later meta-analyses, which pool many studies, found that narratives do shift beliefs, attitudes and intentions, though the effects are generally modest. Stories persuade partly because absorbed readers argue back less.

That's useful to a writer, and also a warning, especially for journalists: a well-told story can carry claims past a reader's guard.

### Interesting but early: brains in sync

Brain-imaging studies have found that when someone listens to a story, activity in parts of their brain can come to follow the storyteller's. A well-known study by Greg Stephens, Lauren Silbert and Uri Hasson (2010) found this "neural coupling" was stronger when listeners understood the story better. It's fascinating work. It doesn't yet tell a writer how to structure a scene.

### Contested: fiction and empathy

A widely reported 2013 study in *Science*, by David Kidd and Emanuele Castano, found that reading a short passage of literary fiction briefly improved people's performance on tests of reading others' minds. Later attempts to replicate it largely failed. There is better, longer-term correlational evidence, from Keith Oatley, Raymond Mar and others, that people who read a lot of fiction tend to score somewhat higher on social understanding, but correlation leaves open which comes first.

### Overstated: the oxytocin story

The claim that stories work by releasing **oxytocin**, sometimes called the "trust hormone", comes mainly from the economist **Paul Zak**. In talks and in an often-shared 2014 *Harvard Business Review* article, he described experiments in which emotional stories raised oxytocin levels and made people more generous, and in which a dose of oxytocin increased donations.

The underlying science has run into trouble. A 2015 review by Gideon Nave, Colin Camerer and Michael McCullough looked at the evidence that oxytocin increases trust, including follow-ups to a famous 2005 study Zak co-authored, and concluded it wasn't robust: replications mostly failed to find the effect. Researchers have also questioned the reliability of the blood tests commonly used to measure oxytocin. The storytelling-specific findings haven't been settled by independent replication. That doesn't prove they're wrong, but it's far too weak a basis for "stories hack your brain chemistry."

### Popular claims

Craft books like *Wired for Story* translate research into confident advice: the brain craves stories, readers want to know what's at stake from the first sentence, and so on. Much of the advice is sound, because it's the same advice good editors have always given. The problem is the claim that neuroscience *proves* it. Studies rarely test craft choices directly, and leaping from "this brain region is active" to "start your novel this way" isn't supported.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Transportation research supports what theatre people already know: absorption depends on attention not being broken. It doesn't tell you to avoid Brechtian interruption; *Brecht* wanted viewers less transported, precisely so they'd argue back.
- **Screen:** Be skeptical of pitches and seminars promising a "scientifically proven" structure. The research supports broad truths, such as that people follow characters with goals and that emotion holds attention, not a beat sheet.
- **Journalism:** The persuasion research is a responsibility. Narrative lowers counterarguing, so a story can make a weak claim feel true. Use story to carry well-sourced facts, and be careful when citing "studies show" about the brain; check whether the study was replicated.

## The pushback

- **From scientists:** Many psychologists and neuroscientists object that popular storytelling books cherry-pick dramatic early findings and skip the failed replications. The broader "replication crisis" in psychology since the early 2010s makes single studies a weak foundation.
- **From humanists:** Some literary scholars argue that evolutionary explanations flatten the variety of stories across cultures, treating one familiar shape as natural. (Compare *Matthew Salesses* and *Kishōtenketsu*.)
- **From the other direction:** It would be an overcorrection to say the science shows nothing. Narrative persuasion is well documented, absorption is measurable, and storytelling's universality is real. The fair position: the broad claims hold up; the precise mechanisms and the craft prescriptions mostly don't yet.

## Check yourself

Q: What is narrative transportation, and what did Green and Brock find?
A: Being absorbed in a story. More transported readers held more story-consistent beliefs and noticed fewer false notes, so stories can persuade by lowering counterarguing.

Q: Is it proven that storytelling evolved as an adaptation?
A: No. It's a reasonable hypothesis, argued by Boyd and others, but it's hard to rule out that storytelling is a by-product of other abilities.

Q: What happened to the 2013 finding that literary fiction boosts mind-reading?
A: Later replication attempts largely failed. Correlational evidence that heavy fiction readers have somewhat better social understanding is more stable but can't show cause.

Q: Why should writers be cautious about the claim that stories release oxytocin?
A: The research behind oxytocin and trust hasn't held up well in replication, blood measures of oxytocin have been questioned, and the storytelling studies haven't been independently settled.

Q: What's the fair overall verdict on the science of story?
A: Broad claims, such as universality and narrative persuasion, are well supported. Specific brain mechanisms and "science-proven" craft rules mostly aren't.
