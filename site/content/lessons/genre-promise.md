## Where it comes from

Genre is older than any theory of it, but the idea of genre as a **promise** to an audience took shape in the study of popular fiction in the 1970s.

**John G. Cawelti** (1929–2023), an American scholar of popular culture, made the key move. In an essay of 1969 and then in ***Adventure, Mystery, and Romance: Formula Stories as Art and Popular Culture*** (1976), he argued that westerns, detective stories and romances are not failed literature but **formulas**: shared patterns that let a culture retell familiar stories with pleasure. Critics since have often described genre as a kind of contract between maker and audience.

The specialists came separately. The Bulgarian-French critic **Tzvetan Todorov** sorted detective fiction in "The Typology of Detective Fiction" (1966). The philosopher **Noël Carroll** analysed horror in ***The Philosophy of Horror*** (1990). The literary scholar **Pamela Regis** defined the romance novel in ***A Natural History of the Romance Novel*** (2003). And the term **film noir** came from French critics, beginning with **Nino Frank** in 1946.

## The big ideas

### Conventions and inventions

Cawelti's distinction is the one to keep. **Conventions** are the elements audience and writer both know in advance: the detective gathering the suspects, the couple who meet and clash, the crew assembled for the job. **Inventions** are what this writer alone brings: a new kind of detective, a barrier no couple has faced before.

Conventions give security; inventions give surprise. All convention is cliché; all invention, and the audience doesn't know what game it's playing. The promise is that the convention will be honoured; the pleasure lies in *how*. Romance readers know the couple will end together. They read to find out how two such people possibly could.

Break a core convention without warning and the audience feels cheated, not surprised. A romance that ends with the lovers apart is no longer a romance, whatever the cover says.

### Mystery, suspense, thriller

These three words get used loosely, but they name different engines.

| Type | The audience's question | What the audience knows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mystery | What happened? Who did it? | Less than the detective, or the same |
| Suspense | When will it happen, and will they stop it? | More than the characters |
| Thriller | Will the hero survive? | Roughly what the hero knows, in a race against danger |

Mystery runs on **curiosity**, looking back from effect to cause. Suspense runs on **anticipation**, looking forward from cause to effect. This is Hitchcock's bomb under the table from *The shared core*: hide the bomb and you get a few seconds of surprise; show it and you get minutes of suspense. Hitchcock told Truffaut he rarely made whodunits for this reason: a puzzle engages the mind, while suspense grips the feelings.

### Todorov's three detective stories

Todorov noticed that every detective story holds two stories: the **crime**, which has already happened, and the **investigation**, which we watch. How a book handles them makes three types.

- **The whodunit** keeps the crime story hidden and lets the investigation uncover it. The detective is safe; nothing much can happen to Poirot. (See *Edgar Allan Poe* for where it began, and *Story vs. telling* for the two-story idea in general.)
- **The thriller** (he was thinking of the French *série noire*, the hard-boiled novel) merges the two. The story is happening now, and the detective is in danger. Curiosity gives way to suspense.
- **The suspense novel** keeps both: a mystery about the past *and* a hero at risk in the present.

### Horror: the monster that shouldn't exist

Carroll defines the horror he studies, "art-horror", as fear mixed with **disgust**, aimed at a monster that is both dangerous and **impure**: it breaks our categories. It is dead yet alive, human yet animal, one thing yet many. Why seek out what disgusts us? Largely curiosity, he argues: horror plots are plots of proof, about discovering and confirming that the impossible thing is real. In his "complex discovery plot", the monster's onset is followed by discovery, confirmation (persuading the sceptics) and confrontation.

*Jaws* (1975) withholds its monster: for most of the film we see a fin, yellow barrels, a dragged swimmer. The first full look comes past the hour, as Brody ladles chum off the back of the boat and the shark rises behind him.

### Romance: courtship and its barrier

Regis defines the romance novel as the story of a courtship and betrothal, and names eight essential elements: **the society defined** (usually flawed), **the meeting**, **the barrier**, **the attraction**, **the declaration**, **the point of ritual death** (when union seems impossible), **the recognition** (of what removes the barrier) and **the betrothal**. She treats them as elements, not a fixed order; they can come in different sequences, and some recur.

*Pride and Prejudice* is her model. Darcy's first proposal is a declaration that fails because the barrier (his pride, her prejudice) still stands. Elizabeth's recognition comes as she rereads his letter and thinks, "Till this moment I never knew myself." The Romance Writers of America's working definition adds the promise readers rely on: an emotionally satisfying, optimistic ending.

### Noir: a mood with a name

In 1946, after the war had kept Hollywood films out of France, Nino Frank saw a batch at once, including *The Maltese Falcon*, *Double Indemnity* and *Laura*, and wrote about a new, darker kind of crime film. "Film noir" spread from French criticism, especially Raymond Borde and Étienne Chaumeton's *Panorama du film noir américain* (1955). The Hollywood makers didn't use the term.

Noir promises doom more than mystery: a flawed man, a desire, a city at night, a trap closing. *Double Indemnity* (1944) opens with Walter Neff, shot and bleeding, confessing into an office dictaphone: "I killed him for money and for a woman." The suspense isn't whether he did it, but how he walked into it. Whether noir is a genre at all is disputed: Paul Schrader's "Notes on Film Noir" (1972) called it a period and a style.

### The heist

The heist promises the pleasure of expertise. Its conventions are some of the most fixed in film: the job, recruiting the specialists, the plan, the execution, the thing that goes wrong, and often a twist showing the plan was cleverer than we were told. A widely noted rule: when the film shows us the whole plan in advance, expect it to fail; when it hides part, expect a reveal. Jules Dassin's *Rififi* (1955) shows the craft, a long break-in played in near silence; *Ocean's Eleven* (2001) plays the twist; *Reservoir Dogs* (1992) skips the heist entirely.

### Snyder cuts the other way

*Blake Snyder* argued that labels like thriller and romance are too vague to help, and sorted films by story problem instead. His categories cut across these genres: a horror film is usually a **Monster in the House**, a romance a **Buddy Love**, a detective story a **Whydunit**, and he files heists under **Golden Fleece**, a team on a road to a prize. The market genre tells you what you've promised; Snyder's type tells you what the story must do.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Genre plays (thrillers like *Deathtrap*, ghost stories like *The Woman in Black*) live on the promise as much as films do. Name the genre in the first scene's tone and keep it.
- **Screen:** List the conventions your genre's audience expects, then mark which ones you'll honour straight and where your invention lives. The trailer test: could a marketer cut one that tells the truth about your film?
- **Journalism:** True crime borrows Todorov's two stories (the crime, and the reporting) and must choose: mystery (hold the answer) or suspense (tell readers what's coming). Never manufacture a mystery the facts don't support.

## The pushback

- **Genres are moving targets.** Steve Neale and Rick Altman argued that genres are made and remade by studios, critics and audiences, not fixed boxes. Noir was named after its films were made.
- **Formula versus art.** Cawelti defended formula; others see genre as standardisation for the market. Many great works sit across genres, or betray one deliberately.
- **Regis's model is debated.** Some critics think fitting four centuries of novels to eight elements flattens real differences.

## Try it
Pick your work in progress, or a genre film you know well.
1. Name its genre in two words, then list five conventions its audience will expect.
2. For each, write "honour", "twist" or "drop". Allow yourself at most one "drop", and make sure it isn't the genre's core promise (the solution, the union, the survival).
3. Write one sentence naming your main invention: the thing no other story in this genre has.
4. Decide whether your central question is mystery (what happened?) or suspense (will it happen?), and check that the first ten pages set up that question.

- **Stage:** Find the one moment the audience will have paid for, the confrontation or the reveal, and make sure it's onstage.
- **Screen:** Write the logline so it states the genre's promise and your invention in one breath.
- **Journalism:** For a crime or investigation story, decide what readers should know before the characters do, and say why.

**Check:** Could a reader tell from your first scene what you've promised, and is your invention visible by the end of the first act?

## Check yourself

Q: What's Cawelti's distinction between conventions and inventions?
A: Conventions are the familiar elements writer and audience both know in advance; inventions are what this writer newly brings. Conventions give security, inventions surprise.

Q: How does mystery differ from suspense?
A: Mystery hides information and runs on curiosity about what already happened. Suspense gives the audience information, like Hitchcock's bomb, and runs on anticipation of what will happen.

Q: What are Todorov's three types of detective fiction?
A: The whodunit (the crime is hidden, the detective safe), the thriller (the story happens now and the detective is in danger), and the suspense novel (a mystery about the past plus danger in the present).

Q: What makes a horror monster, for Carroll?
A: It is both threatening and impure: it breaks our categories, like something dead yet alive. The fear is mixed with disgust.

Q: Where does the term "film noir" come from?
A: French critics, beginning with Nino Frank in 1946, after a batch of wartime Hollywood crime films reached Paris at once.
