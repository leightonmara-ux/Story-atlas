## Who and when

Robert McKee began teaching his *Story* seminar in 1984, first at the University of Southern California and then around the world. The book came out in 1997 and became the most influential screenwriting text of its era. McKee even appears as a character, played by Brian Cox, in the film *Adaptation* (2002).

He's often lumped in with formula writers, but he insists otherwise. The book's first line:

> Story is about principles, not rules.
> — Robert McKee, Story

## The big ideas

### Story values

A **value** is a quality of human experience that can swing from positive to negative: alive/dead, love/hate, freedom/slavery, truth/lie, loyalty/betrayal. McKee's central claim is that *meaning in a story is measured by changes in value*.

### Every scene must turn

A **scene**, for McKee, is an action through conflict, in more or less continuous time and space, that turns a value at stake in a character's life. If a scene starts at "hopeful" and ends at "hopeful", nothing has happened, however much talking went on. McKee calls it a **non-event**.

This is the most practical test in the book. Go through a draft and write, for each scene, the value at the start and the value at the end: *+ trust → − trust*. Any scene that doesn't change sign is a candidate for cutting.

### The building blocks

McKee builds structure from small units up:

- **Beat:** an exchange of action and reaction.
- **Scene:** a series of beats that turns a value.
- **Sequence:** a series of scenes (usually two to five) that ends in a bigger turn.
- **Act:** a series of sequences that peaks in a climactic scene with a major reversal.
- **Story:** a series of acts building to a final climax of absolute, irreversible change.

Notice there's no fixed number of acts. Three is common, but McKee counts acts by their major reversals, not by a template.

### The five-part design

{{diagram:mckee}}

1. **Inciting incident:** an event that radically upsets the balance of forces in the protagonist's life. It sparks a desire to restore balance, the *object of desire*.
2. **Progressive complications:** conflict escalates, with each turn going further than the last, to a point of no return.
3. **Crisis:** a true dilemma. The protagonist must choose between irreconcilable goods or the lesser of two evils. This decision, McKee argues, is where character is revealed.
4. **Climax:** the action that results from that choice. Absolute and irreversible.
5. **Resolution:** whatever is left. The aftermath, consequences, a final image.

### The gap

This is McKee's most original idea. A character takes an action expecting a certain reaction from the world. The world reacts differently, and a **gap** opens between expectation and result. The character must now take a riskier action, which opens a bigger gap. Progression is a series of widening gaps.

It explains why scenes in which characters simply get what they ask for feel dead, and why surprise and escalation are really the same thing seen from two sides.

### True character

> True character is revealed in the choices a human being makes under pressure.
> — Robert McKee, Story

**Characterization** is the surface: looks, job, speech, habits. **True character** is what someone chooses when the stakes are high. Great stories put the two in contrast: a meek clerk who turns out to be brave, a charming hero who turns out to be cruel.

### Archplot, miniplot, antiplot

McKee maps the range of story design as a triangle:

- **Archplot** (classical design): one active protagonist, external conflict, causality, linear time, a closed ending. Most Hollywood films.
- **Miniplot** (minimalism): a more passive protagonist, inner conflict, an open ending. Much art-house film and many plays.
- **Antiplot**: coincidence over cause, broken time, inconsistent realities. Godard and much of the avant-garde.

He treats all three as legitimate but is frank that archplot reaches the widest audience.

### The controlling idea

A finished story expresses one **controlling idea** that can be stated in a sentence: a value plus its cause. For example: "Love survives when we stop trying to control the people we love." You often discover it in revision rather than starting with it.

## Use it

- **Screen:** McKee's natural home. The value-turn test and the crisis decision are the two tools working screenwriters cite most often.
- **Stage:** The scene-turn test applies even more strongly on stage, where scenes are longer and talkier. Every scene still needs a change of sign.
- **Journalism:** The gap is a reporter's friend. Look for moments when a source's expectations collided with reality: the plan that backfired, the official who was sure. Those are your turns.

## The pushback

- **"Principles, not rules" in name only?** Critics argue that in practice the book is highly prescriptive, and that its influence helped standardize Hollywood scripts.
- **Conflict everywhere:** McKee's "nothing moves forward in a story except through conflict" is exactly what Kishōtenketsu and Ursula K. Le Guin push against.
- **The archplot bias:** miniplot and antiplot get a fair description but clearly second place.
- **It's a lot.** The book is dense and long. Many writers find it most useful as a diagnostic for a stuck draft, not as a way to start one.

## Read next

- Robert McKee, *Story: Substance, Structure, Style and the Principles of Screenwriting* (1997).
- Robert McKee, *Dialogue* (2016) and *Character* (2021) apply the same ideas to stage and page.
- Watch *Adaptation* (2002), a film that dramatizes a writer's fight with exactly these rules.

## Check yourself

Q: What makes a scene a "non-event" in McKee's terms?
A: The value at stake has the same charge at the end as at the start. Nothing has turned.

Q: List the five parts of McKee's design in order.
A: Inciting incident, progressive complications, crisis, climax, resolution.

Q: What is "the gap"?
A: The difference between what a character expects their action to produce and what the world actually does. Each gap forces a riskier action.

Q: What's the difference between characterization and true character?
A: Characterization is surface traits. True character is revealed by choices made under pressure.

Q: How does McKee decide how many acts a story has?
A: By counting the major reversals, the act climaxes, rather than following a fixed template.
