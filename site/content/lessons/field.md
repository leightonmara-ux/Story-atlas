## Who and when

Syd Field (1935–2013) didn't start out as a theorist. He worked in documentary television and then ran the story department at Cinemobile Systems, a Hollywood production company, where his job was reading scripts. He later said he read thousands of them and found only a handful worth recommending. He wanted to know what the good ones had in common.

His answer became *Screenplay: The Foundations of Screenwriting* (1979). It was the first widely read book to lay out a simple, page-counted structure for feature films, and it turned "three-act structure" into the industry's working vocabulary. Agents, executives and film students all learned to talk in Field's terms, and they still do.

## The big ideas

### A story told with pictures

Field's starting point is a definition. A screenplay, he writes, is a story told with pictures, in dialogue and description, and placed within the context of dramatic structure. Every part of that matters. *Pictures* means the page should describe what we will see and hear, not what characters think. *Dramatic structure* means the events have to be arranged so they lead somewhere.

He was also blunt about the format. One page of screenplay runs about a minute of screen time. So a 120-page script is roughly a two-hour film, and a page number tells you roughly where you are in the movie. That one rule of thumb is what makes the rest of his model possible.

### The paradigm

{{diagram:field}}

Field called his model **the paradigm**: a model or conceptual scheme, not a recipe. A feature has three acts:

- **Act I: Setup** (about pages 1–30). We meet the main character, learn the dramatic premise (what the story is about) and the situation (the circumstances around the action).
- **Act II: Confrontation** (about pages 30–90). The longest act. The character meets obstacle after obstacle on the way to what they need.
- **Act III: Resolution** (about pages 90–120). The story is resolved. Field is careful to say resolution means *solution*, not just "the end".

The acts are joined by **plot points**. Field defines a plot point as an incident or event that hooks into the action and spins it around in another direction. **Plot Point I** comes near the end of Act I (around page 25–27); **Plot Point II** near the end of Act II (around page 85–90). They aren't necessarily the most dramatic moments. Their job is to turn the story so the next act has something to do.

### The first ten pages

Field put great weight on the opening. In about the first ten pages, he argued, a reader should know who the main character is, what the story is about, and what situation the character is in. Readers like the ones he once supervised decide quickly. If the first ten pages wander, the script may never be finished.

### Know your ending

Field insisted you should know your ending before you start writing, at least in broad outline. Not every detail, but where the story is going. A plot point only works if it turns the story *toward* something, and you can't aim at a target you haven't chosen.

### Dramatic need

Field's characters are defined by **dramatic need**: what the main character wants to win, gain, get or achieve over the course of the story. Once you know that, drama comes from putting obstacles in its way. In his often-repeated summary, all drama is conflict, and without conflict you have no action.

He later added other qualities of strong characters, such as a clear point of view and attitude, and the capacity to change. But dramatic need is the root: it's what lets Act II be a *confrontation* rather than a series of events.

### Context and content

A useful distinction from Field: **context** is the space that holds something, **content** is what fills it. The paradigm is context. It tells you that something has to happen around page 25 to turn the story. It doesn't tell you what. That part is your content, and Field was clear that no structure model could supply it.

### The midpoint and the pinches

The long second act was the obvious weak spot. Readers and writers kept getting lost in sixty pages of confrontation. In *The Screenwriter's Workbook* (1984), Field described a discovery he'd made after *Screenplay* came out: many good films have a **midpoint**, an event around page 60 that splits Act II into two halves, each aimed at a goal.

In later work he added two **pinches**, smaller events roughly halfway between Plot Point I and the midpoint, and between the midpoint and Plot Point II, which keep the story on track. Together they give the second act a skeleton of its own.

## Use it

- **Screen:** Field's home ground. Even writers who reject his page numbers use his vocabulary: "What's your Plot Point I?" is still a normal development question. Try finding the two plot points and the midpoint in a film you know well, with a stopwatch.
- **Stage:** The page counts don't transfer, since plays run at very different speeds and often have two acts with an interval. But the idea of a plot point, an event that spins the action into a new direction and gives the next act its task, is useful for any act break.
- **Journalism:** A long feature has a structural problem much like Act II: a big middle that can sag. Look for a midpoint in your reporting, a discovery or turn that changes what the story is about, and build the middle around getting there and getting away from it.

## The pushback

- **Formula, whatever he called it.** Field said the paradigm was a form, not a formula. Critics answer that page numbers invite box-ticking, and that a generation of readers began checking page 25 for a plot point before asking whether the script was any good.
- **Old wine.** Field didn't invent the three-act idea. Playwrights and teachers had talked about beginnings, middles and ends since *Aristotle*, and Hollywood screenwriting manuals existed long before 1979. His contribution was a clear, measurable version for screenwriters.
- **Is it really three?** Film scholar *Kristin Thompson* studied Hollywood films and argued that most break into four roughly equal parts, not a 1–2–1 shape. Field's own midpoint, splitting Act II in two, quietly concedes much of her point.
- **The page count isn't the story.** *Robert McKee* counts acts by their major reversals, not by where they fall. *John Truby* goes further and calls three-act thinking mechanical. Most writers now treat Field's numbers as a rough check, not a rule.

## Read next

- Syd Field, *Screenplay: The Foundations of Screenwriting* (1979; revised many times).
- Syd Field, *The Screenwriter's Workbook* (1984), for the midpoint and practical exercises.
- William Goldman, *Adventures in the Screen Trade* (1983), a working screenwriter's view of the same industry, famous for "Nobody knows anything."
- *Kristin Thompson*, the lesson that tests Field's three acts against real films.

## Try it

Find Field's paradigm in a film you know well, with a stopwatch, and then in your own script, with page numbers.

1. Mark Plot Point I, the midpoint and Plot Point II, and note where each falls.
2. For each one, write a line: how does it spin the action in a new direction, and what task does it give the act that follows?
3. Check your first ten pages. By page 10, do we know the main character, what the story is about, and the situation they're in?
4. Write your hero's dramatic need in one sentence, then list the obstacles in Act II that stand in its way.

- **Stage:** ignore the page counts. Find the event before your interval that turns the action and gives the second act its job.
- **Screen:** if a plot point lands far from Field's pages, ask whether that's a choice or a symptom.
- **Journalism:** in a long feature, find the discovery that could serve as your midpoint, and build the middle around reaching it and moving on from it.

**Check:** For each plot point, can you say what the next act is now trying to do?

## Check yourself

Q: What is Field's "paradigm"?
A: His three-act model of a feature film: Setup, Confrontation and Resolution, roughly 30, 60 and 30 pages, joined by two plot points.

Q: How does Field define a plot point?
A: An incident or event that hooks into the action and spins it around in another direction, turning the story into the next act.

Q: Why does the page count matter in Field's system?
A: Because one page of screenplay runs about one minute of screen time, so page numbers tell you where in the film you are.

Q: What problem did the midpoint solve?
A: The long second act. An event around page 60 splits Act II into two halves, each with its own direction.

Q: What's the difference between context and content?
A: Context is the structure that holds the story, such as the paradigm. Content is what actually fills it, which only the writer can supply.
