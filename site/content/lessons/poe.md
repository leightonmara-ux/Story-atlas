## Who and when

Edgar Allan Poe published "The Philosophy of Composition" in *Graham's Magazine* in April 1846, a little over a year after "The Raven" had made him famous. The essay claims to show, step by step, how he wrote that poem: not in a fever of inspiration, he says, but as coolly and deliberately as someone solving a mathematical problem.

Poe was a poet, but he was also a working magazine editor and critic, and the author of some of the first detective stories, beginning with "The Murders in the Rue Morgue" (1841). He knew what it was to hold a reader for the length of one issue. Four years earlier, reviewing Nathaniel Hawthorne's *Twice-Told Tales* (1842), he had already set out the core of his theory of the short story. The 1846 essay applies the same thinking to a poem and states it more boldly.

It is short, confident and slightly theatrical. Whether it is an honest account of how "The Raven" was written is another question, and we'll come to it.

## The big ideas

### Begin with the end

Poe's first and most useful claim is that a writer should know the ending before writing anything else:

> Nothing is more clear than that every plot, worth the name, must be elaborated to its dénouement before anything be attempted with the pen.
> — Edgar Allan Poe, "The Philosophy of Composition"

Only with the ending always in view, he argues, can every incident and every shade of tone be made to point toward it. He says he began "The Raven" with its climactic stanza and wrote the rest to lead there.

This is the habit of the mystery writer. A detective story only works if the author knows who did it, and plants each clue for the reveal. Poe had written exactly such stories, and it shows.

### A single effect

Before choosing a plot, Poe says, choose the **effect**: the one impression you want to leave in the reader's mind. Then pick the incidents and the tone that will best produce it.

In the Hawthorne review he put it more sharply. A skilful writer doesn't invent events and then hope they add up to something. He decides on a "single effect" first, and if even the opening sentence doesn't serve it, he has failed at the first step. Everything that doesn't work toward the effect should go.

This is a different kind of unity from Aristotle's. *Aristotle* asks for one unified **action**. Poe asks for one unified **impression** on the audience. The question is not "is this one story?" but "does every part of this produce the same feeling?"

### Length: one sitting

The effect depends on reading the work at one sitting. Once a reader puts it down and the business of the world intervenes, Poe argues, the totality of the impression is destroyed.

So length is not a matter of taste but of effect. He goes so far as to say a long poem is really a series of short ones joined by dull passages, and he says he aimed "The Raven" at around a hundred lines. (It ended up at 108.) The same reasoning is why he championed the short tale over the novel.

### Every choice serves the effect

The rest of the essay walks through Poe's decisions, each justified by the effect he wanted:

- **Tone:** sadness, which he calls the most legitimate of poetic tones.
- **A refrain:** a single repeated word, whose meaning shifts each time it returns. He wanted a long *o* and an *r*, sonorous sounds, and so landed on "Nevermore".
- **A speaker for the refrain:** a creature that could repeat one word without reason. A parrot came to mind first, he says, then a raven, which suited the mood far better.
- **The subject:** the death of a beautiful woman, which he calls the most poetical topic in the world, voiced by her grieving lover.
- **Setting:** a closed room, because a tight space concentrates attention, "as a frame to a picture".

You don't have to share his taste to see the method. Every element is chosen backward from one intended feeling.

### Craft, not inspiration

Underneath it all is a provocation. Poe says most writers would rather the public believed they composed in a kind of fine frenzy, and would shudder to let anyone look behind the scenes. He offers to show the machinery instead. For writers who think structure kills art, he is the first great counter-example: a Romantic poet insisting that the effect was engineered.

## Use it

- **Stage:** The one-act play is Poe's form in theatre: a single setting, a short running time, everything bent toward one impression. His closed room is close to the logic of plays like *No Exit*, where the confinement is the point.
- **Screen:** Short films and tightly controlled genre pieces live by unity of effect. Thrillers and horror in particular reverse-engineer a final reveal and plant its clues. Ask of every scene: does this push toward the one feeling I want to leave? Twist films such as *The Usual Suspects* are written ending-first.
- **Journalism:** Know your kicker before you write. Feature writers often find the last line first and then work backward, so the whole piece is pointed at it. And Poe's "one sitting" is the question every editor asks about length: will a reader get to the end without putting it down?

## The pushback

- **Was it true?** Many readers, from his own century to ours, have suspected the essay is partly a performance: a rationalization written after the poem's success, or even half a joke at the expense of readers who wanted to know the secret. T. S. Eliot, in his 1948 lecture "From Poe to Valéry", doubted that "The Raven" was really composed that way. The French poets Baudelaire and Valéry, by contrast, took the method seriously and admired it.
- **Effect over meaning.** Poe cares about the reader's sensation, not about character, theme or truth. That suits horror and lyric; it suits a realist novel or a news feature far less.
- **Endings aren't always known.** Many writers, especially novelists, discover their ending by writing toward it. Journalists often can't know the ending until events deliver it, which is why *Jon Franklin* says to wait for the resolution rather than invent one.
- **The notorious line about beautiful dead women** has been criticized for turning a woman into a device for a man's grief.

## Try it

Work backward, Poe-style, on a short piece you're writing or about to start.

1. Write the single effect you want to leave in a few words: dread, tenderness, cold anger.
2. Write your last line, final image or final beat before anything else. If the draft exists, find it.
3. Go through the draft from the opening sentence on, marking each scene or paragraph: does it push toward that effect? Cut or bend the ones that don't, starting with the first sentence.

- **Stage:** for a one-act, test the setting too. Does your room concentrate the effect, the way Poe's closed chamber does?
- **Screen:** for a short film or a thriller, list every clue the ending needs and check that each one is planted, and planted early enough.
- **Journalism:** write your kicker first, then test your lede against it. Then ask the editor's question: would a reader finish this at one sitting?

**Check:** Does your opening sentence already point toward your last one?

## Check yourself

Q: What does Poe say a writer must work out before writing a single word?
A: The ending, the dénouement. Only then can every incident and tone be made to lead toward it.

Q: How is Poe's unity different from Aristotle's?
A: Aristotle asks for a unified action. Poe asks for a unified effect, one impression on the reader, and judges every element by whether it serves it.

Q: Why does Poe think length matters so much?
A: The effect depends on reading at one sitting. Once the reader stops, the world intrudes and the single impression is broken.

Q: According to the essay, why "Nevermore"?
A: He wanted a one-word refrain with sonorous sounds, a long *o* and an *r*, whose meaning could change with each repetition.

Q: Why do many critics doubt the essay?
A: It may be a rationalization written after the poem succeeded, or partly a performance. T. S. Eliot was among those who doubted "The Raven" was written that way.
