## Who and when

Sarah Ruhl (born 1974) is an American playwright and essayist. She studied at Brown University, where Paula Vogel was her teacher (see *Paula Vogel*), and received a MacArthur Fellowship in 2006. Her plays include *Eurydice* (2003), *The Clean House* (2004), *Dead Man's Cell Phone* (2007), *In the Next Room (or The Vibrator Play)* (2009), *Stage Kiss* (2011) and an adaptation of Virginia Woolf's *Orlando*. *The Clean House* and *In the Next Room* were Pulitzer Prize finalists.

*100 Essays I Don't Have Time to Write: On Umbrellas and Sword Fights, Parades and Dogs, Fire Alarms, Children, and Theater* came out in 2014. The essays are short, some only a paragraph, written, as she explains, in the gaps of life with small children. They are playful, aphoristic and often end with a question instead of an answer. Several of them take aim at things playwriting teachers usually treat as rules.

## The big ideas

### Against the compulsory arc

The book's sharpest structural argument is against the idea that every character must have an **arc**: a gradual, earned change, in which a character learns something and ends the play a better or wiser person.

Ruhl doesn't argue that arcs are bad. She argues they are **one option** that has become compulsory. She notes that Aristotle thought dramatic form was natural, and that he took the natural form to be an arc; since then, the arc has been treated as the only natural shape. She also observes that the questions workshops so often ask about a character, what has she learned, how has she changed, are moral questions as much as dramatic ones. American dramaturgy, she suggests, sometimes looks less like Aristotle's *Poetics* and more like *The Pilgrim's Progress*: a journey toward improvement.

She also asks, provocatively, whether the arc's familiar shape of rising tension, climax and release might reflect a cultural bias, perhaps a gendered one. She offers this as a question, not a proven claim.

### Ovid instead

Against the arc, Ruhl sets **Ovid**, the Roman poet whose *Metamorphoses* is a collection of transformations. In Ovid, people change suddenly and completely: Daphne becomes a laurel tree, Arachne becomes a spider. The change isn't earned through a sequence of lessons; it happens, and it is strange, beautiful or terrible.

An **Ovidian** play, as Ruhl describes it in her essay "On Ovid", takes pleasure in transformation itself, rather than in a character's moral improvement. Characters can become something else without the play having to justify it psychologically. Ruhl frames this as an addition to the range of plot forms her teacher Paula Vogel taught, not a replacement for them.

### Ruhl's plays as examples

Her own work shows what this looks like in practice:

- **Eurydice** retells the myth from Eurydice's side. In the underworld she meets her dead father, and a chorus of Stones comments on the action. The dead forget language after dipping in a river, so the most important changes happen to memory, not to moral understanding.
- **Orlando**, after Woolf, follows a character who lives for centuries and wakes one day as a woman. The change is a fact, not the climax of a lesson.
- **Dead Man's Cell Phone** starts when a woman picks up a stranger's ringing phone in a café, and the play follows the strange life that opens up from it.
- **The Clean House** mixes realism with fantasy, a maid who would rather invent the perfect joke than clean, and lets tone change as abruptly as the characters do.

### Questioning other rules

Other essays in the book apply the same skepticism to other workshop habits, such as the demand for a clear, single storyline and the insistence that drama needs conflict. Fighting can be dramatic, she allows, but it isn't the only way to make drama. Ruhl's general stance is that these are tools some plays need, not laws every play must obey, and that a writer should notice when a rule is being applied out of habit.

### Lightness

Ruhl's plays are known for their lightness: quick shifts of tone, sudden jokes inside grief, a light touch on serious matter. That tonal freedom is Ovidian too. Moods transform as suddenly as people do, and the play doesn't stop to justify it.

## Use it

- **Stage:** If a workshop asks "what does your character learn?", consider whether the play actually wants a lesson. Try letting a character change suddenly, physically or completely, and see whether the play gains wonder that a gradual arc couldn't give it.
- **Screen:** Studio notes often demand arcs. Ruhl's essays are a useful counterweight, and a reminder that fairy tales, comedies and many beloved films don't hinge on moral growth. But know the market you're writing for.
- **Journalism:** Real people rarely complete neat arcs, and pretending they do can falsify a story. Ruhl's skepticism is a good check: report the change that actually happened, even if it's sudden, partial or unexplained.

## The pushback

- **Arcs are popular for a reason.** Audiences often want to see someone earn a change, and critics of the Ovidian approach say it can feel arbitrary or emotionally weightless.
- **Whimsy?** Some reviewers have found her plays' lightness too sweet or too quirky. Others argue that the lightness is exactly what makes the grief bearable.
- **Essays, not a method.** *100 Essays* is deliberately fragmentary. It raises questions and offers alternatives, but it doesn't lay out a structure you can follow step by step, by design.
- **The gender question.** Her suggestion about the arc and gender is offered as a provocation and should be read that way.

## Read next

- Sarah Ruhl, *100 Essays I Don't Have Time to Write* (2014). Start with "On Ovid".
- Sarah Ruhl, *Eurydice* and *The Clean House*.
- Ovid, *Metamorphoses*. Try a modern verse translation.
- Ted Hughes, *Tales from Ovid* (1997), a vivid retelling of selected stories.
- Then read *Paula Vogel* for the plot forms Ruhl builds on.

## Try it

Take a character from your work-in-progress who you've been told needs to "learn something".

1. Write one sentence for the arc they're supposed to have: what they learn, how they improve.
2. Now write a short scene in which, instead, they change suddenly and completely, the Ovidian way. They become something else, in body or in nature, and the play doesn't explain it.
3. Write the next scene, in which the world simply carries on with the change as a fact. Let the tone shift as quickly as the character did.
4. Compare the two versions. Which gives the play more wonder, and which does it really need?

- **Stage:** let the transformation happen in view, through a costume, an object or a change of light.
- **Screen:** try it in a fairy-tale or comic register, and notice whether the film still wants a lesson.
- **Journalism:** look for a profile draft where you've given someone a neat arc, and rewrite the change as it actually happened, even if it was sudden, partial or unexplained.

**Check:** Does the change give pleasure in itself, without asking the character to have learned anything?

## Check yourself

Q: What is Ruhl's objection to the character arc?
A: Not that arcs are bad, but that they have become compulsory: one natural form treated as the only one, often carrying a moral expectation that characters must learn and improve.

Q: What does Ruhl mean by an Ovidian play?
A: One that takes pleasure in transformation itself, sudden and complete change as in Ovid's *Metamorphoses*, rather than in a character's gradual moral improvement.

Q: What does Ruhl suggest American dramaturgy is really based on?
A: Less on Aristotle's *Poetics* than on something like *The Pilgrim's Progress*, a journey of moral improvement.

Q: How does *Orlando* illustrate the Ovidian approach?
A: Orlando wakes up as a woman, a total change that the story simply accepts rather than presenting as the payoff of a lesson.

Q: How does Ruhl relate her Ovidian form to Paula Vogel's teaching?
A: She presents it as an addition to the range of plot forms Vogel taught, another option beyond the Aristotelian arc.
