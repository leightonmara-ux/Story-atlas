## Who and when

Frank Daniel (1926–1996) was one of the most influential film teachers of the twentieth century, though he never wrote a book on screenwriting. Born František Daniel in Czechoslovakia, he was dean of FAMU, the Prague film school, and left the country after the Soviet-led invasion of 1968. In the United States he taught at the American Film Institute, co-chaired the film program at Columbia University with his former student Miloš Forman, became the first artistic director of the Sundance Institute, and served as dean of the film school at the University of Southern California. Among the people he taught or advised were David Lynch and many Sundance filmmakers.

What he taught was passed on mostly by his students. One of them, Paul Gulino, who learned screenwriting from Daniel at USC, set out the approach in *Screenwriting: The Sequence Approach* (2004). David Howard, who taught alongside Daniel at USC, set out related tools in *The Tools of Screenwriting* (1993, with Edward Mabley).

## The big ideas

### The problem with the second act

Daniel's students had the same trouble everyone has with the three-act model: the second act. Sixty pages is a long way to walk with only "confrontation" as a guide. Writers set up their story well, knew their ending, and got lost in the middle.

Daniel's answer was to break the film into smaller units that a writer can actually hold in their head: **sequences**.

### Eight sequences

{{diagram:sequence}}

In the model, a typical feature of about two hours breaks into **eight sequences**, each roughly **10 to 15 minutes** long. They sit inside the familiar three acts:

- **Act I:** two sequences.
- **Act II:** four sequences.
- **Act III:** two sequences.

So the frightening sixty-page second act becomes four manageable chunks of about fifteen pages each. Gulino doesn't treat the number as a law, and some of the films he analyzes have fewer or more sequences. Eight is a typical pattern for a film of a certain length, not a target.

A common explanation for the size of a sequence goes back to early cinema, when a reel of film ran around ten to fifteen minutes and filmmakers shaped their stories in reel-sized units. It's a plausible bit of history, but the practical case for sequences doesn't depend on it.

### Each sequence is a little film

The key idea is that each sequence works like a short film of its own. It has its own:

- **Protagonist goal,** often a short-term step toward the larger one.
- **Tension,** a question the audience wants answered: will she get into the building? Will he tell her the truth?
- **Resolution,** which answers that question, often in a way that raises a new one and launches the next sequence.

This is what makes the approach so useful. A sequence isn't just a stretch of pages; it's a unit with its own beginning, middle and end, and its own pull on the audience. The tension of each sequence keeps people watching *now*, while the film's larger question keeps them watching to the end.

### Main tension and the dramatic question

Over the sequences sits the film's **main tension**, the overarching question that the story is about. It's often framed as a simple dramatic question, *will this character get what they want?*, and it's usually set up by the end of the first act.

Daniel is often credited with a famously short definition of drama that captures this: somebody wants something badly and is having difficulty getting it. The sequences are the stages of that difficulty.

### Hope and fear

Daniel's school of teaching, as passed on by Gulino and by Howard and Mabley, pays close attention to how films hold an audience moment to moment. Tension, in this view, is built from **hope and fear**: the audience hopes for one outcome and fears another. If they only hope, or only fear, there's no suspense.

These books also discuss **dramatic irony**, when the audience knows something a character doesn't, and **telegraphing**, letting the audience anticipate what's coming so they lean forward to see if it happens. These tools work inside each sequence as well as across the whole film.

## Use it

- **Screen:** Outline your film as eight sequence titles, each with its own question. If one sequence has no question of its own, that's probably where your draft sags. For television, the same thinking applies to acts between commercial breaks, each of which needs its own tension.
- **Stage:** Plays aren't built in reel-sized units, but long scenes and acts often fall into French scenes or movements that work the same way. Ask what question each movement poses and when it's answered.
- **Journalism:** Long-form features, podcasts and documentaries benefit most. A forty-minute audio episode or a 10,000-word feature is easier to plan as four or five sequences, each with its own question, than as one long middle.

## The pushback

- **Still three acts underneath.** The sequence approach doesn't challenge three-act structure; it subdivides it. Writers who find acts themselves too rigid, like *John Truby*, won't find the answer here.
- **Eight is a habit, not a rule.** Popular summaries sometimes present "eight-sequence structure" as fixed. Gulino doesn't, and the length of the film, the genre and the storytelling all change the count.
- **The reel story.** The link between sequences and reel length is plausible and widely repeated, but it's a historical explanation, not the reason the approach works. Treat it as background.
- **Secondhand Daniel.** Because Daniel didn't publish his method, what we know comes through students and colleagues. Their versions differ in emphasis, so "Frank Daniel's sequence approach" is partly Gulino's interpretation.

## Read next

- Paul Joseph Gulino, *Screenwriting: The Sequence Approach* (2004), which applies the method to a range of films.
- David Howard and Edward Mabley, *The Tools of Screenwriting* (1993), another book shaped by Daniel's teaching.
- *Kristin Thompson*, whose study of real films divides them into larger parts that sit comfortably alongside sequences.

## Check yourself

Q: Roughly how long is a sequence, and how many are in a typical two-hour feature?
A: About 10 to 15 minutes, with around eight in a typical feature: two in Act I, four in Act II and two in Act III.

Q: What does each sequence need to work on its own?
A: Its own goal, its own tension (a question the audience wants answered) and a resolution that usually sets up the next sequence.

Q: What problem was the sequence approach designed to solve?
A: The long, shapeless second act. Breaking it into four sequences gives writers manageable units with clear targets.

Q: What is "main tension"?
A: The overarching dramatic question of the whole film, usually set up by the end of Act I, which the sequences work toward answering.

Q: In this school of teaching, what two feelings make up tension?
A: Hope and fear: the audience hopes for one outcome and fears another.
