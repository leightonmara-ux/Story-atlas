## Where it comes from

The oldest division in drama was drawn in Athens. At the **City Dionysia**, a spring festival for the god Dionysus, playwrights competed with tragedies from the later sixth century BCE; comedy got its own official contest in 486 BCE. A tragic playwright entered three tragedies and a rowdy **satyr play**.

The words themselves are Greek. *Tragōidia* seems to mean "goat song", though nobody knows why, and every theory is disputed. *Kōmōidia* comes from *kōmos*, a drunken revel or procession.

**Old Comedy**, the plays of Aristophanes such as *Lysistrata* (411 BCE), was political and fantastical, mocking real Athenians by name. **New Comedy**, led by Menander a century later, was domestic: young lovers, angry fathers, clever slaves, lost children, mistaken identities, and a marriage at the end. The Romans Plautus and Terence adapted Menander's kind of play into Latin, and through them New Comedy reached the Renaissance. Shakespeare's *The Comedy of Errors* is a reworking of Plautus's *Menaechmi*.

**Aristotle**'s *Poetics* (c. 335 BCE) is the first theory of tragedy; see *Aristotle* for plot, reversal, recognition and catharsis. His book on comedy is lost, and *Comedy structure* tells that story. One distinction from the *Poetics* matters here: tragedy imitates people better than average, comedy people worse.

## The big ideas

### The rule of thumb: how it ends

The simplest way to tell the two apart is the ending. Comedy ends in **marriage or reunion**; tragedy ends in **death or separation**. Lord Byron put the classical rule in two lines:

> All tragedies are finish'd by a death,
> All comedies are ended by a marriage.
> — Lord Byron, *Don Juan*, Canto III (1821)

Under the rule sits a deeper difference. Comedy moves toward **inclusion**: separated people are brought together, a community is rebuilt, and even the obstacles are often forgiven. Tragedy moves toward **isolation**: one person is cut off from the world, by death, exile or knowledge no one else can bear.

The ending is only a rule of thumb: a comedy can be full of pain, provided the pain doesn't win.

### What each form promises

Every form makes a contract with its audience. Break it without reason and people feel cheated.

| Form | What it promises | What breaks the promise |
|---|---|---|
| **Tragedy** | Loss that means something; we'll understand why it had to happen | A death by accident, unconnected to choices |
| **Comedy** | Trouble that will be survived; the group will be put back together | Real, lasting harm to someone we love |
| **Tragicomedy** | Real danger, and a turn away from death at the last moment | An ending that feels like a cheat rather than a reprieve |
| **Dark comedy** | Laughter at things we're not supposed to laugh at | Laughter used to dodge the subject, not to look at it |

Watch the end of *Twelfth Night* (Act 5). The twins Viola and Sebastian, each believing the other drowned, finally stand onstage together, and the couples sort themselves out: comedy's reunion and marriage. But Malvolio, humiliated by the trick played on him, storms off swearing to be revenged on the whole pack of them. Comedy's inclusion has a limit, and Shakespeare lets us see who is left outside.

### Shakespeare's four drawers

When Shakespeare's colleagues collected his plays in the **First Folio** (1623), they sorted them into **Comedies, Histories, and Tragedies**. The history plays, about English kings, didn't fit either ancient category, so they got their own.

Two later labels came from critics, not from Shakespeare:

- **Romances.** The late plays (*Pericles*, *Cymbeline*, *The Winter's Tale*, *The Tempest*) mix loss, long separations, magic and miraculous reunions. Critics such as Edward Dowden in the 1870s popularized calling them romances. The Folio had printed *Cymbeline* among the tragedies.
- **Problem plays.** The critic **F. S. Boas**, in *Shakspere and His Predecessors* (1896), borrowed the term from the modern "problem play" of Ibsen's era for *All's Well That Ends Well*, *Measure for Measure*, *Troilus and Cressida* and *Hamlet*. They raise moral questions the endings don't settle. *Measure for Measure* ends in a cluster of marriages, but they feel more like sentences than celebrations.

### Tragicomedy

The word is older than the argument. **Plautus** coined *tragicomoedia* in the prologue to his *Amphitryon*: a play with gods and kings in it ought to be tragedy, but it has slaves and jokes too.

The real fight came in the Renaissance. Critics who followed Aristotle strictly said the forms must not mix. In England, Philip Sidney's *Defence of Poesy* (published 1595) scorned plays that mingle kings and clowns as "mongrel tragi-comedy." In Italy, **Giovanni Battista Guarini** wrote a pastoral tragicomedy, *Il pastor fido* (*The Faithful Shepherd*, published 1590), and defended it against attack, collecting his arguments in the *Compendio della poesia tragicomica* (1601). His case: tragicomedy is a third form in its own right, taking the danger from tragedy but not the death, and the pleasure from comedy but not its excess.

The English playwright **John Fletcher** gave the working definition in the preface to *The Faithful Shepherdess* (around 1609), in paraphrase: a tragicomedy has no deaths, which keeps it from being tragedy, but brings some characters near death, which keeps it from being comedy.

### Chekhov's argument with his director

Modern tragicomedy is less a plot shape than a tone. **Chekhov** insisted that *The Cherry Orchard* (1904) was a comedy, in places even a farce, and he complained that Stanislavski's production at the Moscow Art Theatre had turned it into a weepy drama (see *Anton Chekhov*). A family loses its estate, and the play ends to the sound of axes in the orchard. Both readings are in the text. That doubleness, where the audience isn't sure whether to laugh or grieve, is the modern form.

Samuel Beckett gave *Waiting for Godot* the subtitle "a tragicomedy in two acts" in its English edition: nothing happens, twice, and it's funny and desolate at once.

### Dark comedy and modern blends

**Black humour** (*humour noir*) was named by the surrealist André Breton in his *Anthologie de l'humour noir* (1940). Dark comedy laughs at death, war, cruelty and madness. Stanley Kubrick's *Dr. Strangelove* (1964) plays nuclear annihilation as farce.

Comic relief inside tragedy is old: in *Macbeth*, straight after Duncan's murder, a drunk porter jokes about being the porter of hell-gate while the knocking at the gate goes on (Act 2, Scene 3). Modern blends go further and switch contracts mid-story. Bong Joon-ho's *Parasite* (2019) plays its first half as a caper comedy about a poor family conning a rich one, and its birthday party climax turns into a bloodbath. Television has its own name for the half-hour blend: **dramedy**.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Decide which contract you're offering by the end of the first scene, and if you plan to break it, plant the possibility early. Chekhov's lesson: the same text can be played as comedy or tragedy, so tell your director which you mean, and why.
- **Screen:** Genre is a promise to the audience and to the marketing department. A tonal shift like *Parasite*'s works because the comedy was already about the thing that turns violent: class.
- **Journalism:** Stories have tragic and comic shapes too (see *Hayden White & Joan Didion*). A feature that ends in reunion promises restoration; one that ends in loss promises meaning. Ask whether your ending's shape is true to the facts, or only satisfying.

## The pushback

- **The ending rule is too crude.** Plenty of tragedies end without death (*Oedipus* lives on, blind), and plenty of comedies end without marriage.
- **The labels are later inventions.** Romance, problem play and dark comedy are all critics' categories laid over plays written without them. Useful, but not how the writers thought.
- **Comedy's inclusion has victims.** Shylock in *The Merchant of Venice* is stripped of his wealth and forced to convert so the comedy can end in happiness. The happy community is not happy for everyone.

## Try it
Take the ending of your work-in-progress, or of a play or film you know well.
1. Write one sentence saying what the opening promised the audience: survival and reunion, meaningful loss, or both.
2. Write the last image as it stands. Is it a gathering or a separation?
3. Rewrite the last scene in the opposite form: the reunion becomes a separation, or the death a near-miss. Keep everything before it the same.
4. Read both. Which one did the first half earn?

- **Stage:** Like Chekhov, mark the three funniest and three saddest lines in one scene. Are any the same line?
- **Screen:** Find the scene where your tone could flip, as *Parasite*'s does, and plant one earlier image that makes the flip feel inevitable.
- **Journalism:** For a feature, draft two kickers: one that reunites, one that leaves someone outside. Which is truer?

**Check:** Does your ending deliver the promise your first scene made, or break it on purpose?

## Check yourself

Q: What's the classical rule of thumb for telling comedy from tragedy?
A: How it ends: comedy in marriage or reunion, tragedy in death or separation. Underneath, comedy moves toward inclusion and tragedy toward isolation.

Q: Who coined the term "problem plays" for some of Shakespeare's works, and what are they?
A: F. S. Boas, in 1896. Plays like *Measure for Measure* and *All's Well That Ends Well* that raise moral questions their endings don't settle.

Q: How did Fletcher define tragicomedy?
A: A play without deaths, which keeps it from being tragedy, that brings characters near death, which keeps it from being comedy.

Q: Why is *The Cherry Orchard* a classic case of modern tragicomedy?
A: Chekhov insisted it was a comedy, even a farce in places, while his director staged it as tragedy. The text supports both, and the doubleness is the point.

Q: What does a comedy promise its audience?
A: That the trouble will be survived and the group put back together, so we can laugh at pain without fearing lasting harm.
