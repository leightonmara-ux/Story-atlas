## Where it comes from

Every story decides whose desires drive it, whose eyes we see through, and who is only looked at. For a long time those decisions were invisible because they were so habitual. Three writers, working in different places and forms, made them visible.

**Laura Mulvey**, a British film theorist and filmmaker, published "**Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema**" in the journal *Screen* in 1975. It's one of the most cited essays in film studies, and it gave us the phrase **the male gaze**.

**Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie**, the Nigerian novelist (*Half of a Yellow Sun*, *Americanah*), gave a TED talk in 2009 called "**The Danger of a Single Story**". It has been watched tens of millions of times.

**Thomas King**, a writer born in California who has long lived in Canada and has written mostly about Native North America, gave the 2003 Massey Lectures on CBC Radio. They were published the same year as ***The Truth About Stories: A Native Narrative***. King long said he was part Cherokee through his father; in 2025 he accepted genealogists' findings that he has no Indigenous ancestry.

## The big ideas

### The male gaze

Mulvey used psychoanalysis, which she openly called a political weapon, to ask why classic Hollywood films give pleasure, and to whom. Her answer: in mainstream narrative cinema, the man is the one who looks and the woman is the one looked at. She coined the word *to-be-looked-at-ness* for the way women on screen are displayed as spectacle.

The structure works on three levels at once. The camera looks at the woman. The male characters look at her. And the audience is invited to look through the male characters' eyes, identifying with the hero who drives the story forward while the woman's appearance tends to stop it in its tracks. Her examples included Hitchcock's *Vertigo* and *Rear Window* and Josef von Sternberg's films with Marlene Dietrich.

The point isn't that individual directors are sexist. It's that the grammar of the form, who gets the point-of-view shots, whose desire is the engine, who is framed in close-up as an object, carries a structure that most viewers absorb without noticing.

### Mulvey's own revisions

Mulvey didn't leave the essay alone. In "Afterthoughts on 'Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema'" (1981) she took up a question the first essay skipped: what about women in the audience? In later work, such as *Death 24x a Second* (2006), she wrote about how home video and the pause button let viewers stop, rewind and study images, a more thoughtful kind of spectatorship than the one she described in 1975. She has also said the original essay was a polemic written for a particular moment.

### The single story

Adichie's talk begins with her childhood. Reading British and American books, she wrote stories full of white children with blue eyes who played in snow and ate apples, things she had never seen. She hadn't known that people like her could be in books.

Her argument is about what happens when a person or a place is only ever told one way. Africa as poverty and catastrophe. A poor family as nothing but poor. She is careful about the mechanism:

> The single story creates stereotypes, and the problem with stereotypes is not that they are untrue, but that they are incomplete.
> — Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie, "The Danger of a Single Story" (2009)

She also links storytelling to power: who tells the story, when, and how many stories are told. And she notes how much depends on where you start. Begin a history at a different moment and the same events tell a different story.

### Stories are all we are

King's lectures each open by retelling a creation story, the woman who falls from the sky onto the back of a turtle, and he sets it against Genesis. One world begins with a single, all-powerful creator and a fall; the other with cooperation, many animals working together, and a world made jointly. His point isn't which is true. It's that the stories a culture tells shape what it believes is natural.

His refrain is "The truth about stories is that that's all we are." And each lecture ends by handing the story to the listener, telling them they can do what they like with it, but that they can't now claim they never heard it. Hearing a story makes you responsible for it.

### The Bechdel test

In 1985, Alison Bechdel's comic strip *Dykes to Watch Out For* ran an episode called "The Rule". A character explains that she only sees a movie if it meets three conditions: it has at least two women in it, who talk to each other, about something besides a man. Bechdel has always credited the idea to her friend **Liz Wallace** and prefers the name **Bechdel-Wallace test**. She has suggested Wallace may have been inspired by Virginia Woolf's *A Room of One's Own*.

It's a crude tool, and was meant as a joke. A film can pass and still be demeaning, or fail and be thoughtful. But as a quick count it reveals a pattern: a startling number of films don't pass. Others have proposed variants, such as the film critic Manohla Dargis's "DuVernay test" (2016), which asks whether characters of color have full lives that don't exist only to serve white characters.

## Use it

These ideas become practical when you turn them into questions about your own draft:

- Whose desire drives the plot? Whose choices make things happen?
- Who gets interiority: thoughts, doubts, a point of view? Who is only seen from outside?
- Who looks, and who is looked at? Who is described by their appearance?
- Where does the story start, and what would it look like starting earlier or later?
- Is any group in the story represented by one person, or one kind of story?

Each medium gives you a particular place to look.

- **Stage:** Count lines and scenes. Who speaks to whom, about what? Who gets the soliloquy or the direct address that lets an audience into their head? Who exists only to bring news or comfort to the lead?
- **Screen:** Look at the shot list. Whose point-of-view shots does the film contain, and who is framed in close-up as an object of someone else's look? Mulvey's analysis is a direct tool for this.
- **Journalism:** Ask who is quoted as an expert and who as a victim; who gets a name and a voice and who is a statistic. Adichie's warning applies squarely to foreign coverage, crime reporting and stories about poverty. Where you start a story about a conflict can decide who looks like the aggressor.

## The pushback

- **Psychoanalysis and the passive viewer.** Many film scholars questioned Mulvey's reliance on Freud and Lacan and her assumption that viewers simply absorb the structure. Research on real audiences found they identify across gender and read against the grain.
- **Whose gaze?** bell hooks, in "The Oppositional Gaze" (1992), argued that Mulvey's account ignored race, and that Black women spectators had long looked critically rather than identifying. Queer critics made related points.
- **The danger of a new single story.** "Represent everyone" can turn into box-ticking or into characters who carry a message instead of a life. The aim is fuller stories, not quotas.
- **Tests aren't analysis.** The Bechdel-Wallace test measures presence, not quality. It's a starting point for a question, never the answer.

## Try it
Take your work-in-progress, or a film you know well, and make a quick census.
1. List the five most important characters (or sources).
2. For each, note: Do they want something that drives the plot? Do we get their thoughts or point of view? Are they described mainly by how they look? Do they talk to anyone about something other than the lead?
3. Find the character with the fewest yeses. Write one short scene, or a paragraph, from their point of view, with a desire of their own.
4. Ask whether the story would start somewhere else if it were theirs.

- **Stage:** Count lines and private moments (soliloquies, direct address). Who never gets one?
- **Screen:** Mark every point-of-view shot and every close-up that shows someone being looked at. Whose eyes do we borrow?
- **Journalism:** Sort your sources into "expert" and "affected". Who is named and quoted as an authority, and who appears only as an example or a number?

**Check:** Could the least-seen person in your story recognize themselves as more than one story?

## Check yourself

Q: What does Mulvey mean by the male gaze?
A: In classic narrative cinema, the camera, the male characters and the audience all look at women as objects, while identifying with the man who drives the story.

Q: How did Mulvey revise her argument later?
A: She addressed female spectators in her 1981 "Afterthoughts", and later wrote about a more reflective viewer who can pause and study images on video.

Q: What, for Adichie, is wrong with stereotypes?
A: Not that they're untrue but that they're incomplete: they make one story become the only story.

Q: What is the Bechdel test, and whom does Bechdel credit?
A: A film passes if it has two women who talk to each other about something besides a man. Bechdel credits her friend Liz Wallace and calls it the Bechdel-Wallace test.

Q: What does King mean by ending each lecture by handing the story to the listener?
A: Once you've heard a story you're responsible for it; you can't later say you'd have lived differently if only you'd heard it.
