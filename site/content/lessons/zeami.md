## Who and when

**Zeami Motokiyo** (c. 1363–c. 1443) was an actor, playwright and theorist who, more than anyone, shaped **Noh**, the Japanese masked dance-drama. He was the son of Kan'ami, a celebrated performer, and as a boy caught the attention of the shōgun Ashikaga Yoshimitsu, whose patronage lifted their troupe into the highest circles of Kyoto culture. Late in life, out of favor with a later shōgun, he was exiled to the island of Sado.

He wrote many of the plays still performed today and a series of treatises on the art. The first and best known is the ***Fūshikaden*** (often translated "Teachings on Style and the Flower"), written in stages from around 1400. Others include *Kakyō* ("A Mirror Held to the Flower") and *Sandō* ("The Three Courses"), his most direct advice on writing plays.

The treatises were secret: teachings for his heirs, not for the public. They circulated within the profession for centuries and only became widely known after they were published in 1909. They're now read as some of the world's great writing on performance.

## The big ideas

### Jo-ha-kyū

The idea Zeami is best known for outside Japan is **jo-ha-kyū**, a three-part rhythm he took from court music (*gagaku*) and made the organizing principle of Noh.

- **Jo** (introduction, opening): slow, simple, calm. It lets the audience settle and establishes the mood.
- **Ha** (breaking, development): the longest part. The opening is broken open; things become more complex, more detailed, more intense.
- **Kyū** (rapid, urgent): a fast, concentrated close that brings everything to a swift finish.

Notice what this is *not*. It's not a theory of conflict, or of a protagonist pursuing a goal. It's a theory of **tempo and intensity**: how the energy of a performance should rise and quicken, then end before the audience tires.

### At every scale

Zeami's most striking claim is that jo-ha-kyū isn't just the shape of a play. He treats it as a principle running through everything: a day's program of plays, a single play, a scene, a song, a dance, even a single movement or phrase. Each part of the whole has its own jo, ha and kyū inside it.

In *Sandō* he sets out an ideal play in five sections: one for jo, three for ha, one for kyū. The middle section is itself a small jo-ha-kyū. A day's performance worked the same way, with a calm, auspicious opening play, the richest and most varied plays in the middle, and a quick, vivid finish. (The fixed five-category program associated with Noh, god plays through demon plays, was formalized later, but it follows his logic.)

This nesting is the idea's real power. A writer can ask the same question of a whole script, of an act, and of a single speech: does it open simply, deepen, and then accelerate to a close?

### The audience decides

Zeami was a working performer, intensely aware of the audience. He advises reading the mood of the house: if the audience is restless, a performer may need to begin differently; if the occasion is grand, the opening should be dignified. Jo-ha-kyū is partly a way of matching the performance to how people actually pay attention: they need time to arrive, they want to be drawn in, and they want the end to come before they've had enough.

### The flower

Zeami's central image for success on stage is ***hana***, the **flower**. A flower is admired because it blooms and fades and is never quite the same twice. For Zeami, the flower is the effect on the audience: freshness, the interesting, the unexpected. He links it closely to novelty, and he suggests that some of its power depends on the audience not knowing how it's done. Hence the secrecy of the treatises.

The lesson for any writer: technique matters only as far as it produces a living response in this audience, now.

### Yūgen

**Yūgen** is the quality of beauty Zeami prized most. The word originally suggested something dark, deep and hard to see. In his use it means a refined, graceful elegance with a sense of depth beneath it, beauty that suggests more than it shows. Noh's slow movement, masks and spare stage are built to evoke it.

### The dream play

Many of Zeami's best plays follow a form now called *mugen* Noh, or "dream" Noh. A traveler, often a monk, arrives at a famous place and meets a local person. In the second half that person returns in their true form, the ghost of someone whose story is tied to the place, and relives a moment of passion or suffering through dance. The structure turns on **memory and revelation** rather than action in the present.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Jo-ha-kyū is a superb rehearsal tool. Ask it of an entrance, a monologue, a scene and a whole evening. Actors and directors far beyond Japan, including those influenced by intercultural and physical theatre, use it for exactly this.
- **Screen:** Editors and directors think in jo-ha-kyū without naming it: the quiet opening of a sequence, its building middle, the cut that comes just before you expect it. Kurosawa's *Throne of Blood* (1957) borrows Noh's stillness, masks and music.
- **Journalism:** A long feature can open slowly, give the reader time to settle in, deepen through its middle, and then move briskly to the end. The common mistake is the reverse: an energetic top and a long, slackening tail.

## The pushback

- **It's about rhythm, not plot.** Jo-ha-kyū won't tell you what should happen. Combine it with a theory of action if you need one.
- **Easy to flatten.** Taken out of context it can become "slow, medium, fast", losing Zeami's sense of nesting, audience, and occasion.
- **Written for a specific art.** The treatises assume Noh's conventions, its masks, its music and its training. Some advice, on role types or the actor's age, doesn't translate.
- **Translation matters.** Key terms like *hana* and *yūgen* have no exact English equivalents, and translators differ. Treat any single gloss as an approximation.

## Check yourself

Q: What are jo, ha and kyū?
A: A slow, simple opening; a longer, developing middle that breaks it open and intensifies; and a rapid, concentrated close.

Q: What does it mean that jo-ha-kyū applies "at every scale"?
A: Zeami applied it to a day's program, a play, a section, a song and a single movement. Each part contains its own jo-ha-kyū.

Q: How does jo-ha-kyū differ from conflict-based structures?
A: It describes tempo and intensity in performance, not a protagonist's struggle toward a goal.

Q: What does Zeami mean by the "flower"?
A: The living effect on the audience: freshness, interest and the unexpected, which depends partly on novelty and on not revealing how it's achieved.

Q: What is yūgen?
A: A refined, graceful beauty with a sense of hidden depth, the quality Zeami prized most.
