## Who and when

In 2011, Emma Coats, then a story artist at Pixar, began tweeting short lessons about storytelling that she'd picked up from the directors and senior artists around her. The following year, 22 of them were gathered into a single numbered list, and "Pixar's 22 Rules of Storytelling" went viral. It's now pinned above desks in writers' rooms and classrooms everywhere.

Two things are worth knowing up front. First, the rules were **Coats's own notes**, not official studio doctrine; she later said she wished she'd called them guidelines. They reflect a working culture rather than a rulebook. Second, the most famous of them, the **story spine**, didn't originate at Pixar at all. It came from improvisational theater.

## Where the story spine comes from

Kenn Adams, a playwright and improv teacher, developed the story spine in the late 1980s while working with an improvisational theater company in New York. Improvisers needed a way to build a whole story, live, in front of an audience, without getting lost. Adams's answer was a short series of sentence starters:

1. **Once upon a time…**
2. **Every day…**
3. **But one day…**
4. **Because of that…**
5. **Because of that…** (repeated as often as needed)
6. **Until finally…**
7. **And ever since then…**

Adams wrote about it in *How to Improvise a Full-Length Play* (2007). Coats's rule #4 gives the spine in a slightly trimmed form, ending at "Until finally".

## The big ideas

### The spine is a cause-and-effect machine

Look at what each line does:

- **Once upon a time / Every day** establishes the world and its routine, the balance before the story.
- **One day** breaks the routine. That's the inciting incident.
- **Because of that** is the crucial phrase. Each event has to *cause* the next. You can't write "and then"; the template won't let you.
- **Until finally** is the climax, the event the chain has been building toward.
- **And ever since then** is the new normal. It proves something changed.

Try it on *Toy Story*. Once upon a time there was a toy cowboy who was his boy's favorite. Every day he ran the toy box. One day a flashy space ranger arrived. Because of that, the cowboy grew jealous and, in a scheme gone wrong, knocked the newcomer out a window. Because of that, both of them ended up lost in the outside world. Until finally they worked together to get home. And ever since then, they've been friends.

That's *Aristotle*'s "because of, not after" in seven lines. The spine is useful precisely because it's so small: if your story can't be told this way, something in the causal chain is missing.

### Characters: trying, opinions, opposites

Several of the rules are about making an audience care. The first is the most quoted:

> You admire a character for trying more than for their successes.
> — Emma Coats, rule #1

Effort is sympathetic; success alone isn't. Other rules push the same way: give characters opinions, because passive characters are hard to love; find what a character is good at and comfortable with, then throw the opposite at them; know what's at stake, so the audience has a reason to root for them.

### Coincidence and endings

One rule draws a line that many writers find clarifying: coincidences that get characters *into* trouble are fine, because audiences accept that bad luck happens. Coincidences that get them *out* of trouble feel like cheating, because the character didn't earn the escape. It's the old complaint against the *deus ex machina* in modern dress.

Another rule advises coming up with your ending before you figure out your middle. Endings are hard, and knowing where you're going tells you which complications matter.

### Rules for the stuck writer

A good share of the list isn't about structure at all. It's about process:

- Discount the first few ideas that come to mind. Get the obvious out of the way and surprise yourself.
- Put it on paper. You can't fix an idea that's still in your head.
- When stuck, list what *wouldn't* happen next. Sometimes the right answer appears.
- Finish your story, even if it isn't perfect. You learn by finishing.
- Take apart the stories you love. What you like in them is a part of you.
- Ask why you must tell *this* story. What's the belief burning inside you that it feeds?

This reflects how Pixar's films are actually made: through repeated drafts, screenings of rough versions, and blunt notes from colleagues, a process Ed Catmull describes in *Creativity, Inc.* Many Pixar films were substantially rebuilt during production. The rules make most sense as tools for revision, not a recipe for a first draft.

### Economy

The last rule asks what the essence of your story is and what its most economical telling would be. Like the spine, it pushes toward compression: if you know it, you can say it short.

## Use it

- **Screen:** Write your film as a story spine before you outline. If you find yourself writing "and then" instead of "because of that", you've found a weak link. Pixar's own films show the spine works for features, and it suits short films especially well.
- **Stage:** The spine began in improv theater, and it still works as a rehearsal and devising tool: give a company the first two lines and build the rest live. "Give your characters opinions" is advice every playwright needs.
- **Journalism:** The spine is a fast check on narrative pieces. "Every day" is your subject's routine before the event; "One day" is the news; "And ever since then" is the consequence that makes it matter. If you can't fill in "Until finally", the story may not have resolved.

## The pushback

- **Not a theory.** The 22 rules are a grab bag: some about structure, some about character, some about getting unstuck. They don't add up to a system, and they were never meant to.
- **The Pixar halo.** The list spread partly because of the studio's name. Coats was passing on lessons, and readers sometimes treat them as the secret formula behind the films, which they aren't.
- **The spine is a skeleton, not a story.** It guarantees causality, but not surprise, theme or depth. A spine can be perfectly linked and still dull.
- **Cause and effect isn't everything.** Stories built on mood, juxtaposition or the *Kishōtenketsu* twist don't fit the "because of that" chain, and aren't worse for it.

## Try it

Write your own story as a story spine, with at least three "Because of that" lines.

1. Read it aloud. Circle any line that only works if you say "and then". Fix it by changing the event before it so it truly causes the next one.
2. List every coincidence in your story. Does each get a character into trouble, or out of it? Rewrite any rescue-by-luck so the character earns the escape by trying.
3. If you're stuck at a weak link, list five things that *wouldn't* happen next, and see if the right one appears.

- **Stage:** use it as a devising game. Give actors your first two lines and let them improvise the rest, then compare their chain with yours.
- **Screen:** warm up on a Pixar film, as the lesson does with *Toy Story*, before doing your own feature or short.
- **Journalism:** fill in "Every day", "One day" and "And ever since then" from your reporting. If you can't write "Until finally", the story may still be open.

**Check:** Does every "Because of that" really cause the line after it?
