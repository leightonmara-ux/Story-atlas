## Where it comes from

A film has lights and a score; a play has a lighting rig. A journalist has words, tape and photographs, and must use them on true material. Yet mood matters just as much in fact: a reader who feels the cold of a morning, or a listener who hears a room go quiet, understands differently from one merely told what happened.

These tools grew up in different places: the feature's scene and **kicker** (see *The feature formula*); the New Journalists' **status detail** (see *Tom Wolfe & the New Journalism*); radio and podcast craft of natural sound, tape and music, popularized by *Ira Glass* and *This American Life* (from 1995); and photojournalism's image and caption, with critics such as *Susan Sontag* and *Roland Barthes*.

All of them raise a question film and theatre can mostly ignore: when does shaping feelings about real events become manipulation?

## The big ideas

### The telling detail

The most powerful mood tool in prose is the **telling detail**: one concrete, observed particular that does the work of a paragraph. Wolfe's status details are one kind. The point is selection: you notice a hundred things and use the one that tells.

Gay Talese's "Frank Sinatra Has a Cold" (*Esquire*, April 1966) is the classic. Talese never got his interview, so he watched. The piece opens with Sinatra in a dark corner of a bar, bourbon in one hand and a cigarette in the other, while those around him wait for him to speak. The cold itself is the telling detail: a singer with a cold, Talese suggests, is like a painter without paint, and the whole entourage's mood hangs on it.

### The scene-setting lede

A **scene-setting lede** drops the reader into a place at a moment, with light, weather and sound, before explaining anything. It's the anecdotal lede of *The feature formula* with its senses switched on.

The masterclass in restraint is John Hersey's "Hiroshima" (*The New Yorker*, 1946). It opens at exactly fifteen minutes past eight on the morning of August 6, 1945, with a clerk named Toshiko Sasaki who had just sat down at her desk and turned her head to speak to the girl at the next desk. No adjectives of horror. The ordinary moment, set precisely in time, makes what's coming unbearable. Mood can be built by understatement as much as by intensity.

### Pace: sentence and paragraph length

Prose has tempo. Long sentences, with clauses that unfold and qualify, slow the reader and suit reflection. Short sentences speed things up. They hit.

Paragraphs work the same way. A one-sentence paragraph after several long ones is a drumbeat; overuse it and it becomes a tic. When the action quickens, shorten; when it's time to think, lengthen. Read aloud to hear the rhythm.

### Where a quote falls

A quote carries more weight at the end of a paragraph than buried in the middle, because that's where the reader pauses. A powerful quote can stand alone as its own paragraph. The final quote or image of the piece, the **kicker**, sets the mood the reader leaves with. But a quote moved for drama must still mean what the speaker meant in context.

### Natural sound

In audio and video, **nat sound** (natural sound, or ambience) is sound recorded on location: footsteps on gravel, a kettle, a crowd. A few seconds of it puts the listener in a place faster than any description. Reporters record it on purpose, letting the microphone run before and after the interview.

Used honestly, nat sound is evidence: this is what it sounded like there. That's why many news organizations frown on adding sound that wasn't recorded at the scene, or moving it to where it didn't happen, without telling listeners.

### Tape and the pause

The interview recording, the **tape**, carries what a written quote can't: hesitation, laughter, a voice cracking. Often the strongest moment is a **pause**, and an editor can leave the silence in. A second or two of quiet on the radio is very loud.

For interviewers, the craft lesson is to tolerate silence; people often say the important thing after it. The ethical lesson is not to manufacture pauses by cutting, or stitch answers to different questions together.

### Music beds

Narrative podcasts often lay quiet **music beds** under narration, especially at transitions and reflections. As the *Ira Glass* lesson describes, music can mark that a section is ending and lift the moment where a story says what it means.

Music is also audio's most openly manipulative tool. Ominous drones under a crime story tell listeners to be afraid before the facts do, which is why many news programs keep music to openings and breaks. Ask whether a bed is doing the story's work or yours.

### The photograph and the caption

A photograph's meaning is unstable until words fix it. Roland Barthes, in his essay "Rhetoric of the Image" (1964), called this the caption's job of **anchorage**: the text chooses one of the image's possible meanings. (See *Roland Barthes*.) The same photo of a crowd can read as a celebration or a riot.

Captions can mislead. Dorothea Lange's *Migrant Mother* (1936) became the face of the Depression, but the woman in it, Florence Owens Thompson, was identified only in 1978, and her family disputed details in Lange's notes, such as the claim that they had sold their car's tires for food. *Susan Sontag*, in *Regarding the Pain of Others* (2003), argued that a photograph can haunt but cannot explain on its own; understanding has to come from the words around it.

### The ethics of mood in fact

Every tool here can serve honesty or betray it. Roger Fenton's 1855 Crimean War photograph *The Valley of the Shadow of Death* exists in two versions, one with cannonballs on the road and one without; the filmmaker Errol Morris investigated which came first in *Believing Is Seeing* (2011) and concluded the balls had been placed on the road for the second. In 2003 the *Los Angeles Times* fired the photographer Brian Walski for combining two frames into one more dramatic image from Iraq.

The rule from *Janet Malcolm & John Hersey* applies: the writer must not invent. Mood made from what was really there, selected and placed with care, is craft. Mood made from things that weren't there is fabrication.

## Use it

- **Journalism:** Choose one telling detail per scene, set your lede in a precise moment, vary sentence length with the action, and put your strongest quote last. In audio, record nat sound everywhere and keep real pauses. Write captions as carefully as the story.
- **Stage:** Documentary and verbatim theatre use the same tools and face the same ethics: whose words, placed where, under what light. See *Mood on stage*.
- **Screen:** Documentary filmmakers choose music, nat sound and images under the same contract (see *Bill Nichols*). For fiction, the telling detail is your scene description at its best.

## The pushback

- **All selection is shaping.** There's no neutral way to choose a detail, a quote or a picture. The honest answer is care and disclosure, not pretending to have no point of view.
- **Restraint can be its own manipulation.** Flatness can also steer feeling; the question is whether readers are misled.
- **Audio's house style.** Critics argue that narrative podcasts sound alike: same music, same pauses, same rising reflection. The tool can become a tic.
- **Audiences want feeling.** Refusing mood can leave important stories unread; emotion fairly earned is part of the truth.

## Try it
Take a draft feature, audio script or reported scene of your own.
1. Underline every descriptive detail. Keep the one that tells most about the person or place; cut or demote the rest.
2. Rewrite your opening as a precise moment: a time, a place, one sound, one action.
3. Mark where the action speeds up, and shorten sentences there. Mark the reflective passage and let them lengthen.
4. Move your strongest quote to the end of its paragraph, and check it still means what the speaker meant.
5. Mark every mood choice (music, weather, a pause, a photo) and write beside it: observed, or added?

- **Stage:** In a verbatim scene, choose one real sound to open it and one pause to keep.
- **Screen:** In a documentary sequence, cut one music cue and let nat sound carry the scene.
- **Journalism:** Write the photo caption first, then check it says only what you know.

**Check:** Could you defend every mood choice to the people in the story?

## Check yourself

Q: What makes a detail "telling"?
A: It's one concrete, observed particular, chosen from many, that does the work of description: it reveals character, status or situation on its own.

Q: How does the opening of Hersey's "Hiroshima" create mood?
A: By understatement: an ordinary moment, a clerk at her desk, fixed at the exact minute the bomb fell, with no adjectives of horror.

Q: What is nat sound, and why is moving it a problem?
A: Natural sound recorded at the scene. It works as evidence of what a place sounded like, so adding or relocating it without telling listeners misleads them.

Q: What did Barthes mean by anchorage?
A: The caption's job of fixing one of a photograph's many possible meanings.

Q: Where's the ethical line for mood in journalism?
A: Mood built from what was really there, selected and placed fairly, is craft; mood built from invented or misplaced sound, images or details is fabrication.
