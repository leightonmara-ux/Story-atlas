## Who and when

Bertolt Brecht (1898–1956) was a German playwright, poet and director. He became famous with *The Threepenny Opera* (1928), written with the composer Kurt Weill. A Marxist, he fled Germany when the Nazis took power in 1933 and spent years in exile in Scandinavia and the United States. In exile he wrote his best-known plays, including *Mother Courage and Her Children* (written 1939), *The Good Person of Szechwan* and *The Caucasian Chalk Circle*. He returned to East Berlin and in 1949 founded the Berliner Ensemble with the actor Helene Weigel, his wife.

He wrote with close collaborators, notably Elisabeth Hauptmann, Margarete Steffin and Ruth Berlau. How much credit they deserve is disputed. John Fuegi's *Brecht & Co.* (1994) made the strongest case that he took more than his share; other scholars think Fuegi overstated it.

His theory is scattered across notes, essays and his *Short Organum for the Theatre* (1948). Most English readers know it through John Willett's collection *Brecht on Theatre* (1964).

## The big ideas

### Against Aristotle

Brecht called his theatre **non-Aristotelian**. As he saw it, the Aristotelian tradition (see *Aristotle*) works by drawing the audience in: we identify with the hero, feel pity and fear, and leave purged and satisfied. That, Brecht thought, made audiences passive. They felt the hero's fate as inevitable, like the weather. He wanted them to leave asking *why did that happen, and could it be otherwise?*

### Epic theatre

He borrowed the term **epic** from the old distinction between drama, which shows, and epic poetry, which narrates. Epic theatre tells a story as a story: it can jump in time, address the audience, summarize, and comment. The director Erwin Piscator had used the term before him, and the two influenced each other.

### Dramatic versus epic

In his notes to the opera *Rise and Fall of the City of Mahagonny* (1930), Brecht set out a two-column table contrasting the two forms. A selection, paraphrased:

| Dramatic theatre | Epic theatre |
|---|---|
| Involves the spectator in the action | Makes the spectator an observer |
| Uses up their capacity to act | Arouses their capacity to act |
| Gives them feelings | Forces them to make decisions |
| Suggestion | Argument |
| Eyes on the ending | Eyes on the course of events |
| One scene makes the next | Each scene for itself |
| Linear development | In curves and jumps |
| Human nature as fixed | Human beings as changeable and able to change |

He later said the table showed shifts of emphasis, not absolute opposites. But it's the clearest map of his thinking, and it reads almost like a point-by-point reply to every structure lesson in this course.

### Episodic structure

"Each scene for itself" is a direct challenge to Aristotle's "because of, not after". Brecht's scenes are often self-contained episodes, each making its own point, joined by narration or songs rather than tight causality. *Mother Courage* follows a canteen-woman through years of the Thirty Years' War in twelve scenes, each introduced by a title that tells you in advance what will happen.

That's deliberate. If you know the outcome, you stop asking *what happens next?* and start asking *how and why does it happen?*

### The Verfremdungseffekt

The key technique is the **Verfremdungseffekt**, translated by Willett as the **alienation effect** and by others as the **estrangement**, **distancing** or **defamiliarization effect**. "Alienation" is the best-known rendering but misleading: Brecht didn't want to repel the audience, he wanted to make the familiar look strange, so it could be seen freshly and questioned.

He developed the term after seeing the Chinese actor Mei Lanfang perform in Moscow in 1935, and described it in an essay on Chinese acting. Some scholars link it to Viktor Shklovsky's *ostranenie* ("making strange"); whether Brecht took the idea from the Russian formalists is debated.

Techniques Brecht used to produce it:

- **Titles and projections** announcing what a scene will show.
- **Songs** that break the action and comment on it.
- **Visible stage machinery**: lights in view, a half-height curtain, scene changes in sight.
- **Actors who show the character** rather than disappear into it, as if quoting someone's behavior, sometimes speaking in the third person or past tense in rehearsal.
- **Gestus**: a gesture or attitude that reveals a character's social relationships, not just their feelings.

### A theatre that teaches, and entertains

Brecht is sometimes caricatured as joyless. He wasn't. The *Short Organum* insists that theatre's first business is to entertain, and his plays are full of jokes, songs and clowning. The pleasure he wanted was the pleasure of understanding.

## Use it

- **Stage:** You don't have to share Brecht's politics to use his tools. A title card, a direct address, a song that comments: any of them lets you control the audience's distance. Many contemporary plays move in and out of identification on purpose.
- **Screen:** Breaking the fourth wall, chapter titles, a narrator who tells you the ending first: all are Brechtian moves, from *Fleabag*'s glances to the camera to the documentary narrator who explains as you watch. Ask whether you want viewers to feel with a character or to judge the situation.
- **Journalism:** Telling the reader the outcome in the first paragraph and then explaining how it happened is Brecht's logic. So is stepping back from a vivid scene to show the system behind it.

## The pushback

- **Audiences identify anyway.** After *Mother Courage*'s Zurich premiere in 1941, many found Courage a tragic, suffering mother, not the war profiteer Brecht intended. He revised the play to make her harder to sympathize with, with mixed results.
- **His best plays break his rules.** Some critics argue the plays succeed because of their emotional power, not despite it.
- **Aristotle caricatured?** Aristotle didn't argue for passive spectators, and his idea of catharsis is far less clear than Brecht's attack implies.
- **Politics.** His Marxism and his accommodations with the East German state remain contested.

## Try it

Take a scene from your own play or script where you want the audience to judge a situation, not just feel it.

1. Write a title for the scene, as *Mother Courage* does, that tells the audience in advance what will happen.
2. Put the title at the top and rewrite the scene, cutting the suspense the title has made pointless. Spend that space on *how* and *why* it happens.
3. Add one more distancing device: a direct address, a song or speech that comments, or a gestus that shows a character's social relationships.

- **Stage:** try the title as a projection or a spoken announcement, and read the scene to friends both ways.
- **Screen:** try a chapter card or a narrator who gives away the outcome before the sequence plays.
- **Journalism:** take a narrative draft that saves its outcome for the end, state the outcome in the first paragraph, and spend the piece on how and why.

**Check:** Now that the audience knows what happens, are they asking why it happened, and whether it could have gone otherwise?

## Check yourself

Q: Why did Brecht reject the Aristotelian model?
A: He believed identification and catharsis made audiences passive, leaving them feeling events were inevitable instead of asking why they happened and how they could change.

Q: What does the Verfremdungseffekt aim to do?
A: Make the familiar strange, so the audience sees a situation freshly and critically. "Alienation" is a common but misleading translation; "estrangement" or "defamiliarization" is closer.

Q: Why do the scene titles in *Mother Courage* tell you what's going to happen?
A: To shift attention from "what happens next?" to "how and why does it happen?", which is the question Brecht wanted audiences to think about.

Q: Name three epic-theatre techniques.
A: Any three of: titles or projections, songs that comment, visible lights and machinery, actors showing rather than becoming the character, gestus.

Q: In Brecht's table, how does epic structure differ from dramatic structure?
A: Dramatic theatre links scenes causally toward an ending; epic theatre treats each scene for itself, moving in curves and jumps with the focus on the course of events.
