## Who and when

Jon Franklin was a science writer at the Baltimore *Evening Sun*. He won the first-ever Pulitzer Prize for Feature Writing in 1979 for "Mrs. Kelly's Monster", an account of a brain operation, and a second Pulitzer, for Explanatory Journalism, in 1985 for "The Mind Fixers", a series on the science of the brain.

His book *Writing for Story* (1986) set out to teach something reporters had been told couldn't be taught: how to write true stories with the shape and pull of fiction. It became the founding text of narrative journalism courses.

## The big ideas

### What a story is

Franklin's definition, roughly: a story is a sequence of actions that happens when a **sympathetic character** meets a **complicating situation** that they **confront and resolve**.

Each part rules things out:

- **Sympathetic character:** the reader has to care what happens to someone. Not necessarily a nice person, but a human one we can follow.
- **Complication:** something that happens to a person and complicates their life. The strongest touch universals: love, death, pain, fear, loss.
- **Confront and resolve:** the character *acts*, and the resolution comes from them, not from luck or outside forces.

### Find the resolution first

This is Franklin's most important lesson for reporters. A story's power lives in its resolution, and **you can't invent one**. So before you plan a narrative, ask: *has this resolved?* If it hasn't, you may have an important article, but you don't yet have a story in his sense.

He also observes that the resolution is often an **insight**: the moment a character understands something, and so changes how they act.

### The outline

Franklin's outline is famously short: a **complication**, a series of **developments** (often three), and a **resolution**, each written as a statement of just a few words. A subject, an active verb, an object.

The constraint is the point. If you can't state your story as a handful of active statements, you haven't found it yet, and no amount of beautiful writing will hide that. Many writers only find the outline after reporting, and then use it to decide what to cut.

### Complication and resolution are a pair

The complication and the resolution have to be about the same thing. If a story opens with a man's fear of surgery and ends with the hospital's funding problems, the resolution doesn't answer the complication. Franklin calls matching them finding the story's **focus**.

## How this differs from fiction theory

Franklin's structure is really Aristotle's tying and untying, or McKee's inciting incident and climax, translated for reality. But reality adds constraints fiction doesn't have:

- **You choose your story by its ending.** A novelist invents a resolution; a journalist goes looking for situations that have one.
- **Selection is your plot.** Everything happened; your art is deciding which actions to include and in what order.
- **The character must be real and consenting.** Franklin's method depends on long access to the people involved.

## Use it

- **Journalism:** Before pitching a narrative, write the outline: one complication, three developments, one resolution. If you can't, you need more reporting or a different form, such as the inverted pyramid or an explanatory feature.
- **Stage and screen:** The few-word outline is a fine test for any dramatic idea. "Daughter defies father. Father disowns daughter…" If the verbs aren't active, the story isn't either.
- **Documentary and fact-based drama:** Franklin's rule, find the real resolution and work backwards, is how many true-story scripts are shaped.

## The pushback

- **Tidy endings distort reality.** Critics argue that hunting for complications that resolve biases journalism toward personal triumph stories and away from systemic problems that never resolve.
- **The sympathetic-character requirement** can push writers toward individual heroes when the real story is institutional.
- **Not every piece should be a story.** Breaking news, investigations and explainers often serve readers better with other structures.
- Later writers, notably **Jack Hart** in *Storycraft*, kept Franklin's foundation but loosened it, describing a fuller narrative arc and a wider range of narrative forms.

## Read next

- Jack Hart, *Storycraft: The Complete Guide to Writing Narrative Nonfiction* (2011; 2nd ed. 2021).

## Try it

Take a true story you're reporting, or any story idea you're developing.

1. First ask: has it resolved? Write the resolution in a few words: subject, active verb, object.
2. Write the complication the same way, and check that the two are a pair: does the resolution answer this complication?
3. Fill in three developments between them, each also a short active statement.
4. Check that the resolution comes from the character's own action, or from an insight that changes how they act, not from luck or outside forces.

- **Stage:** write your play as Franklin's five lines ("Daughter defies father…"), and replace any passive or vague verbs.
- **Screen:** for a fact-based script, start from the real resolution and work backwards to the complication it answers.
- **Journalism:** if you can't write step 1, consider another form, such as an inverted pyramid or explanatory feature, before pitching it as a narrative.

**Check:** Does your resolution resolve the same problem your complication posed?

## Check yourself

Q: What are the essential parts of Franklin's definition of a story?
A: A sympathetic character, a complicating situation, and the character's own action to confront and resolve it.

Q: Why does Franklin tell reporters to look for the resolution first?
A: In nonfiction you can't invent one. A situation without a real resolution doesn't yet make a story in his sense, however important it is.

Q: What is the shape of Franklin's outline?
A: A complication, a few developments (often three) and a resolution, each a short active statement: subject, active verb, object.

Q: What does it mean for a story to have "focus"?
A: Its complication and resolution are a matched pair: the ending resolves the same problem the opening posed.

Q: What's a major criticism of Franklin's model?
A: Favoring stories that resolve neatly can bias journalism toward individual triumph narratives and away from ongoing, systemic problems.
