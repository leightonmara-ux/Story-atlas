## Who and when

Dan Harmon is a television writer and producer, best known as the creator of the sitcom *Community* (2009–2015) and the co-creator, with Justin Roiland, of the animated series *Rick and Morty* (2013–). Before either, he co-founded Channel 101 in Los Angeles in the early 2000s with Rob Schrab: a monthly screening night where amateur filmmakers showed five-minute pilots and the audience voted on which shows got another episode.

The **story circle** came out of that world. Harmon has described how he worked it out by boiling down Joseph Campbell's hero's journey until he had something he could use every day, and he later wrote it up in a series of informal online tutorials for Channel 101's filmmakers. He has sometimes called it "the embryo", because he sees it as the basic cell every story grows from.

So unlike almost everyone else in this course, Harmon never published a book about it. The circle spread through his tutorials, interviews and podcast, and then through writers' rooms.

## The big ideas

### Eight steps around a circle

Draw a circle and divide it into eight. Going clockwise from the top:

1. **You:** a character is in a zone of comfort,
2. **Need:** but they want something.
3. **Go:** They enter an unfamiliar situation,
4. **Search:** adapt to it,
5. **Find:** get what they wanted,
6. **Take:** pay a heavy price for it,
7. **Return:** then return to their familiar situation,
8. **Change:** having changed.

Read it as one sentence and you have the whole story. That's the point. Harmon wanted something short enough to check against any script in a few seconds.

### Two halves: order and chaos

Now draw a horizontal line through the middle. The top half is the familiar world: order, the known, the conscious. The bottom half is the unfamiliar: chaos, the unknown, the unconscious. Steps 1, 2, 7 and 8 sit above the line; steps 3 to 6 below it.

So every story is a **descent and return**. The character crosses down into the unknown at step 3, reaches the deepest point at the bottom of the circle, around steps 5 and 6, and climbs back up at step 7. This is Campbell's departure, initiation and return, with the mythological vocabulary stripped out.

Harmon has compared the circle to rhythms we live by: waking and sleeping, life and death, the seasons. Whether or not you buy that, it explains why the circle feels natural. It describes a journey out and back.

### Find, then take

The bottom of the circle is where the circle differs from many three-act models. At step 5 the character **gets what they wanted**. At step 6 they **pay for it**.

That pairing is worth stealing. Many stories have a midpoint where the hero seems to win. Harmon's version insists that the win has a cost, and the cost is where the meaning starts. It's similar to *Blake Snyder*'s false victory at the Midpoint, but Harmon makes the price itself the turn.

### Want versus need

Harmon draws the same distinction many theorists make. The character goes down into the unknown chasing what they *want* (step 2), and often finds, at the bottom, what they *need*, which is something different. In some tellings of the circle, step 5 is explicitly finding the need rather than the want. Compare *John Truby*'s desire and need.

### Circles everywhere

The circle is fractal. Harmon uses it for an entire series, for a season, for an episode, for each storyline within the episode, and even for a single scene. A twenty-two-minute sitcom might run an A story and a B story as two small circles that cross at the bottom.

This scale-independence is why it caught on in television. TV writers have to break a lot of stories quickly, and a circle you can apply to a two-minute runner as easily as to a season is very handy.

### Change, but come home

Step 7, **Return**, matters for television in particular. Sitcom characters usually have to end the episode roughly where they started so the next one can begin. The circle allows that: the character returns to the familiar world, but step 8 says something has changed, even if only a small thing, like an understanding or a relationship. The world resets; the person doesn't, quite.

## Use it

- **Screen:** Run the circle over each storyline in an episode outline. If you can't say what a character finds at step 5 and what it costs them at step 6, that storyline probably lacks a turn. For features, it works as a quick check on a structure built another way.
- **Stage:** The descent-and-return shape suits plays well: many take a character from a safe room into a night of upheaval and back to morning. Use the line through the middle to ask where the "unfamiliar situation" of your play really begins.
- **Journalism:** Many profiles and personal essays already have this shape: someone leaves the familiar (a job, a home, a belief), adapts, gets something and pays for it, then comes back changed. The circle is a fast way to test whether a feature is a story or just a situation.

## The pushback

- **It's Campbell, simplified.** Harmon says so himself. The circle inherits the criticisms of the hero's journey: that it fits quest stories far better than other kinds, and that by being so general it can be laid over almost anything after the fact. See *Joseph Campbell & Christopher Vogler*.
- **Too loose to diagnose?** "They adapt to it" or "they pay a price" can describe almost any event. Writers who want more precision often pair the circle with a more specific tool, like *Robert McKee*'s scene turns.
- **Change isn't required everywhere.** Some of the best television characters barely change at all, and much literary fiction resists the neat return. The *Counterpoints* track questions whether every story needs a journey out and back.
- **No canonical text.** Because it lives in blog posts, talks and secondhand summaries, the wording of the eight steps varies from source to source. Check Harmon's own tutorials before quoting a version.

## Try it

Draw a circle, divide it into eight, and draw a line across the middle. Then take one storyline: a whole project, an episode, or a single scene.

1. Write one short sentence for each of the eight steps, from You to Change.
2. Mark where the character crosses the line at step 3. Where does the unfamiliar situation really begin?
3. Look hard at steps 5 and 6. What do they get, and what does it cost them? If there's no price, write one.
4. Draw a smaller circle for a B story or a single scene.

- **Stage:** many plays take a character through one night of upheaval and back to morning. Mark the moment your play goes below the line.
- **Screen:** run a circle over every storyline in an episode of a show you know, such as *Community*, then do the same for your own outline.
- **Journalism:** for a profile, ask what the subject left, what they adapted to, what they got and what it cost. If you can't fill step 6 or 8, you may have a situation, not a story.

**Check:** Does the price paid at step 6 cause the change at step 8?

## Check yourself

Q: List the eight steps of the story circle using Harmon's one-word labels.
A: You, Need, Go, Search, Find, Take, Return, Change.

Q: What does the horizontal line through the circle represent?
A: The boundary between the familiar world of order (top) and the unfamiliar world of chaos (bottom). The story is a descent below the line and a return.

Q: What's distinctive about steps 5 and 6?
A: The character gets what they wanted, then pays a heavy price for it. The win and its cost come as a pair.

Q: Why has the circle been so popular in television?
A: It works at any scale, from a season to a single storyline or scene, and it lets characters return to the familiar world each episode while still changing a little.

Q: Where did Harmon's circle come from?
A: He simplified Joseph Campbell's hero's journey into eight steps, and shared it in tutorials for filmmakers at Channel 101.
