## Who and when

María Irene Fornés (1930–2018) was born in Havana and moved to the United States with her mother and sister in 1945. She first trained as a painter. In Paris in the 1950s she saw Roger Blin's production of Beckett's *Waiting for Godot* in French, a language she barely understood, and was overwhelmed. Not long after, she began writing plays.

She became a central figure of New York's Off-Off-Broadway movement in the 1960s, working with Judson Poets' Theater (her musical *Promenade* was a collaboration with the composer Al Carmines) and later directing nearly all her own premieres. Over a long career she won a string of Obie Awards. Her best-known plays include *Fefu and Her Friends* (1977), *The Danube* (1982), *Mud* (1983), *Sarita* (1984), *The Conduct of Life* (1985), *Abingdon Square* (1987) and *What of the Night?* (a Pulitzer finalist).

She was equally famous as a **teacher**. From 1981 to the early 1990s she led the **INTAR Hispanic Playwrights-in-Residence Laboratory** in New York, which trained a generation of Latinx playwrights, among them Migdalia Cruz, Eduardo Machado, Cherríe Moraga, Caridad Svich and Nilo Cruz, who later won the Pulitzer Prize. She also taught at the Padua Hills Playwrights' Festival in California and in workshops around the world. Paula Vogel has named Fornés as one of her own key influences.

In her later years she lived with dementia. Michelle Memran's documentary *The Rest I Make Up* (2018) records her during that time.

## The big ideas

### The plays: structure from image, not plot

Fornés distrusted plot built from an outline. Her plays tend to be made of short, sharply framed scenes, each one an image: a woman ironing, a man reading aloud, two people at a table. Scenes often end abruptly or hold a final picture. The effect is closer to a series of paintings than a causal chain, but the pressure builds as the images accumulate.

### Fefu and Her Friends

Eight women gather at a New England country house in 1935. The first and third parts are played in a conventional living room. In the middle part, the audience is split into groups and led through four other spaces (the lawn, the study, a bedroom, the kitchen), where four scenes play simultaneously and are repeated until every group has seen all of them, in different orders.

It's one of the earliest and most admired examples of what we'd now call environmental or immersive staging, and it's also a structural idea: the audience assembles the middle of the play for itself. The play ends with a sudden, ambiguous act of violence.

### Mud and The Conduct of Life

*Mud* concerns three people in rural poverty: Mae, who irons for a living and longs to learn, and the two men who depend on her. Her reaching toward knowledge is the play's spine, and it leads to violence.

*The Conduct of Life* is set in an unnamed Latin American country and follows a military officer who tortures prisoners for the state, and the women in his house, including a young girl he holds captive. It's short, built of brief scenes, and deeply disturbing.

In both, Fornés writes about power and language from the inside of characters who are rarely in control of their own story. She lets scenes be quiet, private and strange, and keeps the violence unbearably close.

### The teacher: start from the inside

Fornés's workshops became legendary partly because her method was so unlike the usual playwriting class. Accounts from her students describe a consistent shape:

- **Physical warm-up**, often yoga, to quiet the mind and wake the body.
- **Visualization**: with eyes closed, writers picture a character, a place, or a situation in close sensory detail (the room, the clothes, the colors, the sounds) before writing a word.
- **Prompts and found materials**: a line, an image, an object or a drawing used as a starting point, with no plan for where it will lead.
- **Writing together in silence**, in a circle, for extended stretches, followed by reading aloud.

The aim was to bypass the critical, plotting part of the mind and reach the imagination directly. Students describe her as demanding and blunt in response, but always about the work, and focused on what was alive in it rather than on fixing it to a formula.

### Character before story

Fornés described her own practice as discovery: she followed characters to see what they would do, rather than designing a plot and placing them in it. For her, structure emerged from the work. That runs directly against the outline-first methods of *John Truby*, *Blake Snyder* and much screenwriting teaching, and it's why so many writers trained by her produce plays that don't follow the conventional arc.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Try her warm-up before a writing session: ten minutes of stillness, then visualize a character in a specific room in detail, then write without stopping. Don't decide what the scene is about until you've read it back.
- **Screen:** Her image-first thinking suits a visual medium. Write a sequence of held images and only then ask how they connect. Many short films are built this way.
- **Journalism:** Visualization is a strong revision tool for scene-writing in narrative nonfiction. Close your eyes and recall the room from your reporting notes before drafting, but only write what you actually observed or verified.

## The pushback

- **Hard to pin down.** Her method was taught in person, and accounts vary. Much of what is passed on comes from former students adapting it, so be wary of anyone presenting a fixed "Fornés system".
- **Difficult plays.** Her work can be oblique and violent; some critics and audiences have found it cold or opaque, and it's rarely produced outside a few theatres and universities.
- **Underproduced.** Her admirers argue the opposite problem: that she was one of the major American dramatists of the century and has never been given commensurate attention.
- **Not a recipe.** Free-writing from images can produce rich fragments with no throughline. Her students still had to do the work of shaping them.

## Try it

Try a session in the shape her students describe. Set aside half an hour and don't plan anything.

1. Spend a few minutes in stillness, or doing simple stretches.
2. Close your eyes and picture one character from your work-in-progress in a specific room. Notice the colors, the clothes, what's on the table, what their hands are doing.
3. Write for fifteen minutes without stopping, starting from what you saw.
4. Only then read it back and ask what the scene is about, and which single image you'd hold at its end.

- **Stage:** shape the result into one short, framed scene that ends on a held picture rather than a resolved line.
- **Screen:** write it first as a sequence of held images, and only then ask how they connect.
- **Journalism:** visualize a room from your reporting notes, write only what you observed or verified, then check every detail against the notes.

**Check:** Is there something on the page you couldn't have put in an outline, because you didn't know it until you wrote it?

## Check yourself

Q: What is structurally unusual about the middle part of *Fefu and Her Friends*?
A: The audience is split into groups that move through four spaces to see four scenes played simultaneously and repeated, each group in a different order.

Q: What did Fornés's writing workshops typically include?
A: A physical warm-up such as yoga, eyes-closed visualization of a character or place, prompts or found images, and long periods of writing in silence.

Q: How did Fornés's approach to structure differ from outline-first methods?
A: She discovered structure by following characters and images, rather than designing a plot and fitting characters into it.

Q: What was the INTAR lab, and why does it matter?
A: The Hispanic Playwrights-in-Residence Laboratory she led in New York from 1981, which trained a generation of Latinx playwrights, including Nilo Cruz and Migdalia Cruz.

Q: Why should you be cautious about any "official" Fornés method?
A: She taught in person and accounts vary; most of what circulates is adapted by former students.
