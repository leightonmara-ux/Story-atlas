## Who and when

Phoebe Waller-Bridge (born 1985) is a London-born actor and writer who trained at RADA. In 2007 she and the director and playwright Vicky Jones set up DryWrite, a small company that ran new-writing nights in London.

*Fleabag* began as a short comic piece of about ten minutes, written for a storytelling night. Jones pushed her to expand it, and directed the result: an hour-long monologue performed by Waller-Bridge alone on a chair. It opened at the Edinburgh Fringe in August 2013, in a small room at the Underbelly, won a Fringe First award and transferred to Soho Theatre in London. (Accounts differ on which night the first short version was written for, so treat that detail loosely.)

The television version followed: two series of six half-hour episodes, made by BBC Three with Amazon in 2016 and 2019 and directed by Harry Bradbeer. Series 2 won Emmys for comedy series, writing, directing and Waller-Bridge's performance. In between she wrote *Crashing* (Channel 4, 2016) and adapted Luke Jennings's spy novellas as *Killing Eve* (BBC America, 2018), running the writing of its first series before handing on to a new head writer each year, a pattern the show kept. In 2019 the scripts of both *Fleabag* series came out as *Fleabag: The Scriptures*, with her stage directions and commentary.

## The big ideas

### The audience as confidant

On stage, a solo performer talking to the audience is nothing new (see the monologue play in *Stage forms*, and direct address in *Mood on stage*). What Waller-Bridge did was carry the device to television nearly intact. Fleabag looks into the lens, raises an eyebrow, finishes a thought, makes a joke at someone's expense, then turns back to the scene. The other characters don't notice.

She has been clear that this relationship is the core of the show. In a *W* magazine profile after the first series, she said that she had to convince herself whoever was watching was instantly complicit, and that:

> The main relationship in the whole series was the one between the camera and Fleabag.
— Phoebe Waller-Bridge, *W* magazine

On *Fresh Air* (NPR, May 2019) she connected the device to control: Fleabag is narrating, editing and spinning her own life as it happens. The camera isn't a window. It's an accomplice.

Watch how this works in the first minutes of series 1. Fleabag is outside a door late at night, briefing us on the man who has just texted her. The sex that follows is narrated to us in asides, and the joke is that she's performing a confident version of herself, for him and for us. We're flattered to be let in. We don't yet see that being let in is part of the act.

### Comedy as armour

*Point of view and voice* explains how a narrator can be seen through. Fleabag is a sharp example: what she tells us is mostly true, but *how* she tells it is a defence. Every aside turns a painful moment into a joke before it can land. In a 2016 NPR interview she described her character as keeping her life funny for the benefit of the audience she has invited in.

So the comedy has a structural job. It's the want of *Want, need and wound*, made visible: Fleabag wants to be entertaining, unbothered, in control. Her need is to stop performing and grieve. Every time she glances at us, she's choosing the want.

Waller-Bridge has said in the same NPR interview that she avoided explaining Fleabag's sadness too early because it would betray the character's complexity. That leads to the show's second device.

### The withheld wound

From the first episode, Fleabag mentions her friend Boo, with whom she set up the café she runs. Boo is dead. The flashbacks are brief, a few seconds at a time, and Fleabag skips past them. Only in the last episode of series 1 does the story come out in full: Fleabag slept with Boo's boyfriend; Boo, hoping to get a little hurt and win his sympathy, stepped into a busy cycle lane and was killed.

This is a classic **withheld backstory**, and it does two things. It gives the series a question the audience keeps asking (what happened?), so six loosely plotted episodes feel like one story. And it changes everything we've seen. Rewatch the series knowing the ending and the asides read differently: they're a way of not thinking about it. The comedy we enjoyed was the symptom.

The same shape is the backbone of the stage monologue. *Stage forms* notes that the monologue play is usually a story told around an avoidance, which the speaker finally reaches. The TV series keeps that spine and adds the family scenes around it.

### When the device becomes the story

Series 2 opens with Fleabag in a restaurant bathroom, wiping blood from her nose. She turns to camera:

> This is a love story.
— Fleabag, series 2, episode 1

It's a promise to the audience about genre (see *Genre as a promise*), and a teasing one, since we're watching a disastrous family dinner.

The love story is with a Catholic priest, played by Andrew Scott. The turn comes at the end of episode 3. Fleabag makes one of her usual glances to camera, and the Priest notices. He asks where she just went. For the first time, someone inside the story sees the aside.

Reviewers singled this out, and it's worth understanding why it works. For a whole series, the asides were how Fleabag hid. A man who notices them is a man who sees her, which is both what she wants and what she's most afraid of. The formal device has become a plot point. From then on, every glance to camera carries a risk that he'll catch it.

The series ends the same way. After the Priest chooses God, Fleabag walks away alone. The camera starts to follow her; she turns, shakes her head, and goes on without us. The last beat of the love story is her leaving the relationship she began with the audience. She doesn't need the performance any more.

### From stage to screen

The stage show is one woman, one chair, no other actors. Everyone else, including her sister and father, exists only in her description. Adapting it, Waller-Bridge had to decide what to keep:

- **Keep the voice.** The asides carry the monologue's intimacy across.
- **Split the monologue into scenes.** Characters who were only described, like her sister Claire, the father and the godmother, became people who can push back.
- **Keep the spine.** The Boo story is still the avoidance at the centre, revealed at the end.
- **Change details freely.** Some events differ between the play and the series; what transfers is the shape and the voice, not every incident.

*Killing Eve* shows the same voice in a genre piece: the comedy runs underneath a spy thriller, and the two leads' obsession with each other is the real story, as in *Fleabag*'s second series.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Decide who the audience is to your speaker (confidant, jury, friend) and let that relationship change. A monologue that never revises its relationship to the house is a recital. Build the avoidance first, then the jokes around it.
- **Screen:** Direct address needs rules. Establish them in the first minutes, then decide where you'll break them: who notices, when the character stops, when they refuse. Each break is a turn. Use the asides to create a gap between what the character tells us and what we see.
- **Journalism:** The first-person columnist and the personal essayist use the same contract: the reader is a confidant. Waller-Bridge's lesson for nonfiction is the withheld wound: knowing what the piece is really about, and placing that revelation where it changes what came before. Unlike fiction, though, the facts can't be adjusted to suit.

## The pushback

- **Too familiar a device?** Direct address is old: Shakespeare, Restoration comedy, *Alfie* (1966), *Ferris Bueller's Day Off* (1986), *House of Cards*. Some critics found the glances a gimmick that imitators quickly wore out. The defence is what series 2 did with it: a device earns its place when the story turns on it.
- **Whose story?** Some critics pointed out that Fleabag's world is comfortable, white and upper-middle-class, and asked why its frankness about women's sex lives was hailed as new when other writers had been as frank with less attention. That's a fair point about how the show was received, more than about its craft.
- **Trauma as twist.** Withholding a wound to reveal it late can turn grief into a plot device (see the critique in *Want, need and wound*). *Fleabag* largely avoids this because the reveal isn't a twist for its own sake; it explains the comedy we've been laughing at.

## Try it

Take a scene from your work-in-progress with a character who's hiding something painful.

1. Write three asides the character could make to the audience during the scene: jokes or comments that steer us away from what hurts.
2. Write down the wound they're avoiding in one sentence. Decide in which scene the audience will finally learn it.
3. Now write one moment where another character notices the deflection and asks about it. Write what changes in the scene when they do.

- **Stage:** do it as direct address to the house, and decide whether the audience is confidant, jury or friend.
- **Screen:** mark where the character looks into the lens, and where a second character catches the look.
- **Journalism:** for a personal essay, find the joke or digression you use to avoid your real subject, and decide where in the piece you'll stop avoiding it.

**Check:** When the wound is finally revealed, does it make the earlier jokes read differently?

## Check yourself

Q: What job does direct address do in *Fleabag*, beyond comedy?
A: It makes the audience Fleabag's confidant and accomplice, and it's a defence: she turns pain into jokes for us instead of facing it.

Q: Why does it matter that the Priest notices Fleabag's asides in series 2?
A: The device she used to hide is seen by someone inside the story, so the form becomes plot: being seen is the love story.

Q: How does Boo's death work as structure?
A: It's a withheld backstory, revealed in glimpses and fully only in the series 1 finale; it gives the series its central question and makes the earlier comedy read as avoidance.

Q: What carried over from the stage monologue to the TV series?
A: The voice (through direct address) and the spine (a story told around an avoidance), while described characters became people who could push back.

Q: What does the final shot of series 2 say about the device?
A: Fleabag stops the camera following her: she no longer needs the audience she's been performing for.
