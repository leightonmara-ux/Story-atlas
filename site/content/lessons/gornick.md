## Who and when

Vivian Gornick is a New York writer and critic. In the 1970s she was a reporter for *The Village Voice*, where she covered the women's movement. She is best known for *Fierce Attachments* (1987), a memoir of her relationship with her mother, told largely through the long walks the two of them took through Manhattan when Gornick was an adult. It's now considered a classic of the form.

*The Situation and the Story: The Art of Personal Narrative* (2001) grew out of her years teaching nonfiction writing. It's a short book, part craft guide and part literary criticism, built on close readings of essays and memoirs. It's mostly about the personal essay and the memoir, but its central idea applies to almost any kind of writing.

## The big ideas

### The situation and the story

Gornick's distinction is simple and powerful. Every piece of writing has two layers.

- The **situation** is the context or circumstance: what happened, who was there, the plot. A divorce, a parent's illness, a summer job, a trip.
- The **story** is what the writer makes of it: the emotional experience at the center, the understanding the writer has come to.

In her own words, the story is

> the insight, the wisdom, the thing one has come to say.
> — Vivian Gornick, The Situation and the Story

The situation is what the piece is *about* on the surface. The story is what it's *really* about.

### Why this matters

Most weak personal writing, Gornick suggests, has a situation and no story. The writer recounts what happened, often vividly, sometimes painfully, and assumes that the events will carry their own meaning. They don't. A reader can follow the events of a bad marriage with no idea why they're being told, or what the writer now understands that they didn't before.

The story is what makes a reader care about strangers' lives. Two people can write about the same situation, a father's death say, and tell entirely different stories: one about guilt, one about relief, one about discovering who the father was.

### You can't write until you know the story

Gornick argues that the writer often begins with only the situation, and has to write, and rewrite, to discover the story. The first drafts are a search. The piece only comes together when the writer finally understands what they are trying to say, and that understanding then reorganizes everything: what to include, what to cut, where to begin.

This is a cousin of *Jon Franklin*'s "find the resolution first", applied to inner experience. For Franklin, the resolution is an event in the world. For Gornick, it's an insight in the writer.

### The narrator as persona

The second big idea concerns the "I" in personal writing. Gornick insists that the narrator of an essay or memoir is not simply the writer. It's a **persona**, a version of the self built for this particular piece of writing.

The everyday self is confused, defensive and inconsistent. The narrator the reader needs has to be clear-sighted: someone who can tell the truth, including the truth about their own part in what happened. A narrator who is only ever wronged, or who can't see past their own grievances, can't be trusted, and the reader stops listening.

So the writer has to create a narrator who is, in a sense, wiser than they are day to day. Gornick describes this as one of the hardest and most essential acts in personal writing. The persona is a craft achievement, not a confession.

### Detachment

Building that persona requires **detachment**: enough distance from the experience to see it whole. That's why so much personal writing about recent events fails. The writer is still inside the situation and can't yet see the story. Time, or the discipline of revision, provides the distance.

## Use it

- **Journalism:** For personal essays, columns and first-person pieces, write down the situation in one sentence and the story in another. If you can only write the first, you're not ready. For profiles and features too, the same test helps: the assignment is the situation; what you came to understand about this person is the story.
- **Stage:** Solo shows and memory plays live or die on Gornick's distinction. A monologue that recounts events without arriving at an insight feels like testimony; one that has found its story feels like drama. The persona idea also helps: the "I" on stage is a constructed character, even when it's the writer.
- **Screen:** Personal documentaries and voiceover-driven films face the same problem. The footage is the situation. The narration has to supply a story, or the film is a home movie.

## The pushback

- **It's not a structural method.** Gornick offers a way of thinking, not an outline. Writers looking for a step-by-step guide to arranging material will find more in *John McPhee* or *Jack Hart*.
- **Insight can become a formula.** Pushed too far, the demand that every piece "arrive" at wisdom can produce essays that end with a neat epiphany, a moral tacked on. Some readers prefer work that stays inside uncertainty.
- **Persona and truth.** If the narrator is a constructed persona, how far can the construction go? In 2003, after a public talk, *Salon* reported that Gornick had described using composite characters in some of her earlier reporting, an account she disputed. In her published reply she explained that in *Fierce Attachments* she had sometimes combined elements of separate real incidents to move the narrative along. The episode set off a debate about what memoir readers are entitled to expect. Gornick defended the practice as consistent with emotional truth; critics saw it as crossing a line. (See *Janet Malcolm & John Hersey*.)
- **Literary taste.** Her close readings favor a particular kind of introspective, literary essay. Writers of other traditions may find her standards narrow.

## Try it

Take a personal essay, memoir piece or first-person draft you're working on.

1. Write one sentence for the situation: what happened.
2. Write one sentence for the story: what you have come to understand that you didn't before. If you can't, freewrite for ten minutes on why this matters to you now, and look for that sentence in what you wrote.
3. Reread the draft and mark what serves the story. Cut or compress what only recounts the situation.
4. Find a passage where the narrator is only ever wronged, and rewrite it so they see their own part.

- **Stage:** for a solo show or memory play, write both sentences for the "I" on stage, and treat that "I" as a character.
- **Screen:** for a personal documentary, write the story sentence before you write any narration.
- **Journalism:** for a profile, the assignment is the situation; write a sentence for what you came to understand about this person.

**Check:** Could a stranger read your story sentence and know why the piece is worth their time?

## Check yourself

Q: What is the difference between the situation and the story?
A: The situation is the context or circumstance, what happened. The story is the emotional experience or insight the writer makes of it, what the piece is really about.

Q: Why does much weak personal writing fail, in Gornick's view?
A: It has a situation but no story. The writer recounts events and assumes their meaning is obvious, without arriving at an insight.

Q: What does Gornick mean by the narrator as persona?
A: The "I" of an essay or memoir is a constructed version of the writer, clear-sighted enough to tell the truth, including about their own part.

Q: Why is detachment important?
A: The writer needs enough distance from an experience to see it whole and find the story in it. Writing from inside the situation usually produces only the situation.

Q: How does Gornick's idea relate to Franklin's?
A: Both say you need to know where the piece arrives before it works. For Franklin it's a resolution in the world; for Gornick, an insight in the writer.
