## Where it comes from

Braiding means telling two or more stories in alternation, so that each changes how we read the others. It's as old as the novel with several plots (Tolstoy cuts between Anna and Levin in *Anna Karenina*), and film made it a basic tool: D. W. Griffith's *Intolerance* (1916) weaves four stories from four eras. *Editing* covers the film technique, cross-cutting.

In nonfiction, "braided" became a teaching term through the essay. The writer Brenda Miller popularized the **braided essay** in creative-writing teaching, notably in *Tell It Slant* (2003, with Suzanne Paola). Jo Ann Beard's "The Fourth State of Matter" (*The New Yorker*, 1996), which twists a mass shooting at her workplace together with her dying dog and her collapsing marriage, is the example most often taught.

This lesson looks at three book-length braids, each built a different way:

- **Isabel Wilkerson**, *The Warmth of Other Suns* (2010): three migrants' lives, side by side.
- **Erik Larson**, *The Devil in the White City* (2003): two men in one city who never meet.
- **David Grann**, *Killers of the Flower Moon* (2017): three viewpoints in sequence, the last one the author's own.

## The big ideas

### Choose strands that cover the ground

*The Warmth of Other Suns* tells the story of the Great Migration, when about six million Black Americans left the South between roughly 1915 and 1970. Wilkerson interviewed more than 1,200 people and chose three to carry the book. She has explained that she wanted one person for each of the migration's main streams:

- **Ida Mae Gladney**, a sharecropper's wife who left Mississippi in 1937 and made her life in Chicago;
- **George Starling**, a fruit picker who fled Florida for Harlem in 1945, after organizing other pickers made him a target;
- **Robert Foster**, a surgeon who drove from Louisiana to Los Angeles in 1953.

Three routes, three decades, three classes and temperaments. Together they map the whole movement; separately, each is one person you can follow. Between their chapters Wilkerson sets shorter passages of history and data, so the reader moves between the life and the larger picture.

The lesson is that strands are chosen, not found. Each one should cover ground the others can't. If two strands show the same thing, cut one.

### Alternate, and let the cuts do work

Larson's *The Devil in the White City* follows two men in Chicago in the years before the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition. Daniel Burnham, the architect in charge, races to build a fair on swampy lakefront in impossible time. H. H. Holmes, a con man and murderer, builds a hotel near the fairgrounds and preys on young women who come to the city.

The chapters alternate between them. Larson's opening note frames the book as a story of the White City and the Black, daylight and darkness, and the alternation makes that contrast felt rather than stated. A chapter of committees, deadlines and rain is followed by a chapter of quiet horror, and each colors the other: the fair's ambition looks shadowed, the murders look like the dark side of the same booming city.

Each switch is also a chance for a hook. Leaving one strand at a moment of tension and cutting to the other makes the reader read the second strand faster to get back to the first. That's the cross-cutting of a chase sequence, slowed down to the length of chapters.

### Convergence or contrast

Strands relate in two basic ways.

**Convergence:** the strands are heading toward each other, and the reader waits for them to meet. Grann's three chronicles converge on one truth; the lines of a heist film converge on the job.

**Contrast:** the strands never touch, and their meaning comes from the comparison. Burnham and Holmes never meet in Larson's book. Wilkerson's three migrants never meet either. The braid works because the reader holds them side by side.

Know which you're writing. A reader who expects convergence and doesn't get it feels cheated; a reader who senses a contrast early can enjoy the echoes without waiting for a collision.

### Braid in sequence: shifting the point of view

Grann's *Killers of the Flower Moon* is about the murders of members of the Osage Nation in Oklahoma in the 1920s, when oil had made the Osage rich and white neighbors schemed to take their wealth. Grann tells it in three parts he calls chronicles:

1. **"The Marked Woman"**: Mollie Burkhart, an Osage woman whose family is killed one by one.
2. **"The Evidence Man"**: Tom White, the former Texas Ranger sent by the young Bureau of Investigation (later the FBI) to solve the case.
3. **"The Reporter"**: Grann himself, decades later, going through archives and talking to descendants.

This is a braid laid end to end. Each chronicle re-sees what came before. The first is a story of terror from inside; the second looks like a classic detective story, ending with convictions; the third undoes that tidy ending, as Grann finds evidence that the killing was far wider than the Bureau's case admitted, and that many more people were complicit. Grann has said that finding this structure was one of the essential problems of the book.

The final shift is the boldest move: the author steps into the story, and his investigation becomes the last strand. It works because the reader has been given a solution and is ready to have it questioned.

### Pacing the braid

A few practical rules follow from these books:

- **Keep strands distinct.** Different places, periods or voices help the reader know where they are at the start of each section.
- **Vary the length.** Strict alternation (A, B, A, B) is clear but can feel mechanical. Let a strand run longer when it's hot; cut short when it's slow.
- **Cut on tension.** Leave a strand at a question, not at a resting point.
- **Echo across the cut.** An image, word or situation that repeats from one strand to the next tells the reader the strands belong together.
- **Bring them home.** Whether they converge or not, the ending should gather all the strands, even if only by setting them side by side one last time.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Theatre can braid in a single space. Tom Stoppard's *Arcadia* (1993) alternates between scenes set in 1809 and the present day in the same room of an English country house; in the final scene both periods share the stage at once, the characters of two centuries moving around each other. That's convergence without meeting.
- **Screen:** Christopher Nolan's *Dunkirk* (2017) braids three strands on three clocks, a week on the beach, a day at sea and an hour in the air, cutting among them until they converge in the rescue. *The Godfather Part II* (1974) alternates the young Vito's rise with Michael's hardening, a contrast braid across half a century. For a script, write the strands separately first, then intercut.
- **Journalism:** A feature or series can braid two or three people facing the same problem, or a past event and the present-day reporting on it, as Grann does. Choose strands that each show something the others can't, and tell the reader early why they're side by side.

## The pushback

- **Imposed meaning.** Alternation implies connection. Placing a murderer next to an architect suggests they share something, a claim the reader may absorb without the writer ever arguing it. Critics of braided nonfiction warn that the cut can assert what the evidence doesn't.
- **Reconstructed scenes.** Larson writes some of Holmes's actions in vivid detail, based on trial records and contemporary accounts. Holmes's own confessions were unreliable, his victim count is disputed, and the lurid "murder castle" of popular retellings owes much to sensational newspapers of the 1890s. Readers should know where reconstruction begins.
- **The reader gets lost.** Too many strands, or strands too alike, and readers lose track and stop caring. Three is common for a reason.
- **Whose story?** Grann's choice to make the last chronicle his own investigation was praised, but some readers have asked how far a story of Osage suffering should end with an outside reporter's discovery. Who tells the story was also debated around Martin Scorsese's 2023 film, with Osage voices arguing for their own perspective at the center.

## Try it

Take a project with more than one character, timeline or thread.

1. Write each strand in one sentence. Next to each, write what it shows that the others can't. Cut or merge any strand without an answer.
2. Decide: do the strands converge, or work by contrast? Write the point where they meet, or the final image that sets them side by side.
3. Lay out your sections as a sequence of letters (A, B, A, C...). Mark every switch that happens at a resting point, and move it to a moment of tension.
4. Find one echo, an image or line, to carry across a switch.

- **Stage:** try *Arcadia*'s trick: two periods in one set. Which object can belong to both?
- **Screen:** cut-test it on index cards, one color per strand. Is any color absent for too long?
- **Journalism:** braid a person's story with the history behind it, alternating scene and context.

**Check:** If you deleted one strand, would the others lose meaning?

## Check yourself

Q: How did Wilkerson choose the three lives in *The Warmth of Other Suns*?
A: From more than 1,200 interviews, she chose one migrant for each main stream of the Great Migration, so together they cover the whole movement.

Q: What does alternating Burnham and Holmes achieve, given they never meet?
A: Contrast: the fair and the murders color each other, building the book's light and dark without a single meeting.

Q: What's the difference between a convergence braid and a contrast braid?
A: In convergence the strands head toward a meeting the reader waits for; in contrast they never touch, and meaning comes from comparing them.

Q: How does the structure of *Killers of the Flower Moon* work?
A: Three chronicles from different viewpoints in sequence: the victim's family, the investigator, then Grann's own reporting, which overturns the tidy ending of the second.

Q: Where should you switch strands?
A: At a moment of tension, so the reader is pulled through the next strand to get back.
