## Who and when

**Christopher Booker** (1937–2019) was a British journalist, best known as the founding editor of the satirical magazine *Private Eye* in 1961 and later as a long-running newspaper columnist. He was not an academic, and his big book is an outsider's project.

***The Seven Basic Plots: Why We Tell Stories*** (2004) runs to more than 700 pages. Booker said he worked on it for 34 years. It surveys everything from *Gilgamesh* and Greek myth to Dickens, Tolstoy and Hollywood, and argues that beneath the surface variety of stories lie a handful of deep patterns, and a reason we keep telling them.

The book has two halves. The first describes the plots; the second explains, through the psychology of **Carl Jung**, what Booker thinks they are for, and attacks much modern fiction for forgetting it.

## The big ideas

### The seven plots

| Plot | The pattern | Classic examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Overcoming the Monster** | A hero confronts a deadly force threatening a community and destroys it | *Beowulf*, *Dracula*, *Jaws* |
| **Rags to Riches** | A humble, overlooked figure rises, loses everything at a central crisis, then wins through to a full and deserved fulfilment | *Cinderella*, *Jane Eyre*, *David Copperfield* |
| **The Quest** | A hero and companions set out for a distant goal and win it through ordeals | *The Lord of the Rings*, *Watership Down* |
| **Voyage and Return** | A hero leaves the familiar world for a strange one, is first enchanted, then threatened, then escapes home, changed or not | *Alice in Wonderland*, *The Wizard of Oz*, *Robinson Crusoe* |
| **Comedy** | Confusion and misunderstanding keep people apart until a recognition brings everything to light and they're united | Shakespeare's comedies, Jane Austen |
| **Tragedy** | A hero is tempted into a dark course of action, which goes well, then wrong, then ends in destruction | *Macbeth*, *Anna Karenina*, *Bonnie and Clyde* |
| **Rebirth** | A hero falls under a dark power and into a kind of living death, until a redemptive act frees them | *A Christmas Carol*, *Sleeping Beauty* |

Some of these names were already in use; Booker's contribution is the set, the stage-by-stage anatomy of each, and the claim that they share one underlying structure. His Comedy and Tragedy overlap with the oldest division in drama (see *Tragedy and comedy*), and his Quest with *Joseph Campbell & Christopher Vogler*.

Later in the book he adds two more: **Rebellion Against "The One"**, in which a hero resists an all-powerful system or ruler, and **Mystery**, the detective story. He treats both as later, more limited forms than the original seven.

### The five stages

Booker's most usable idea for working writers is that each plot moves through recognizable stages. Overcoming the Monster runs like this:

1. **Anticipation Stage and Call.** We learn of the monster, and the hero is called to face it.
2. **Dream Stage.** Preparations go well; the threat still seems far away and the hero feels equal to it.
3. **Frustration Stage.** The first real confrontation. The monster's full power shows and the hero is on the back foot.
4. **Nightmare Stage.** The final ordeal. All seems lost.
5. **Thrilling Escape from Death, and Death of the Monster.** A last-minute reversal; the hero wins and the community is freed.

Watch *Jaws* (1975) with this in mind. The shark's attacks and the beach closures are the call; the hunt sets out with banter and bravado; the shark proves far bigger than expected; Quint is eaten and the *Orca* sinks; and Brody, alone on the mast, fires at the oxygen tank in the shark's jaws.

Tragedy uses the same frame turned inside out. Booker's stages are **Anticipation** (the hero feels unfulfilled and fixes on a forbidden goal), **Dream** (it goes well: Macbeth is king), **Frustration** (things start to slip; more crimes are needed), **Nightmare** (forces close in and the hero grows desperate) and **Destruction or Death Wish**. The Dream Stage is the key: tragedy lets its hero, and us, enjoy getting away with it before the bill arrives.

### The Jungian frame

Booker reads all of this through Jung. The monster, the tyrant, the dark power are projections of the **ego**: self-centred, power-hungry, cut off. The happy ending stands for wholeness, often imaged as the union of hero and heroine, in which masculine and feminine qualities are brought into balance and the hero comes into his true self. A story resolves properly, for Booker, when the light defeats the dark and the hero matures.

### Lost the plot

In the second half Booker argues that from roughly the nineteenth century, much Western storytelling broke away from these archetypes. Stories became ego-driven: dark figures win, heroes fail to mature, endings refuse to resolve, sex and violence stand in for meaning. In effect, he argues, modern fiction lost the plot. His examples of failure include many works the critical tradition counts among its greatest.

## Use it

- **Screen:** The five stages are an excellent diagnostic for genre films. If your second act drags, check whether you skipped the Dream Stage: the audience needs the hero to feel equal to the task before the Frustration Stage bites. If the climax feels flat, check whether the Nightmare Stage went deep enough.
- **Stage:** Booker's Tragedy is a practical rehearsal map for a fall. Let the Dream Stage play as genuine pleasure; the audience's complicity is what makes the Nightmare hurt.
- **Journalism:** Many investigations are Overcoming the Monster with the reporter or the source as hero; many business profiles are Rags to Riches. Notice when the archetype is doing your thinking: real people rarely fit the monster role cleanly, and the five-stage arc can tempt you to stage a Nightmare the reporting doesn't show.

## The pushback

The reception was sharp. Reviewers generally admired the first half's range and stamina and balked at the second.

- **Michiko Kakutani**, in *The New York Times* (2005), found the first half informative but the second increasingly tendentious.
- **Adam Mars-Jones**, in *The Observer* (2004), pointed out that Booker's criteria end up dismissing a large part of the canon, from Proust and Joyce to Kafka, while praising far slighter works that happen to fit the patterns.
- **Too flexible to fail.** With categories this broad, almost any story fits somewhere, and Booker sometimes reads works selectively to make them fit. Critics also noted the overlap between plots: many stories are a Quest, a Voyage and Return and a Rebirth at once.
- **Taste disguised as theory.** Using Jungian wholeness to rank stories turns a description of what stories often do into a rule for what they ought to do, and the rule happens to favour a conservative, happy-ending view of art.

## Read next

- Christopher Booker, *The Seven Basic Plots: Why We Tell Stories* (2004). Read Part One and the chapter on each plot; skim the rest.
- Compare *Northrop Frye* for an earlier, more scholarly attempt to map all plots, and *Georges Polti* for a list of situations rather than shapes.
- Watch *Jaws* and *The Wizard of Oz* back to back: Overcoming the Monster and Voyage and Return in their purest forms.

## Try it
Take your work-in-progress, or a film you know well.
1. Pick the one of Booker's seven plots it's closest to. Name the decisive clue in a sentence.
2. Write one line for each of the five stages as your story currently plays them. Leave a blank where a stage is missing.
3. For each blank, ask: did I skip it on purpose? If not, draft the missing scene, most often the Dream Stage or the Nightmare.
4. Now ask the critics' question: is anything in your story true because it breaks the pattern? Protect it.

- **Stage:** In a tragedy, mark the exact scene where the Dream Stage turns to Frustration. Is the turn visible onstage?
- **Screen:** Time the stages against your page count. Does any one take too long?
- **Journalism:** Map an investigation onto Overcoming the Monster, then list every fact that doesn't fit the monster role.

**Check:** Can you name the scene for each of the five stages, and say why any missing one is missing?

## Check yourself

Q: Name Booker's seven basic plots.
A: Overcoming the Monster, Rags to Riches, the Quest, Voyage and Return, Comedy, Tragedy and Rebirth.

Q: What are the five stages of Overcoming the Monster?
A: Anticipation Stage and Call, Dream Stage, Frustration Stage, Nightmare Stage, and Thrilling Escape from Death and Death of the Monster.

Q: What distinguishes Voyage and Return from the Quest?
A: In Voyage and Return the hero falls into a strange world and the point is getting home; in the Quest the hero sets out deliberately for a distant goal and the point is winning it.

Q: What psychological theory frames Booker's argument?
A: Jung's: the dark figures stand for the ego, and the happy ending for the hero's growth into wholeness.

Q: What was the main criticism of the book?
A: That its second half turned description into prescription, using the plots to dismiss much of modern literature, and that its categories are broad enough to fit almost anything.
