## Who and when

Aaron Sorkin (born 1961) grew up in New York and studied musical theatre at Syracuse University, meaning to be an actor. He has often said in interviews that he fell for the sound of dialogue as a boy, taken to Broadway shows, before he could follow what the plays were about.

His first big success was on stage. *A Few Good Men* opened on Broadway in 1989. It grew out of a story his sister Deborah, a Navy lawyer, told him about defending Marines in a hazing case. Rob Reiner's film followed in 1992. Then came television (*Sports Night*, 1998–2000; *The West Wing*, 1999–2006, whose first four seasons he wrote; *The Newsroom*, 2012–2014) and films, including *The Social Network* (2010), adapted from Ben Mezrich's book *The Accidental Billionaires* and winner of the Oscar for adapted screenplay, *Moneyball* (2011, with Steven Zaillian) and *Steve Jobs* (2015).

He has taught his method widely, above all in his online MasterClass (2016) and in many interviews. It comes down to two words.

## The big ideas

### Intention and obstacle

Sorkin's rule: **somebody wants something, and something is standing in the way of getting it.** That's a scene, an episode, a film. He calls it the drive shaft of drama, and says he worships at its altar.

The words are his, but he doesn't claim the idea. He sends writers to Aristotle's *Poetics*, which he calls the rule book (see *Aristotle*). Be careful, though: Aristotle never uses the phrase "intention and obstacle". It's Sorkin's shorthand for Aristotle's point that drama is an imitation of action. The vocabulary also comes from the acting class Sorkin trained in, where an actor looks for the character's **objective** and what blocks it (see *Konstantin Stanislavski*).

What makes the rule useful is how blunt it is. Two questions, asked of any scene:

1. What does this character want, here, now?
2. What's stopping them?

If you can't answer both, the scene is probably talk. Sorkin's advice is to make both as strong as you can. A weak want ("she'd like to chat") or a soft obstacle ("he's a bit busy") produces scenes nobody remembers.

### The tactic is the story

The third piece is the **tactic**: what the character does to get past the obstacle. Flatter, bully, bargain, lie, change the subject, walk out. Sorkin made the point in a 2016 Reddit AMA: the character's tactics for overcoming the obstacle *are* your story.

This is also where character comes from. Sorkin follows Aristotle in putting action first: we learn who people are from the choices they make in pursuit of what they want, not from backstory. Two characters with the same intention and the same obstacle become different people the moment they pick different tactics.

A good scene usually shows tactics *changing*. The first one fails, so the character tries a second, riskier one, then a third. Each switch is a small turn, and the audience leans in to see what they'll try next.

### Watch it work: *A Few Good Men*

The climax of *A Few Good Men* is a clean demonstration. *Dialogue reveals character* describes the scene, so here's the machinery.

- **Intention:** the defence lawyer, Kaffee, needs Colonel Jessup to admit on the record that he ordered the "Code Red", the punishment that killed a Marine.
- **Obstacle:** Jessup will never simply confess, Kaffee has no proof, and accusing a senior officer without it could end Kaffee's career.
- **Tactics:** Kaffee starts politely, catches Jessup in small contradictions, then stops being polite and goads him, betting that Jessup's pride is stronger than his caution. The bet pays: Jessup's contempt boils over into a speech, and the speech contains the admission.

The famous line, "You can't handle the truth!", is the moment the obstacle breaks.

### Rhythm, and the walk and talk

Sorkin writes dialogue like music: fast exchanges, words batted back and forth, long sentences landing on a hard final beat. *Dialogue reveals character* covers the style, and the glossary's **Walk and talk** entry covers the technique he made famous on *Sports Night* and *The West Wing* with director Thomas Schlamme.

What's worth adding here is how the rhythm serves intention and obstacle. In a walk and talk, the destination is a deadline: the character has until the end of the corridor to get what they want. The speed isn't decoration. It's two people with clashing intentions, each trying to win before time runs out.

Watch the opening of *The Social Network*. Mark and his girlfriend Erica sit in a crowded bar near Harvard, talking for about five minutes in two overlapping conversations. Mark's intention is to be taken seriously, and he keeps steering back to the exclusive final clubs he wants to get into. Erica's intention, increasingly, is to get out. Each is the other's obstacle. His tactics (showing off, correcting her, condescending) make her obstacle bigger with every line, until she ends the relationship and walks out. The whole film is set up in one scene.

### Writing for the stage first

Sorkin learned his craft in the theatre, and it shows. On stage there are no car chases, so talk must *be* action. A courtroom suits him perfectly: every witness is someone with something to hide, facing someone who wants it out.

He carries the courtroom into his screen work. *The Social Network* is built around two depositions, from lawsuits brought against Mark Zuckerberg, and the film cuts between those rooms and the story of Facebook's founding. The depositions give a scattered true story a clear frame: two sides, each wanting something, in a room where someone will have to answer. He returned to the stage with an adaptation of *To Kill a Mockingbird* on Broadway (2018).

## Use it

- **Stage:** Before rehearsal, write each character's intention and obstacle at the top of every scene. If two characters want things that don't clash, add a clash or cut the scene. Give actors tactics to play, not feelings.
- **Screen:** Use a destination as a deadline: a door to reach, a vote in ten minutes, a train leaving. Exposition goes down easier when it's a weapon in somebody's argument. And find a frame, like the depositions, that turns a sprawling story into a contest.
- **Journalism:** Every source in a narrative piece wants something, and something is in their way; that's your story's engine (compare *Jon Franklin*'s complication and resolution). The interview itself is a scene with intention and obstacle: you want the truth, they want to look good. But don't borrow Sorkin's licence with facts. Dialogue you didn't hear isn't yours to write.

## The pushback

- **Everyone sounds like Sorkin.** Critics complain that his characters share one voice: quick, articulate, a little superior. Fans have cut together videos of phrases and exchanges he reuses from show to show.
- **Speechifying.** His characters often deliver set-piece speeches about values, which can feel like the writer lecturing through a mouthpiece, especially in *The Newsroom*.
- **Women at the edges.** *The Social Network* and *The Newsroom* were criticised for their women characters. Sorkin replied that the film depicted men behaving badly rather than endorsing them, but the criticism stuck.
- **Truth versus story.** *The Social Network* compresses and invents; Erica, for example, is a fictional character. Sorkin has said openly that his loyalty is to storytelling rather than to the record. That's a choice dramatists are entitled to make, but it's a warning for anyone writing fact.
- **Not every story is a contest.** Intention and obstacle suits drama built on conflict. Stories of drift, mood or community (see *Anton Chekhov* and *Kishōtenketsu*) need other tools.

## Try it

Take a scene from your draft that feels flat.

1. At the top, write one line for each character: *wants ___, blocked by ___*. If you can't fill in both blanks, that's the problem.
2. Strengthen the weaker side: make the want more urgent or the obstacle harder.
3. List the tactics the main character uses, as verbs. If there's only one, have it fail halfway through and make them switch to a riskier one.
4. Add a deadline, even a small one: someone's about to leave.

- **Stage:** do it for a two-hander, giving each actor a list of tactics to play.
- **Screen:** stage the rewritten scene as a walk and talk, with the destination as the deadline.
- **Journalism:** for a profile, write what your subject wanted at the key moment and what stood in their way. Is that tension in your first five paragraphs?

**Check:** Could an actor tell you, line by line, what their character is trying to get and how?

## Check yourself

Q: What is Sorkin's rule of intention and obstacle?
A: Somebody wants something, and something stands in their way. Every scene, and every story, needs both.

Q: What's a tactic, and why does Sorkin care about it?
A: It's what a character does to get past the obstacle. The choice of tactics reveals character, and the sequence of tactics is the story.

Q: Where does Sorkin say the idea comes from, and what's the caveat?
A: He credits Aristotle's *Poetics*, but the phrase "intention and obstacle" is his own shorthand, close to the actor's language of objectives.

Q: How does a walk and talk serve intention and obstacle?
A: The destination acts as a deadline: characters have until they arrive to get what they want, so speed becomes pressure.

Q: What's the most common criticism of Sorkin's writing?
A: That every character sounds like Sorkin, and that they make speeches rather than talk.
