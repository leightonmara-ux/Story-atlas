## Who and when

Charlie Kaufman (born 1958) is an American screenwriter and director. He started out writing television comedy in the early 1990s, including the sitcom *Get a Life*, and broke through with *Being John Malkovich* (1999), directed by Spike Jonze. *Adaptation* (2002), also with Jonze, and *Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind* (2004), directed by Michel Gondry, followed. *Eternal Sunshine* won the Oscar for original screenplay, shared with Gondry and the artist Pierre Bismuth, who had the original idea with them. Kaufman then directed his own scripts: *Synecdoche, New York* (2008), *Anomalisa* (2015, co-directed with Duke Johnson) and *I'm Thinking of Ending Things* (2020). He also published a novel, *Antkind* (2020).

On 30 September 2011 he gave the last of that year's BAFTA and BFI Screenwriters' Lectures, at BFI Southbank in London. It was described as his first public lecture. The video and a full transcript are on BAFTA's website, and it has become one of the most widely shared talks about writing. This lesson paraphrases it; read the transcript for his exact words.

## The big ideas

### No tips

Kaufman began by declining to do what such lectures usually do. He didn't offer a method or a set of rules. Instead he talked about how hard it is to make something that doesn't exist yet, including his own uncertainty about what to say that evening, and let that uncertainty be part of the talk.

That's a lesson in itself. Kaufman's position is that a formula can only produce what's already been made. If you want to write something new, the not-knowing isn't a phase to get through quickly; it's where the work happens.

### Say who you are

The heart of the lecture is a plea for honesty. Be honest about who you are, he told the audience: **say who you are**, in your life and in your work. Your writing will be a record of your time and of you, and it may reach someone, now or long after, who feels lost and needs to know that someone else felt this way too.

Notice what he's *not* saying. "Write what you know" (see the glossary) is about subject matter. Kaufman's films are about portals into actors' heads and companies that erase memories, which nobody knows. What he means is closer to *Jonathan Franzen*'s "autobiography by invention": the strange premise is a way of telling the truth about your own anxieties, longings and failures. Being vulnerable on the page means letting those show, especially the parts that embarrass you.

### Failure is the price of trying

He told writers not to fear failure, calling it a badge of honour, because it means you took a risk. A script that plays it safe can't fail interestingly. This sits behind everything else in the lecture: you can't say who you are and also guarantee success.

### Against the marketplace

Kaufman was sharp about a film business that tests and markets stories like products and treats audiences as consumers to be managed. He asked writers to resist being used that way: to make something that respects people's intelligence rather than something designed to sell. That's his quarrel with formula. Not that structure is bad, but that structure applied because it sells is a way of not saying who you are.

### *Adaptation*: the fight with the rules, on screen

*Adaptation* turns that quarrel into a film, and it's the best place to see what Kaufman means.

A screenwriter called Charlie Kaufman (Nicolas Cage) is hired to adapt Susan Orlean's *The Orchid Thief* (1998), a book about an orchid poacher in Florida with no real plot. Early on, over lunch with a studio executive, Charlie says he wants to stay true to the book: no sex, no guns, no car chases, no characters learning big life lessons. Then he can't write it. The film shows his block, his self-loathing voice-over, and his growing insertion of himself into the script.

Meanwhile his invented twin brother Donald, also played by Cage, takes *Robert McKee*'s *Story* seminar and cheerfully writes a formulaic serial-killer thriller that sells. Desperate, Charlie goes to the seminar himself. McKee (Brian Cox) scorns his idea that in real life nothing much happens, and tells him to wow the audience in the end. The joke is in the sound mix: McKee warns against voice-over while we're hearing Charlie's.

The last act then becomes exactly what Charlie swore off: drugs, sex, guns, a car chase, a death in a swamp, and a character learning a life lesson. It plays as if Donald wrote it. On screen, Donald Kaufman shares the writing credit, and the fictional twin was nominated for the Oscar alongside his "brother".

Is the ending a parody of the rules or a surrender to them? Critics still argue. The best answer may be both: the film shows a writer discovering he can't escape structure, and shows it so knowingly that the formula becomes its subject.

### Structure is the meaning

Kaufman doesn't reject structure; he invents it for each story, so the shape of the film carries its idea. Three examples:

- ***Being John Malkovich*.** A failing puppeteer finds a small door in an office building that leads into the actor John Malkovich's head for fifteen minutes. The wish to be someone else, which the puppeteer already acts out with his puppets, becomes a literal place.
- ***Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind*.** Most of the film takes place inside Joel's (Jim Carrey) mind while his memories of Clementine (Kate Winslet) are erased, newest first. Halfway through, he decides he wants to keep them, and runs with her through his memories trying to hide her. The opening, where the two meet on a train from Montauk and the titles only roll after it, turns out to come *after* the erasure. The structure asks its question directly: knowing how it ended, would you do it again?
- ***Synecdoche, New York*.** A theatre director (Philip Seymour Hoffman) builds a full-scale replica of New York in a warehouse for a play about his own life, then casts an actor to play himself, and later an actor to play that actor. Years pass within scenes. The form is the anxiety: the attempt to capture a whole life swallows the life.

The lesson for writers isn't "be weird". It's to ask what shape your particular story needs, rather than which template it fits.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Theatre welcomes self-awareness, and plays about the making of plays go back centuries. If your play stalls, try putting your own struggle with it on stage, but only if that struggle is the real subject. Ask whether your structure expresses the play's idea or just organizes it.
- **Screen:** Before borrowing a beat sheet, ask what your premise does to time, memory or identity, and build the structure from that. If you do use the conventions, use them knowingly. *Adaptation* shows a formula can be a subject.
- **Journalism:** Being honest and being vulnerable aren't the same as inserting yourself into the story. In a personal essay or first-person feature, Kaufman's question applies: are you saying who you really are, or performing a version of yourself? In reporting, the equivalent courage is admitting what you don't know (see *Vivian Gornick* and *Joan Didion*).

## The pushback

- **Solipsism.** The commonest complaint is that Kaufman's work is self-absorbed: films about Charlie Kaufman's anxiety, by Charlie Kaufman. *Synecdoche, New York* in particular divided critics; some called it a masterpiece, others impenetrable.
- **Easy for him to say.** Resisting the marketplace is harder for a writer without Oscar-winning films. Most screenwriters work to commission, and a lecture against formula can feel like advice for the already successful.
- **He needs the rules too.** *Adaptation* works partly because it uses the structure it mocks, and *Eternal Sunshine* is, underneath, a love story with a clear want and a clear clock.
- **Honesty isn't a craft.** Sincerity doesn't guarantee a good script. Saying who you are still needs scenes that work.

## Try it

Take a project you're stuck on or one you've been writing safely.

1. Write one honest paragraph about what scares or embarrasses you in your own life that this story is secretly about. Don't show anyone.
2. Find the scene that's furthest from that paragraph, the one that's there because stories "need" one, and rewrite it so the fear is in it.
3. Ask whether your structure could express the idea. If it's about memory, could it run out of order? If it's about losing yourself, could the form lose itself?

- **Stage:** try one scene where the play becomes aware of itself, and keep it only if it tells the truth.
- **Screen:** take the most conventional beat in your outline and either cut it or let the film know it's conventional.
- **Journalism:** in a first-person piece, find the sentence where you're performing and replace it with what you actually felt.

**Check:** Is there anything in the draft you'd be uneasy for someone who knows you to read?

## Check yourself

Q: What was the central plea of Kaufman's 2011 BAFTA lecture?
A: To be honest: say who you are, in your life and your work, so your writing is a true record that might reach someone who feels lost.

Q: How is "say who you are" different from "write what you know"?
A: It's about emotional honesty, not subject matter. Kaufman's premises are invented, but they carry his real anxieties.

Q: How does *Adaptation* dramatize the fight with McKee's rules?
A: Charlie refuses formula and can't write; his twin follows McKee and sells a thriller; and the film's last act turns into the very formula Charlie swore off.

Q: Why does *Eternal Sunshine* tell its story inside an erasure, running backwards?
A: Because the structure is the question: the film shows a love ending first, then asks whether, knowing how it ends, you'd do it again.

Q: What's the strongest objection to Kaufman's position?
A: That it can turn solipsistic, and that refusing the marketplace is easier for an established writer; his best films also rely on the conventions he criticizes.
