## Where it comes from

A film audience sees only what the camera shows. A theatre audience can look anywhere, all the time. So the stage has had to invent its own ways of setting a mood and telling people where to look: light, sound, space and the arrangement of bodies.

For most of theatre's history, light was a necessity: daylight for Greek and Elizabethan plays, then candles, oil and gas indoors. Electric light, which spread through theatres from the 1880s, made it possible to shape, colour and change light during a performance.

Two designers saw what that meant. **Adolphe Appia** (1862–1928), a Swiss designer, argued in ***Music and the Art of the Theatre*** (published in German in 1899 as *Die Musik und die Inscenierung*) that flat painted scenery should give way to three-dimensional spaces of steps and platforms, and that light, changing like music, should be the force that unifies a production. **Edward Gordon Craig** (1872–1966), English actor, designer and son of the actress Ellen Terry, pushed for simplified, suggestive settings and for a single artist in charge of the whole. His essays were collected in ***On the Art of the Theatre*** (1911).

Their ideas crossed to the United States as the **New Stagecraft** of the 1910s and 1920s, led by designers such as Robert Edmond Jones. And lighting design became a profession of its own partly through **Jean Rosenthal** (1912–1969), who lit Martha Graham's dances for decades and the original Broadway *West Side Story* (1957). Her memoir, ***The Magic of Light***, appeared in 1972.

Today the whole visual and spatial design of a show is often called **scenography**, a term associated with the Czech designer Josef Svoboda, who mixed projection, light and live performance.

## The big ideas

### Light chooses where you look

On stage, light does the camera's job. The eye goes to the brightest area, so lighting directs attention: a tight **special** on one face, a **follow spot** that tracks a singer, a slow narrowing until only one person is lit. Light also tells time and place (dawn, a streetlamp, a fire) and sets mood through angle, colour and intensity.

Light can be part of the story too. In Tennessee Williams's ***A Streetcar Named Desire*** (1947), Blanche covers the bare bulb in the apartment with a paper lantern, so no one will see her in harsh light. In scene 9, near the end, Mitch tears the lantern off and holds her face to the bulb. A lighting cue becomes the play's cruelest action.

### Sound and music cues

Sound design creates offstage worlds (traffic, rain, a party next door), marks time and carries mood. A single sound can hold a play's meaning.

In Chekhov's ***The Cherry Orchard*** (1904), in Act 2, the characters hear a distant sound like a string snapping, dying away, sad. No one can say what it is. At the very end, as the house is locked up, the sound comes again, followed by the strokes of an axe on the cherry trees. (See *Anton Chekhov*.)

Music cues work the same way. *Death of a Salesman* (1949) opens with a melody played on a flute; *Streetcar* calls for a "blue piano" from a bar around the corner. Writers can put these in stage directions, sparingly.

### Blocking and stage pictures

**Blocking** is the director's plan for where actors stand and move. Every moment makes a **stage picture**, and the picture tells the audience who matters, who holds power and who is alone.

Directors often treat some positions as stronger than others: centre stage, nearer the audience, higher up, facing out. A character isolated on one side while others cluster on the other reads as an outsider without a word. A cross toward or away from someone is an action.

### Upstage and the art of focus

**Upstage** means the back of the stage, away from the audience; **downstage** is the front. The words come from **raked** stages that sloped upward toward the back, so the back was literally *up*.

An actor who moves upstage of a scene partner forces the partner to turn away from the audience to talk to them. That's where **upstaging** comes from, now a word for stealing attention anywhere. Directors use the same geometry on purpose: place the character you want us to watch upstage, facing out, and the others must turn toward them.

### The blackout

A **blackout**, cutting all the stage light at once, is theatre's hard cut. It can end a scene on a punch, hide a change, or leave an image burning in the dark. A slow fade lets a moment linger instead. Short comic sketches that end on a sudden blackout gave the name **blackout sketch**. Choose deliberately: the last image before darkness is what the audience carries into the next scene.

### The empty space

The director **Peter Brook** began his book ***The Empty Space*** (1968) with the line "I can take any empty space and call it a bare stage." A person walking across it while someone watches, he argued, is all theatre needs. His book is short and worth reading whole.

The bare stage is a tool, not a lack. With almost nothing, the audience's imagination does the building, and every object that *is* there becomes significant. Thornton Wilder's ***Our Town*** (1938) plays on a nearly bare stage with a few tables and chairs; actors mime their props, and a Stage Manager talks directly to us.

### Direct address

Theatre can do what film rarely does: speak straight to the audience. Shakespeare's soliloquies, Wilder's Stage Manager, Tom narrating *The Glass Menagerie* (1944): direct address shares a secret, sets a frame, or turns the audience into a jury. It breaks the **fourth wall**, and it changes the mood instantly from watching to being spoken to.

### Visible light: Brecht

Most lighting wants to be invisible: you feel the sunset and forget the lamps. *Bertolt Brecht* wanted the opposite. He liked the lights in plain view, a bright, even white light, a half-height curtain and scene changes done in sight, so the audience would remember it was in a theatre and think rather than drift. Mood, for Brecht, was something to interrupt.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Write light and sound into the play where they carry story, not decoration: a lamp a character controls, a sound that returns, a final image before blackout. Picture the stage from the audience: who is where, and who is facing us? Then leave space for designers.
- **Screen:** Film borrowed these tools (blocking, motivated light, the music cue) and added the camera (see *Light, colour and setting*). Directing focus without a close-up is a good discipline for writing ensemble scenes.
- **Journalism:** Audio and live storytelling use stage tools: the pause, a sound that returns, music that enters at the turn. See *Mood in journalism and audio*.

## The pushback

- **Design isn't the writer's job.** Overloaded stage directions annoy directors and designers, who may ignore them. Write the cues that matter to the story, and trust collaborators with the rest.
- **Spectacle can swamp the play.** Big designs can overwhelm actors and text; Brook called lifeless, conventional theatre "Deadly," and spectacle can be deadly too.
- **Mood can manipulate.** Brecht's point stands: a swelling music cue and a warm glow can make an audience feel instead of think.
- **Rules of position are habits, not laws.** "Downstage centre is strongest" depends on the theatre. In the round or in a thrust space, there is no single front.

## Read next

- Peter Brook, *The Empty Space* (1968), especially the first chapter.
- Jean Rosenthal and Lael Wertenbaker, *The Magic of Light* (1972).
- Read *A Streetcar Named Desire* for the lantern, and *Our Town* for the empty stage.
- Compare *Bertolt Brecht* and *Anton Chekhov*.

## Try it
Take a scene from your play, or a key scene of *Streetcar* or *Our Town*.
1. Draw the stage as a rectangle, audience at the bottom. Mark where each character is at the start, the turn and the end.
2. Write the light in three cues: what's lit, how brightly, what colour.
3. Choose one sound or music cue that could return later in the play.
4. Decide the final image before the blackout or fade, and rewrite the scene's last stage direction so it creates that image in one or two sentences.

- **Stage:** Read it aloud to someone with your diagram; can they say whom to watch at each point?
- **Screen:** Block the scene in one wide shot with no close-ups, and see if focus still reads.
- **Journalism:** For a live event or audio piece, plan where a pause or a returning sound marks the turn.

**Check:** At the scene's turn, would every audience member be looking at the same person?

## Check yourself

Q: What did Appia want light to do?
A: To shape three-dimensional space and change like music, unifying the production instead of just illuminating painted scenery.

Q: Why is the back of the stage called "upstage"?
A: Because on raked stages the floor sloped up toward the back. An actor upstage of a partner forces them to turn away from the audience, which is where "upstaging" comes from.

Q: How does Williams make a light cue an action in *Streetcar*?
A: Blanche hides the bare bulb under a paper lantern; Mitch tears it off to see her in harsh light, exposing her.

Q: What's Brook's point in *The Empty Space*?
A: That any empty space can be a stage: a person crossing it while someone watches is enough, and a bare stage makes imagination do the work.

Q: Why did Brecht leave the lights visible?
A: To keep the audience aware it was in a theatre, so it would think critically rather than be carried away by mood.
