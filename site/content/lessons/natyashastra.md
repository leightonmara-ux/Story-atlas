## Who and when

The *Nāṭyaśāstra* ("treatise on drama") is the great founding text of Indian performance. Tradition attributes it to the sage **Bharata Muni**. Nobody knows exactly who compiled it or when; dates between about 200 BCE and 200 CE are commonly given, and many scholars think it grew over time from several hands.

It's vast: some three dozen chapters covering theatre architecture, music, dance, gesture, costume, makeup, poetic meter, acting and audience response, as well as the structure of plays. It opens with a myth of origin: the gods ask for an entertainment that everyone can enjoy, and Brahmā creates drama as a "fifth Veda", drawing on the four existing ones.

Where Aristotle's *Poetics* is short and centered on plot, the *Nāṭyaśāstra* is encyclopedic and centered on **the audience's experience**. It shaped classical Sanskrit drama, including the plays of Kālidāsa, and its theory of *rasa* still underpins Indian classical dance, music and aesthetics.

## The big ideas

### Rasa: the taste of a performance

**Rasa** literally means juice, flavor or taste. It's the aesthetic experience the audience savors. The text's famous analogy is food: just as a dish's flavor comes from combining many ingredients and spices, rasa arises from combining many elements of performance.

The core formula, often called the *rasa sūtra*, says that rasa arises from the combination of three things:

- **Vibhāva** (determinants): the causes and settings that call up an emotion. A lover, a moonlit garden.
- **Anubhāva** (consequents): the visible signs of the emotion. A glance, a tremble, tears.
- **Vyabhicāribhāva** (transitory states): passing feelings that color the main one. Anxiety, joy, fatigue.

Behind these lies a **sthāyibhāva**, a durable or dominant emotion. The audience doesn't simply *feel* the character's sorrow; through the performance, they taste a distilled, shareable version of it. That distinction between ordinary emotion and aesthetic savoring is the theory's great insight.

### The eight rasas

The text names eight:

| Rasa | Rough gloss |
|---|---|
| Śṛṅgāra | the erotic, romantic love |
| Hāsya | the comic |
| Karuṇa | pathos, compassion |
| Raudra | fury |
| Vīra | the heroic |
| Bhayānaka | the fearful |
| Bībhatsa | the odious, disgust |
| Adbhuta | the marvelous |

Later thinkers added a ninth, **śānta** (peace, tranquility). The most influential commentator, the Kashmiri philosopher **Abhinavagupta** (around the turn of the 11th century), argued for it. A play is expected to have one dominant rasa, supported by others.

### Bhāva: the actor's work

**Bhāva** means state or emotion, the thing the performer brings into being. The *Nāṭyaśāstra* is precise about how: each emotion has its own gestures, glances and movements. This is why it reads partly as an actor's manual. Story structure is never separate from the craft of making emotion visible.

### The shape of the plot

The plot is the "body" of the drama, and it's organized around the hero's pursuit of a goal, the **phala** (fruit, result). The text describes the plot through three linked sets of five.

**Five stages of action (avasthās):** what the hero goes through.

{{diagram:natyashastra}}

1. **Ārambha** (beginning): eagerness for the result.
2. **Prayatna** (effort): striving toward it.
3. **Prāptyāśā** (possibility of attainment): hope of success, with obstacles still in the way.
4. **Niyatāpti** (certainty of attainment): the obstacles fall away and success becomes sure.
5. **Phalāgama** (attainment of the result): the goal is reached.

**Five elements of plot (arthaprakṛtis):** the material the plot is made of. The best known are the **bīja** (seed), the small beginning from which the whole action grows, and the **bindu** (drop), which keeps the main thread alive when other matters interrupt it. The others are **patākā** (an extended subplot), **prakarī** (a brief episode) and **kārya** (the end action).

**Five junctures (sandhis):** the joints of the plot, which pair with the five stages: **mukha** (opening), **pratimukha** (progression), **garbha** (development, literally "womb"), **vimarśa** (pause or deliberation, where doubts and obstacles arise) and **nirvahaṇa** (conclusion).

The seed image is a lovely piece of craft advice: plant early the small thing from which the whole play will grow.

### A different idea of an ending

Classical Sanskrit drama doesn't do tragedy in the Greek sense. Plays move toward the attainment of the goal and, conventionally, a closing benediction. Certain things, such as death, aren't shown on stage. The aim is to leave the audience in a state of savored emotion, not purged by catastrophe.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Ask which rasa should dominate your play and which should support it, then check whether every scene feeds that taste. Directors of Indian classical forms still work this way, and the idea translates to any stage.
- **Screen:** Indian popular cinema is often discussed in terms of rasa: the deliberate blending of romance, comedy, heroism and pathos in a single film. The "masala" film's mixing is not a failure of unity but a different aesthetic.
- **Journalism:** The seed and drop are useful tools for long features. Plant the small detail early that will grow into the meaning of the piece, and keep returning to it when digressions threaten to take over.

## The pushback

- **Dating and authorship are uncertain.** "Bharata" may be a name for a tradition rather than one author, and the text we have has layers.
- **A system for its own theatre.** It was written for a courtly performance culture with stock character types and happy endings. Its plot rules fit that theatre better than modern realism.
- **Commentary shapes the reading.** Much of what people now say about rasa comes from Abhinavagupta and other later writers, so "what the *Nāṭyaśāstra* says" and "the rasa tradition" aren't always the same thing.
- **Schematic.** Sets of five map neatly onto each other on paper; real plays are messier.

## Try it

Plan and write a short scene from your work-in-progress for a taste rather than a conflict.

1. Choose the dominant rasa you want the audience to savor, such as karuṇa (pathos) or adbhuta (the marvelous), and one rasa to support it.
2. List the vibhāva (determinants): the people, place and causes that call it up.
3. List the anubhāva (consequents): the visible signs, such as a glance, a tremble, tears.
4. Add two vyabhicāribhāva (transitory states) that pass through and color it.
5. Write the scene, and plant one small bīja (seed) that the rest of the play could grow from.

- **Stage:** check that movement, costume and light feed the same rasa as the words.
- **Screen:** blend two rasas in one sequence, say comedy inside pathos, the way Indian popular cinema mixes them on purpose.
- **Journalism:** plant a small detail early in a feature as the seed, and return to it, as the bindu (drop), after each digression.

**Check:** Could someone in the audience name the taste the scene left, not just what happened in it?

## Check yourself

Q: What does rasa mean, and what is the food analogy?
A: Taste or flavor: the aesthetic experience the audience savors. Like a dish's flavor from combined ingredients, rasa arises from the combination of many performance elements.

Q: Name the three things whose combination produces rasa.
A: Vibhāva (determinants or causes), anubhāva (visible consequents) and vyabhicāribhāva (transitory states).

Q: List the five stages of action in order.
A: Ārambha (beginning), prayatna (effort), prāptyāśā (possibility of attainment), niyatāpti (certainty of attainment), phalāgama (attainment of the result).

Q: What is the bīja, and why is it useful advice?
A: The seed: the small beginning from which the whole action grows. It reminds writers to plant early what the ending will grow from.

Q: Which ninth rasa did later thinkers add?
A: Śānta, peace or tranquility, championed by Abhinavagupta.
