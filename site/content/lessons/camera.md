## Where it comes from

Early films mostly placed the camera where a theatre audience would sit and let the action play in front of it. Over the next few decades filmmakers learned to move closer, shift angle, follow characters and choose what's sharp and what's blurred. The vocabulary that resulted, shot sizes, angles, movement, focus, isn't one person's theory. It's a shared craft language, refined by cinematographers and taught in every film school.

A few names recur. **Gregg Toland**, cinematographer of Orson Welles's *Citizen Kane* (1941), made deep focus famous. The French critic **André Bazin**, in essays from the 1940s and 1950s, argued that long takes and deep focus respect reality and let the viewer choose where to look, in contrast to montage that dictates meaning (see *Editing*). And **Laura Mulvey**'s essay "Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema" (1975) asked whose eyes the camera borrows, covered in *Who gets to be the subject*.

The writer's interest is simple. Every camera choice decides how close we stand to a character, whose eyes we share, and what we're allowed to notice. Those are story decisions, and many of them can be written on the page.

## The big ideas

### Shot size is emotional distance

Shot sizes run, roughly, from the **extreme wide shot** (a figure tiny in a landscape) through the **wide** or **long shot** (the whole body), the **medium shot** (waist up), the **close-up** (the face) and the **extreme close-up** (an eye, a hand, a detail). The names vary between countries and crews; the logic doesn't.

The wider the shot, the more a person belongs to their surroundings: small, social, observed. The closer, the more we're inside their feelings, which is why the close-up is the shot of intimacy and judgment (see *The face and the cut*). An extreme wide shot can make someone look lonely or powerless; an extreme close-up can make a small object, a key or a ring, into the center of the story.

Many directors save the closest shots for the moment that matters most, so the cut-in itself tells the audience: this is it.

### Angle is power

A **low angle**, looking up at a character, tends to make them look strong, threatening or monumental. A **high angle**, looking down, tends to make them look small or vulnerable. *Citizen Kane* shoots Kane from low angles so often that the sets had to be built with ceilings, unusual for studio films of the time, and the low ceilings press down on him as his empire grows. These are tendencies, not codes: context can flip them.

The **Dutch angle** (or canted angle) tilts the camera so the horizon isn't level, signalling that something is off. Carol Reed's *The Third Man* (1949) uses it throughout its ruined, morally crooked postwar Vienna. The name is usually explained as a corruption of "Deutsch," a nod to German Expressionist film, though that origin is hard to document.

### Point-of-view and the subjective camera

A **point-of-view shot** shows what a character sees, usually framed by a shot of them looking, so we share their eyes for a moment. Push this further and you get the **subjective camera**, where the camera *is* a character. Robert Montgomery's *Lady in the Lake* (1947) tried it for an entire film, and the experiment is usually judged a failure: always being in someone's eyes, we never see their face, and lose the reactions that build sympathy.

Used briefly, it's powerful. The opening of John Carpenter's *Halloween* (1978) is a long, unbroken point-of-view shot of someone approaching a house, picking up a knife and killing a teenage girl. Only when his parents arrive and pull off his mask does the film cut to show us the killer: a small boy in a clown costume. The camera had made us the killer.

For prose writers, this is close to the distinction in *Point of view and voice* between whose eyes we share and who is telling. A film can sit in a character's point of view without a single POV shot, just by staying with them and showing only what they know.

### Movement and the long take

The camera can **pan** (turn left or right), **tilt** (up or down), **track** or **dolly** (move through space), **crane** (rise or fall), or be **handheld**. Each has a feel. A slow push-in builds focus and tension. Handheld suggests urgency, instability and the immediacy of documentary. A smooth tracking shot can glide us into a world.

The **long take** holds one shot without cutting, often while the camera moves. In Martin Scorsese's *Goodfellas* (1990), early in the film, as Henry Hill courts Karen, he takes her to the Copacabana nightclub: instead of waiting in line, they go in through a side door, down corridors and through the kitchen, people greeting Henry and passing tips, until a table is set for them right in front of the stage. Roughly three minutes, one Steadicam shot, photographed by Michael Ballhaus. The unbroken movement *is* the story: this is what it feels like to be let in, and why Karen falls for this life.

Long takes cost something: they can't be shortened in the edit, and every performance must hold. Use them when continuity of time and space is itself the meaning.

### Depth of field and deep focus

**Depth of field** is how much of the image, front to back, is sharp. Shallow focus isolates a character from a blurred background, directing our eye. **Deep focus** keeps near and far sharp at once, so several things can matter in one frame.

The classic example comes early in *Citizen Kane*. In the foreground, Kane's mother signs the papers that send him away; in the middle distance, his father protests; through the window at the back, small and sharp, the boy plays in the snow, unaware that his childhood is being signed away. No cut is needed. The composition makes the connection, and leaves us to notice it, which is what Bazin admired.

### Framing and negative space

What's inside the frame, and where, carries meaning. A character pushed to the edge with empty space around them, **negative space**, can feel isolated or watched. Doorways and windows frame people within the frame, often suggesting entrapment. A character looking toward empty space feels expectant; looking into the edge of the frame, cut off.

### Whose eyes?

Mulvey's **male gaze** argument applies directly here: in much classic cinema, the camera takes the hero's look, and women are framed as objects of it, with lingering shots that stop the story to display them. Many filmmakers now ask, shot by shot, whose desire the camera is serving. That's covered fully in *Who gets to be the subject*; the craft point is that framing and shot choice are where the gaze is built.

## Use it

- **Stage:** The theatre has no close-up, so it directs focus in other ways: blocking, levels, a character standing while others sit, light picking out one face, everyone onstage turning to look at one person. The audience always has a wide shot, so the playwright must tell them where to look.
- **Screen:** Writers can't call shots without annoying directors, but they can write images that imply them: "Her hand, shaking, on the doorknob" is a close-up; "Far below, a single car on an empty road" is an extreme wide. Write the moment as a point of view by showing only what a character sees. Save your biggest images for your biggest moments.
- **Journalism:** The reporter's vantage point is a camera position. Where you stood, what you could see and whom you could hear shape the story. Say so when it matters, and choose detail as a cinematographer would: one wide paragraph to establish a scene, then a close-up on a telling object. Photojournalism makes the same choices about angle, distance and who is framed as subject.

## The pushback

- **No fixed grammar.** Low angles don't always mean power, and handheld doesn't always mean realism. Audiences read shots in context, and directors routinely invert the conventions.
- **Style can become decoration.** Showy long takes and constant movement draw attention to the filmmaker. Ask whether the technique serves the story or just shows off.
- **The writer's limits.** Much of this is the director's and cinematographer's job. Screenwriters who direct on the page can annoy the people who make the film; imply shots, don't dictate them.

## Try it
Take one important scene from your draft, or one scene from a film you love.
1. Write a shot list in prose, five to eight lines, choosing a size and angle for each and one sentence on why.
2. Mark the single closest shot. Is it on the moment that matters most? If not, move it.
3. Decide whose eyes the scene belongs to. Remove any shot that shows something that person couldn't know or see, unless you want us to know more than them.

- **Stage:** Write how the audience's eye is directed at each beat: who stands, who moves, where the light falls.
- **Screen:** Rewrite the scene's action lines so each paragraph implies one shot without naming it.
- **Journalism:** Note where you stood when you reported the scene. Rewrite the opening as a wide shot followed by one close-up detail.

**Check:** Does the closest image fall on the moment that matters most, and is it clear whose eyes we're borrowing?

## Check yourself

Q: What does shot size usually do to the audience's relationship with a character?
A: Wider shots set the character in their world and keep us at a distance; closer shots bring us into their feelings and make moments feel intimate or decisive.

Q: What's the difference between a point-of-view shot and the subjective camera?
A: A point-of-view shot briefly shows what a character sees; the subjective camera makes the camera the character for a long stretch, as in the opening of *Halloween* or all of *Lady in the Lake*.

Q: Why is the Copacabana shot in *Goodfellas* a long take rather than a series of cuts?
A: The unbroken movement through the back door and kitchen to a table at the front makes us feel Henry's access and Karen's seduction in real time.

Q: What does deep focus allow in the snow scene of *Citizen Kane*?
A: The adults deciding the boy's future and the boy playing outside are sharp in one frame, so the audience makes the connection without a cut.

Q: How does the stage direct focus without a camera?
A: Through blocking, levels, light, stillness and movement, and other characters' looks, telling the audience where to look within a permanent wide shot.
