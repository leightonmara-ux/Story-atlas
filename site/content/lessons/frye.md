## Who and when

**Northrop Frye** (1912–1991) was a Canadian literary critic who taught for most of his life at Victoria College in the University of Toronto. His first book, *Fearful Symmetry* (1947), was a study of William Blake, and Blake's habit of building a whole mythology taught him to look for the large patterns under individual works.

His major book is ***Anatomy of Criticism: Four Essays*** (1957). Its "Polemical Introduction" argues that criticism should be a systematic body of knowledge, closer to a science than to a collection of opinions, and that ranking works as good or bad is not its main job. The four essays then lay out a theory of **modes**, of symbols, of **myths** (the archetypes) and of genres. Writers mostly use the first and third.

Frye's influence ran wide. **Hayden White** borrowed his four story types for *Metahistory* (1973), arguing that historians emplot the past as romance, tragedy, comedy or satire; see *Hayden White & Joan Didion*.

## The big ideas

### The modes: how strong is the hero?

The first essay sorts fiction by one question: how much power of action does the hero have, compared with us and with the world around them? Frye's answer gives five **modes**.

{{diagram:frye}}

| Mode | The hero is... | Typical forms |
|---|---|---|
| **Mythic** | Superior in *kind* to people and their world: a god | Myth |
| **Romantic** | Superior in *degree* to people and their world: marvellous, but human | Legend, folk tale, romance |
| **High mimetic** | Superior in degree to other people, but not to nature: a leader | Epic, most tragedy |
| **Low mimetic** | Superior to neither: one of us | Most comedy, realist fiction |
| **Ironic** | Inferior to us in power or intelligence: we look down on them | Satire, much modern fiction |

Frye observed that European fiction had moved steadily down this list over fifteen centuries, from gods to heroes to kings to ordinary people to the trapped and absurd. He also suggested that the ironic mode was beginning to curve back toward myth, so the list might be a cycle rather than a slide.

The modes are a practical tool. Ask where your protagonist sits. Robin Hood is romantic; Willy Loman in *Death of a Salesman* is low mimetic sliding into ironic; Kafka's Gregor Samsa, waking as an insect, is ironic. The mode sets what the audience expects your hero to be able to do.

### The four mythoi: a story's season

The third essay is the one writers quote. Frye argues that all stories are built from four basic plot movements, which he calls **mythoi** (the Greek plural of *mythos*, Aristotle's word for plot). He ties each to a season, because each moves the way a season does in the natural cycle of birth, maturity, decline and death.

| Mythos | Season | Its movement |
|---|---|---|
| **Comedy** | Spring | A new society forms around a young hero, overcoming those who block it |
| **Romance** | Summer | The quest: the hero's adventure and triumph in an idealized world |
| **Tragedy** | Autumn | The fall of a hero who stands above others into isolation and death |
| **Irony and satire** | Winter | A world without heroes, where effective action seems impossible |

The four sit on a circle. Comedy and tragedy are opposites, as are romance and irony, and each mythos shades into its neighbours. Frye divides each into six phases, so the early phases of comedy are close to irony and the late ones close to romance.

He gives each mythos an archetypal theme from Greek: **agon** (conflict) for romance, **pathos** (suffering, catastrophe) for tragedy, **sparagmos** (tearing apart; the sense that heroism is absent) for irony and satire, and **anagnorisis** (recognition, the discovery of a new society around the hero) for comedy.

### Comedy, spring

Frye's comedy comes from Greek New Comedy (see *Tragedy and comedy*). A young man wants a young woman; an older figure, often a father, blocks them; a twist frees them; and a new society crystallizes around the couple, usually at a wedding or a feast. He calls the obstacles **blocking characters**, and notes that comedy prefers to convert them rather than destroy them.

He also named the **green world** (in a 1948 essay, "The Argument of Comedy"): the forest or countryside in Shakespeare's comedies, such as the Forest of Arden in *As You Like It*, where characters escape a harsh court, are transformed, and return to remake it.

### Romance, summer

Romance is the **quest**, and Frye describes it in three stages: a perilous journey with minor adventures; a crucial struggle, often a battle in which hero or foe must die; and the exaltation of the hero, recognized even if he does not survive. His romance is wish-fulfilment, the dream of a world where good and evil are clear. Compare *Joseph Campbell & Christopher Vogler*, who found a similar shape in myth.

### Tragedy, autumn

Tragedy isolates a hero who is more than ordinary but less than divine, and moves them from the top of the wheel of fortune toward a fall. Frye read *hamartia* not as a moral flaw but as the condition of standing out: the higher a person stands, the more exposed (see *Aristotle* for the term).

### Irony and satire, winter

Satire attacks a world it measures against a standard; irony gives up even the standard. Both are, for Frye, a parody of romance: the quest that goes nowhere, the hero who can't act. Think of *Waiting for Godot*, or a news satire where every institution fails at once.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Name your play's mythos, then its phase. A comedy whose blocking figure wins would be near irony; a comedy that ends in a feast with the old father welcomed back is pure spring. Shakespeare's comedies are a ready source of green worlds: what is your play's escape place, and what changes there?
- **Screen:** Genre marketing is Frye's circle in disguise. Romantic comedy is spring, the superhero film summer, the fall of the crime boss autumn, the bleak satire winter. Know which promise the poster makes, and decide where you'll honour it and where you'll shade into the next season.
- **Journalism:** Follow Hayden White and ask which mythos your story uses. A profile of a reformer told as romance makes her a questing hero; the same facts told as irony show a system that swallows good intentions. Then check the mode: are you writing your subject up as a hero, down as a victim, or level with the reader?

## The pushback

- **Too schematic.** Seasons, mythoi, six phases each, modes, archetypes: critics found the system tidier than literature is. The New Critic W. K. Wimsatt was an early, sharp sceptic, in "Northrop Frye: Criticism as Myth" (1966).
- **Ahistorical.** Frye's categories float free of who wrote, for whom, and why. Fredric Jameson took his romance seriously in *The Political Unconscious* (1981) but insisted genres be read as products of their history.
- **No value judgments.** Frye's wish to keep evaluation out of criticism strikes many as impossible, and as a way of avoiding the arguments that matter.
- **A Western canon.** The examples are overwhelmingly Greek, biblical and European. Whether the seasonal cycle fits stories from other traditions is an open question (compare *Kishōtenketsu*).

## Read next

- Northrop Frye, *Anatomy of Criticism: Four Essays* (1957). The third essay, "Archetypal Criticism: Theory of Myths", is the place to start.
- Northrop Frye, "The Argument of Comedy" (1948), short and very readable.
- Hayden White, *Metahistory* (1973), for Frye applied to history, or *Hayden White & Joan Didion* in this course.

## Try it
Take your work-in-progress, or a film you know well, and place it on Frye's two maps.
1. Mode: write one sentence on what your protagonist can do that we can't, or can't do that we can. Mythic, romantic, high mimetic, low mimetic or ironic?
2. Mythos: which season is it? Name the movement in one line (a new society forms; a quest succeeds; a hero falls; heroism fails).
3. Find one scene where the story leans toward a neighbouring season. Strengthen it, or cut it, on purpose.

- **Stage:** In a comedy, list your blocking characters and decide which are converted at the end and which are expelled.
- **Screen:** Write a logline in each of the four mythoi for the same premise. Which promises the film you want to make?
- **Journalism:** Draft a feature's top in two mythoi, romance and irony, using the same facts. Which can the reporting support?

**Check:** Could you say your story's season in one word, and point to the scene that proves it?

## Check yourself

Q: What decides a work's mode, for Frye?
A: The hero's power of action relative to us and to their world, from mythic (a god) down through romantic, high mimetic, low mimetic, to ironic (weaker than us).

Q: Name Frye's four mythoi and their seasons.
A: Comedy is spring, romance summer, tragedy autumn, and irony and satire winter.

Q: What is a blocking character, and what does comedy tend to do with one?
A: The figure, often a father or rival, who stands in the way of the young hero's desire. Comedy prefers to convert and include them at the end rather than destroy them.

Q: What is the green world?
A: Frye's term for the forest or countryside in Shakespeare's comedies, where characters escape, are transformed, and return to renew their society.

Q: How did Frye influence the way historians and journalists think?
A: Hayden White adopted Frye's four plot types to argue that historians emplot the same facts as romance, tragedy, comedy or satire, a choice that carries a judgment.
