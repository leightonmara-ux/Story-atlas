## Who and when

Robert A. Caro (born 1935) was an investigative reporter at *Newsday*, the Long Island paper, in the early 1960s. He left to write a biography of Robert Moses, the unelected official who reshaped New York with bridges, parkways, parks and housing projects. *The Power Broker* (1974) won the Pulitzer Prize. He then began *The Years of Lyndon Johnson*: four volumes so far, *The Path to Power* (1982), *Means of Ascent* (1990), *Master of the Senate* (2002, a second Pulitzer) and *The Passage of Power* (2012), with a fifth in progress.

*Working* (2019) is his short book about how he does it: researching, interviewing, writing. It's the main source for this lesson. His editor on all these books, Robert Gottlieb, is the other half of the documentary *Turn Every Page* (2022), directed by Gottlieb's daughter Lizzie.

## The big ideas

### Turn every page

Caro tells the story in *Working*. As a young reporter he was sent to look through files, and his managing editor at *Newsday*, Alan Hathway, gave him the advice that became his method:

> Turn every page.
— Alan Hathway, as recalled by Robert Caro in *Working* (2019)

Caro's versions of the line are sometimes saltier. The point is the same: never assume you know what a file contains. The document that changes everything is rarely labelled as such. It's in a box of routine correspondence, between a memo about parking and an invoice.

This is what gives his books their authority. In *The Path to Power*, for instance, he shows how Johnson, as a young congressman in 1940, became the channel through which Texas contractors' money reached other Democrats' campaigns, building a network of debts that he would later call in. That story wasn't waiting in a single document; it was assembled from many unremarkable ones. Caro's claim is that power works through details no one bothers to read, so you have to read them.

### Power, shown not explained

Caro has said many times that his books aren't really about Moses or Johnson. They're about how political power works, and what it does to the people who have it and the people it's used on. He has often repeated a variation on an old saying: power doesn't always corrupt, but it always reveals. Give someone power and you see who they really are.

So he shows power in action, at ground level. In *The Power Broker*, a chapter called "One Mile" follows a single mile of the Cross-Bronx Expressway through the neighborhood of East Tremont. Caro interviewed the residents whose homes were destroyed when Moses refused to shift the route a few blocks. An abstract idea, the cost of unaccountable power, becomes particular people and particular apartment buildings.

*Master of the Senate* does the same with a legislature. Caro explains the Senate's rules and history at length so that, when Johnson bends them to pass the Civil Rights Act of 1957, the reader understands exactly what he's doing and why it's hard.

### Interviewing: be quiet

Caro is a patient interviewer, often returning to the same person many times. In *Working* he describes writing "SU", for "shut up", in his notebook during interviews, to remind himself not to fill silences. People who are left in a pause will often fill it with the thing they hadn't planned to say.

He also describes the value of persistence. Many people close to Johnson wouldn't talk to him at first, or told him the official story. Only by going back, and by knowing the documents well enough to recognize what was missing, did he get beneath it.

### Sense of place

In the late 1970s, working on Johnson's early life, Caro realized he wasn't understanding the people of the Texas Hill Country, where Johnson grew up, and so he wasn't understanding Johnson. In 1978 he and his wife Ina, who works with him on research, moved there and lived there for much of the next three years. Ina won the trust of local women, partly by bringing them gifts of the fig preserves she'd learned to make; they told her things they wouldn't tell him.

He went further. To understand the isolation of Johnson's mother, Rebekah, he spent a night alone out in the Hill Country in a sleeping bag. He has said you find out things that way that you could never realize otherwise.

The payoff is on the page. *The Path to Power* makes the reader feel the poverty of the land, the loneliness, and the punishing work of women's lives before electricity (the chapter "The Sad Irons" describes the irons heated on a wood stove). When Johnson later brings electric power to the Hill Country, the reader knows what it means. Caro's principle: the reader has to *see* a place, and you can't make them see what you haven't seen yourself.

### Know the ending, then write

Caro does almost all the research before writing. Then he boils the book down, in interviews he has described trying to state it in a few paragraphs, and makes a detailed outline that he pins on a long cork board in his office, so the whole shape is in front of him.

He has said he can't start writing until he knows the last sentence. Knowing where the book ends tells him what every chapter is building toward and what to leave out.

He writes first drafts in longhand, then on a typewriter, a Smith-Corona Electra 210, in many drafts. Writing by hand slows him down, and he says that slowness makes him think. Compare *John McPhee*, who also finds the structure before writing, and *Jon Franklin*, who tells reporters to find the resolution first.

## Use it

- **Stage:** For a play based on real events, research the world the way Caro does: go there, stay, and listen. The set and the rhythm of the dialogue should come from somewhere you've been. And know your final image before you draft the first scene.
- **Screen:** Fact-based films and series live on details only deep research finds. Turn every page of the trial transcript, not just the famous testimony. Write a scene like "One Mile": one place, one group of people, where the big decision lands.
- **Journalism:** Read the whole file, including the parts that look routine. In interviews, write SU in your notebook and let silences run. Before you write, state the story in a paragraph and know your last line. Show the reader a place through what you saw, heard and felt there.

## The pushback

- **Time and length.** *The Power Broker* runs to well over a thousand pages, and Gottlieb cut hundreds of thousands of words from the manuscript. The Johnson series has taken more than four decades, and Caro is now in his nineties. Few writers or editors can afford his method, and critics say some of his chapters are longer than they need to be.
- **The low-bridges story.** *The Power Broker* reports that Moses built Long Island parkway overpasses low to keep buses, and the poorer, often Black, New Yorkers who rode them, away from his beaches. It's one of the most quoted claims in the book, and it has been disputed: later researchers have argued the evidence for that intent is thin, since commercial vehicles were barred from parkways anyway.
- **Too hard on Moses?** Historians, notably in the collection *Robert Moses and the Modern City* (2007), edited by Hilary Ballon and Kenneth T. Jackson, argued for a fairer view of what Moses built.
- **Heroes and villains.** Some critics find Caro's Johnson painted in stark light and dark, and argued that *Means of Ascent* idealized Johnson's 1948 rival, Coke Stevenson, to sharpen the contrast.

## Try it

Take a research-based project, or one source you've already gathered.

1. Go back to one file, transcript or archive you've skimmed, and read every page. Note one detail you'd missed.
2. Write a paragraph describing your main place as if the reader must *see* it: light, sound, distance, what people do there all day. Mark every detail you know firsthand. If there are few, plan a visit.
3. Write your last sentence or final image now. Then list the three scenes that most need to build toward it.

- **Stage:** describe your set from the place itself, not a photo. What does the room sound like at night?
- **Screen:** pick one location where a big decision lands on ordinary people, and write it as Caro wrote "One Mile".
- **Journalism:** in your next interview, write SU in your notebook and count the silences you let run.

**Check:** Does your best detail come from a page nobody else turned, or a place you actually stood?

## Check yourself

Q: Where does "Turn every page" come from, and what does it mean?
A: From Alan Hathway, Caro's managing editor at *Newsday*. Never assume you know what a file holds; the crucial document is usually buried in routine material.

Q: Why did Caro and his wife move to the Texas Hill Country?
A: Because he realized he didn't understand the people there, and so didn't understand Johnson. Living there let him make readers see and feel the place.

Q: What does "SU" in Caro's notebook stand for, and why?
A: "Shut up": a reminder to let silences run, because people often fill them with what they hadn't meant to say.

Q: What must Caro know before he starts writing?
A: The ending, down to the last sentence, along with a full outline of the book.

Q: Name one serious criticism of *The Power Broker*.
A: Its claim that Moses built low overpasses to keep buses off his parkways has been disputed, and some historians argue it's too harsh on what Moses built.
