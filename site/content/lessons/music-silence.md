## Where it comes from

Films were never really silent. From the start, "silent" pictures were shown with a pianist, an organist or an orchestra, and the music shaped how audiences felt. When synchronized sound arrived in the late 1920s, Hollywood composers, many trained in European opera and concert music, built the classical film score on nineteenth-century models, above all Richard Wagner's.

The most useful modern thinking comes from two critics. **Michel Chion**, the French composer and critic behind *Audio-Vision* (1990; English 1994), named the two basic ways music can relate to a scene. And the film musicologist **Claudia Gorbman**, his translator, wrote ***Unheard Melodies: Narrative Film Music*** (1987), which argued that classical scores work best when we don't consciously notice them.

This lesson builds on *Sound inside and outside the story*, which covers diegetic and non-diegetic sound.

## The big ideas

### Empathetic and anempathetic music

Chion's distinction is simple and powerful. **Empathetic** music expresses the feeling of the scene: it swells when the lovers meet and darkens at the funeral. It feels with the characters, and asks us to as well. Most film scoring is empathetic.

**Anempathetic** music is indifferent. It carries on in its own mood while something terrible happens, like a music box tinkling beside a corpse or a radio playing a cheerful song during violence. The indifference doesn't cancel our feeling. It sharpens it, because the world seems not to care.

The classic case is the ear scene in Quentin Tarantino's *Reservoir Dogs* (1992). In the middle of the film, Mr. Blonde, alone in a warehouse with a captured policeman, turns on the radio. Stealers Wheel's bouncy "Stuck in the Middle with You" comes on, and he dances, razor in hand. As he cuts, the camera drifts away to an empty corner, and the song keeps bouncing. Many viewers remember seeing the cut, but they didn't; the music made them imagine it.

Other famous examples: Stanley Kubrick's *Dr. Strangelove* (1964) ends with nuclear explosions to Vera Lynn's "We'll Meet Again", and in Martin Scorsese's *Goodfellas* (1990) the gentle piano ending of "Layla" plays over the discovery of bodies after the big heist. In both, a beautiful song makes the horror harder to bear.

### Telling us how to feel

Gorbman's point is that the classical score works on us below the level of attention. It cues emotion, smooths over cuts and tells us what matters. That's powerful and also manipulative, which is why critics complain about scores that **over-tell**: violins arriving to instruct us to cry at a moment that hasn't earned it.

The extreme of over-telling is **Mickey-Mousing**, music that imitates the action note for note: a trombone slide for a fall, a plucked string for each tiptoe. The name comes from early Disney cartoons, where it's delightful. In live-action drama it usually feels cartoonish, though comedy still uses it on purpose.

### Leitmotif

A **leitmotif** ("leading motif") is a short musical idea tied to a character, place or idea, which returns and develops as the story does. It's associated with Wagner's operas, especially the *Ring* cycle, but Wagner rarely used the word; it was popularized by commentators, notably Hans von Wolzogen in his guides to the *Ring* in the 1870s.

John Williams brought the method to blockbusters. His two-note figure for *Jaws* (1975) stands in for a shark we barely see for most of the film, so the music alone becomes the threat. In the *Star Wars* films, themes for characters and forces return in new arrangements as the story changes. A leitmotif lets music carry memory: a theme heard softly can remind us of someone who isn't on screen.

### Songs, score and the needle drop

A **score** is composed for the film. A **needle drop** is an existing recording placed in it (the name comes from lowering a record player's needle). Songs bring their own associations, era and lyrics, which can be a shortcut or a trap. Since *The Graduate* (1967), with its Simon and Garfunkel songs, and *Easy Rider* (1969), filmmakers have built whole soundtracks from them.

A song with lyrics competes with dialogue, so it usually plays under montage, silence or action. Choosing whether it's diegetic (on the car radio) or non-diegetic (over the scene) changes everything: see *Sound inside and outside the story*.

### Hitchcock, Herrmann and the shower

Bernard Herrmann scored Hitchcock's *Psycho* (1960) for strings alone. The story, well documented by Stephen Rebello among others, is that Hitchcock originally wanted the shower murder, less than halfway into the film, played with no music at all. Herrmann wrote the shrieking violin cue anyway, and once Hitchcock heard it with the scene, he changed his mind. Those stabbing strings are not a melody; they're closer to a scream, or a blade. The lesson for writers: try a big moment both ways.

### Silence and the drop-out

Silence is the loudest tool in sound. Because the ear is used to a constant bed of music and ambience, removing it makes us lean in.

A **drop-out** cuts the sound suddenly: in a war film the noise of battle vanishes, leaving only a ringing or a heartbeat (*Sound inside and outside the story* looks at this as point-of-view sound). Withholding a score altogether creates its own tension. The Coen brothers' *No Country for Old Men* (2007) has almost no music; its suspense lives in footsteps, wind and the beep of a tracking device. Kubrick's *2001: A Space Odyssey* (1968) plays its spacewalks with nothing but an astronaut's breathing.

Silence also works within a scene. Hold a cue back until the turn, and the turn lands harder. Cut the music an instant before the shock, and the audience's stomach drops first.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Theatre music is often live and always present in the room, so it can be harder to make invisible. Brecht used songs to interrupt and comment rather than to soothe (see *Bertolt Brecht*). Silence onstage is shared by everyone in the room, which is why Harold Pinter wrote "Pause" and "Silence" into his scripts as different lengths of held breath.
- **Screen:** For each cue, decide in a word whether it's empathetic or anempathetic, and whether it's telling the audience something they don't already feel. If it isn't, try the scene with no music. Choose needle drops for what their lyrics and era do to the scene, not because you love the song.
- **Journalism:** In podcasts and video, music under narration is an editorial choice: it tells listeners how to feel about real people. Use it to mark structure (a sting between sections, a bed under a scene-setting passage), and let interviews and actuality play in silence when they're strong.

## The pushback

- **Music isn't neutral.** Critics of the classical score, including Gorbman's account of it, point out that "unheard" music shapes our judgments without our consent. That should worry documentarians most.
- **Anempathy can become a mannerism.** After *Reservoir Dogs*, the cheerful-pop-over-violence trick was imitated so often that it now risks feeling like a pose.
- **Leitmotif can be a label.** A theme that just announces "villain enters" adds nothing. Wagner's motifs develop and combine; many film motifs merely repeat.
- **Silence needs context.** It only works against expectation. A film that is quiet throughout must find other ways to create contrast.

## Try it
Choose one emotionally important scene from your draft, or a scene from a film you know well.
1. Write down the feeling you want the audience to have, in one word.
2. Imagine it three ways: with empathetic music that expresses that feeling, with anempathetic music that carries on regardless, and in silence apart from diegetic sound.
3. Pick the version that makes the feeling strongest without stating it. Note exactly where the music starts and stops, and where silence falls.
4. If the scene uses a song, check what its lyrics say. Do they comment on the scene, or just repeat it?

- **Stage:** Mark one moment for a silence the whole audience shares. What breaks it?
- **Screen:** Rewatch a film scene with the sound muted, then with it on. What exactly did the music add?
- **Journalism:** Take an audio piece you made and remove the music bed under one interview. Is it stronger?

**Check:** Can you name what each music cue does that the image and dialogue don't already do?

## Check yourself

Q: What's the difference between empathetic and anempathetic music?
A: Empathetic music expresses the scene's feeling; anempathetic music carries on indifferent to it, like a cheerful song during violence, which often intensifies our response.

Q: Why does "Stuck in the Middle with You" make the *Reservoir Dogs* ear scene so disturbing?
A: The bouncy song is indifferent to the cruelty, and the camera looks away, so the music and our imagination do the work.

Q: What is a leitmotif?
A: A short musical idea attached to a character, place or idea that returns and develops through the story, associated with Wagner and used by film composers such as John Williams.

Q: What is Mickey-Mousing?
A: Music that mimics the on-screen action beat for beat, named after early Disney cartoons. It usually feels cartoonish in drama.

Q: What did Hitchcock first want for the *Psycho* shower scene?
A: No music. Bernard Herrmann scored it anyway, and Hitchcock changed his mind when he heard the shrieking strings.
