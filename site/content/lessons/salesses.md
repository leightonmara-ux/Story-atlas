## Who and when

Matthew Salesses is a Korean American novelist and essayist, adopted from Korea as a child, and the author of novels including *The Hundred-Year Flood* and *Disappear Doppelgänger Disappear*. He has taught fiction writing in several MFA programs.

***Craft in the Real World: Rethinking Fiction Writing and Workshopping*** (2021) became one of the most discussed craft books of its decade. It's in two parts: the first rethinks familiar craft terms, the second rethinks the writing workshop, with alternative models, exercises and a sample syllabus.

Where most lessons in this course describe a structure, Salesses asks a prior question: *who decided what counts as good structure, and for whom?*

## The big ideas

### Craft is a set of expectations

Salesses's central claim is that **craft is not a set of universal rules**. It's a set of expectations, built up in particular communities of readers and writers. What feels "believable", what counts as a satisfying ending, how much a protagonist should want, how much backstory is too much: all of these are learned conventions, not laws of nature.

That doesn't make craft meaningless. It makes it **cultural**. A writer always writes in relation to some set of expectations, and good craft is using them, or deliberately working against them, with full awareness.

This is why he insists that writers name their audience. Not to narrow it, but because a story always makes assumptions about what its reader already knows, values and finds normal. Knowing those assumptions lets a writer choose them, instead of inheriting them by default.

### The default reader

If craft depends on expectations, then every story has an audience, explicit or not. Salesses argues that the traditional workshop pretends to a neutral, universal reader, but that this "default" reader in practice tends to reflect the dominant tradition of American literary fiction: largely white, middle-class and Western.

So when a workshop says a story "doesn't work", it may really mean it doesn't meet that reader's expectations. A writer drawing on other traditions, other communities, other ways of telling, can be "corrected" toward a norm they never chose.

### Rethinking the craft terms

The first half of the book takes familiar words, such as plot, character, conflict, tone, pacing and believability, and shows how each carries cultural assumptions. For example:

- **Plot and agency.** The Western workshop tends to prize a protagonist who drives the story through choices. Salesses suggests that this reflects an individualist culture. Stories shaped by fate, community, obligation or chance aren't failures of agency; they reflect different beliefs about how lives work. (Compare *Charles Baxter*, who defends agency as a moral value.)
- **Conflict.** The assumption that every story needs a central conflict is one convention among others. (Compare *Kishōtenketsu* and *Ursula K. Le Guin*.)
- **Believability.** What a reader finds plausible depends on what they've lived and read. A detail that strikes one reader as melodramatic may be ordinary to another.

### Reforming the workshop

The second half is practical. In the traditional "silent" workshop, the writer listens without speaking while peers discuss their story. Salesses argues this structure puts all the power with the readers and their default expectations.

His alternatives include:

- **The writer leads.** The writer can say who the story is for and what they're trying to do, ask the group specific questions, and steer the discussion.
- **Discuss the story's goals, not the group's preferences.** Feedback starts from what the writer intends and asks whether the story achieves it.
- **Make expectations explicit.** When a reader says something "doesn't work", ask: for whom, and according to what convention?
- **The teacher as moderator of power**, attentive to whose voices dominate the room.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Theatre has many traditions with their own expectations: Noh, Sanskrit drama, Black American theatre, devised work. Before applying a note like "the protagonist needs to be more active", ask which tradition that note comes from, and whether it's the one your play belongs to.
- **Screen:** Development notes ("make her want something", "raise the stakes") are expectations of a specific industry and audience. Salesses helps you tell the difference between a note that will help your film reach its intended audience and one that will make it more like every other film.
- **Journalism:** Questions of audience are central to reporting: who is this story for, and whose idea of a "real story" shapes it? Salesses's argument parallels debates about whose perspectives count as the neutral default in newsrooms.

## The pushback

- **Is it new?** Some reviewers called the workshop reforms modest. Letting writers speak, centering their goals and examining assumptions are things good teachers already did.
- **"Western craft" as a monolith.** Critics note that the Western tradition he critiques is itself diverse, and that the opposing categories can become generalizations of their own.
- **The relativism worry.** If craft is entirely relative to audience, how can a workshop judge anything? Salesses's answer is that judgment should be explicit about its standards, not abandoned, but readers disagree about where that leaves teaching.
- **Defenders of the silent workshop** argue that it mimics a real reader, who meets the text without the author there to explain it, and protects writers from defending their work in the moment.
- **Wide influence.** Whatever the criticisms, many programs have changed their workshop practice in response to Salesses and to Felicia Rose Chavez's *The Anti-Racist Writing Workshop* (2021).

## Read next

- Matthew Salesses, *Craft in the Real World: Rethinking Fiction Writing and Workshopping* (2021).
- Felicia Rose Chavez, *The Anti-Racist Writing Workshop: How to Decolonize the Creative Classroom* (2021).
- Charles Baxter, "Dysfunctional Narratives", to read the case for agency alongside Salesses's critique of it.

## Try it

Take a story or script you're drafting, and a craft note you've had on it, such as "the protagonist needs to be more active" or "this ending isn't believable".

1. Write who the piece is for: the readers whose knowledge, values and sense of normal it assumes.
2. Write which convention the note comes from, and whose expectations it reflects.
3. Rewrite one scene twice: once following the note, and once deliberately going the other way, for example letting fate, community or obligation shape events instead of one character's choices.
4. Write three specific questions you'd ask a workshop about the two versions, starting from your goals.

- **Stage:** name the theatre tradition your play belongs to, and test the note against that tradition's expectations.
- **Screen:** sort your development notes into those that help the film reach its intended audience and those that would make it like every other film.
- **Journalism:** write who the story is for and whose idea of a "real story" is shaping it, then reread your lede with that in mind.

**Check:** Can you say which choices you made knowingly, and for whom?
