## Who and when

Paula Vogel (born 1951) is one of the most influential American playwrights and playwriting teachers of her generation. Her plays include *The Baltimore Waltz* (1992), written in response to her brother's death from AIDS; *How I Learned to Drive* (1997), which won the 1998 Pulitzer Prize for Drama; and *Indecent* (2015), which reached Broadway in 2017. *Mother Play* opened on Broadway in 2024.

She taught playwriting at Brown University from 1984 to 2008, where she built its graduate program, and then chaired the playwriting program at the Yale School of Drama. Her former students include Sarah Ruhl, Lynn Nottage, Quiara Alegría Hudes and Nilo Cruz, a list that covers several Pulitzer Prizes.

Vogel hasn't written a single structure manual. Her ideas reach us through her plays, interviews, and the accounts of students, so this lesson sticks to what is well documented.

## The big ideas

### Form is content

Vogel has said in interviews that she doesn't see form and content as separable. The structure of a play isn't a container you pour a story into; it is part of what the play means. A play about memory might be built the way memory works, out of order. A play about a history that keeps repeating might repeat.

### There's more than one plot form

The most practical thing Vogel teaches is that the Aristotelian arc, events linked by cause and effect toward a climax (see *Aristotle*), is **one** form among several. According to Sarah Ruhl's account in *100 Essays I Don't Have Time to Write*, Vogel teaches a set of distinct plot forms, among them:

- **Linear (Aristotelian):** cause and effect toward a climax.
- **Circular:** the end returns to the beginning, as in Arthur Schnitzler's *La Ronde*, a chain of encounters that comes back to where it started.
- **Backward:** the story told in reverse, as in Harold Pinter's *Betrayal*, which starts with the end of an affair and ends with its beginning.
- **Repetitive:** a pattern played again with variations, as in *Waiting for Godot*, where the second act echoes the first.

Accounts of her list name further forms as well, and don't always agree on their labels. The value of the list is the permission it gives: if your play resists a climax, the question isn't how to force one in, but which form it actually wants.

### Time works backwards

A phrase associated with Vogel is that time works backwards in the theatre. She has described writing a play backwards herself, starting from the emotional situation and characters and finding the structure that will deliver them. And she points to the way a play's ending rewrites what came before: the audience understands the first scene only once they've seen the last.

### Defamiliarization

Vogel's teaching leans on the Russian critic **Viktor Shklovsky** and his idea of *ostranenie*, defamiliarization: art makes familiar things strange again so that we actually see them. (Compare Brecht's Verfremdungseffekt, in *Bertolt Brecht*.) She has described the moment when something you've stepped over many times suddenly grabs you by the ankle. Her plays use this constantly: comedy, direct address and strange structures that make the audience see what they thought they knew.

### How I Learned to Drive

The play is the clearest showcase of her methods. Li'l Bit, now an adult, looks back at her relationship with her Uncle Peck, who sexually abused her through her adolescence. The scenes are out of order, jumping between ages, and many are framed by titles taken from driving lessons, such as shifting gears or idling in neutral. A **Greek Chorus** of three actors plays everyone else in the family and the town.

The structure does the moral work. Vogel shows Peck as charming and damaged, someone the audience might like, which makes the harm harder to dismiss. And the play holds back its earliest and most painful memory until near the end, so time does work backwards: the last revelation changes how you read everything you've seen.

### Indecent

*Indecent* tells the story of Sholem Asch's Yiddish play *God of Vengeance* (1907) and the obscenity trial that followed its 1923 Broadway production. A troupe of actors presents it across decades, with projected titles announcing each scene and repeated images (a rain scene between two women returns more than once) carrying the emotional line. It's an example of a play that is structured as a remembering.

### The bake-off

Vogel is known for a teaching exercise called the **bake-off**: writers have a short, fixed time, often 48 hours, to write a play that includes a list of assigned "ingredients", such as a title, a setting, a kind of character, an object, or something deliberately impossible to stage. The constraints push writers past their habits and make the structure a puzzle to solve rather than a template to fill.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Before forcing a stuck play into an arc, ask which form it is. If the material is about recurrence, try repetition; if it's about how something came to be, try going backward; if it's a memory, let memory's order lead.
- **Screen:** Films and series built on non-linear forms, such as reverse chronology, loops and braided timelines, are the screen cousins of her plot forms. The question is the same: does this order reveal something the straight version can't?
- **Journalism:** Long-form features sometimes take a backward or braided shape, opening with the outcome and moving back through causes, or weaving past and present. Choose the order for what it reveals, never to obscure what happened when.

## The pushback

- **Not a system.** Vogel teaches through provocations, exercises and examples, not a codified framework. Accounts of her plot forms differ in details from source to source.
- **Arcs still work.** Critics of non-linear structure note that audiences can lose the thread, and many great plays are entirely linear.
- **Difficult subjects.** *How I Learned to Drive* has been praised for refusing easy villains, and some viewers find Peck uncomfortably sympathetic. The play's defenders argue that this discomfort is the point: it shows how the harm was able to happen.
- **Known secondhand.** Much of what circulates about her teaching comes from students and journalists who sat in on her classes, so details vary in the retelling.

## Try it

Take a play or script of yours that's stuck, or whose climax feels forced.

1. List its main events in chronological order, one line each.
2. Reorder the list in three of the forms Vogel teaches: circular (it ends where it began), backward (as in *Betrayal*), and repetitive (a pattern that plays again, with variations).
3. Under each version, write one sentence on what that order reveals that the straight one doesn't.
4. Choose the form that matches what the play is about, and draft its first scene that way.

- **Stage:** decide which moment to hold back until near the end, as *How I Learned to Drive* holds back its earliest memory, so that it changes how the audience reads everything before it.
- **Screen:** outline the backward or looped version scene by scene and see which reveal gains most from coming last.
- **Journalism:** try a backward or braided order for a feature, and make sure the reader can always tell what happened when.

**Check:** Does the form you chose say something about your subject, or is it only a trick?

## Check yourself

Q: What does Vogel mean by saying form and content are inseparable?
A: The structure of a play is part of its meaning, not a neutral container: a play about memory might be built like memory, a play about recurrence might repeat.

Q: Name three non-Aristotelian plot forms Vogel teaches, with examples.
A: Circular (*La Ronde*), backward (*Betrayal*) and repetitive (*Waiting for Godot*).

Q: How does the structure of *How I Learned to Drive* do its moral work?
A: Scenes are out of order and framed as driving lessons, and the earliest, most painful memory is held back until near the end, so the final revelation changes how the audience reads everything before it.

Q: What is defamiliarization, and whom does Vogel take it from?
A: Making the familiar strange so that we really see it, from the Russian formalist critic Viktor Shklovsky.

Q: What is a bake-off?
A: A timed exercise, often 48 hours, in which writers produce a play that must include a list of assigned ingredients.
