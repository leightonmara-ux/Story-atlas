## Where it comes from

Ask a screenwriting teacher what a character is, and you'll probably hear three words: **want**, **need** and **wound**. None of them belongs to one inventor. They're a working vocabulary that grew up in screenwriting classes and handbooks from the late 1970s onward, built on older ideas from the theatre.

The theatre roots come first. *Konstantin Stanislavski* asked actors to find a character's **super-objective**, the one aim that runs through the whole play (Elizabeth Hapgood's translation, *An Actor Prepares*, 1936). *Lajos Egri*, in *The Art of Dramatic Writing* (1946), told playwrights to build each character from a **bone structure** of physiology, sociology and psychology, so that behavior has causes.

The screen versions came later. *Syd Field* wrote of a character's **dramatic need** in *Screenplay* (1979). Michael Hauge, in *Writing Screenplays That Sell* (1988), split motivation into outer and inner. *John Truby*'s *The Anatomy of Story* (2007) set the hero's **desire** against a psychological and moral **need**, and named the past event that haunts them the **ghost**. K. M. Weiland's *Creating Character Arcs* (2016) calls the false belief at the center of it all **the Lie** the character believes.

This lesson pulls those threads together, and looks at the argument over the third word.

## The big ideas

### The want: what they're chasing

The **want** is the conscious goal: the thing a character would name if you asked them. Win the fight. Get the money. Find the killer. Get home. It's concrete and outward, and the audience can tell whether it has been achieved.

The want drives the plot: it makes the character act, and action meets opposition. Stanislavski's super-objective is the actor's version of the same thing, though his is deeper: not just what the character pursues, but the drive beneath every scene.

A good want is specific and has a deadline. "To be happy" isn't a want. "To win back my wife before she remarries on Saturday" is.

### The need: what they must learn

The **need** is what the character lacks inside, usually without knowing it: to trust, to stop lying, to forgive, to accept that they can't control everything. Truby's distinction between **psychological** need (a flaw that hurts the character) and **moral** need (a flaw that hurts others) is the most useful refinement; see *John Truby* for it.

The craft lies in the relationship between the two. In the classic shape, the want and the need pull in different directions, and the climax forces a choice between them.

*Casablanca* (1942) is the standard example. Rick's want, for most of the film, is to stay out of other people's trouble and, once Ilsa reappears, to have her back. His need is to rejoin the world and its war. In the closing scene at the foggy airfield, he gives up the want for the need: he puts Ilsa on the plane with her husband.

Sometimes the want shrinks to fit the need. In *Rocky* (1976), the night before the fight, Rocky tells Adrian he can't beat the champion. All he wants now is to "go the distance", to still be standing at the final bell, and prove he isn't just another bum from the neighborhood. The outward goal has turned into the inner one.

### Not every character arcs

The shape above is a **positive change arc**: the character learns their need. There are others. In a **negative arc**, the character refuses the need, or learns the wrong lesson, and falls (Michael Corleone across *The Godfather*, 1972). Weiland also describes a **flat arc**, where the protagonist already knows the truth and the story tests it, changing the people around them instead. Many detectives, action heroes and comic leads work this way; see *Comedy structure* on why sitcom characters mustn't learn.

### Three dimensions

Egri's bone structure (physiology, sociology, psychology) is covered in *Lajos Egri*. The point to carry here is that want and need don't float free. A character's want often comes from their sociology (a debt, a job, a family's expectation) and their need from their psychology.

### The wound: why they're like this

The **wound**, Truby's ghost, or simply the **backstory**, is a past event that explains the character's weakness. A betrayal taught them never to trust; a failure taught them never to try. Many teachers, Michael Hauge and K. M. Weiland among them, treat it as the root of the whole system: the wound creates a false belief, the belief creates the weakness, and the story forces the character to face it.

Used well, it gives a character weight. A common piece of advice is to reveal it late, when the audience already cares, rather than up front.

Film history also shows its risks. In *Psycho* (1960), a psychiatrist steps in near the end to explain Norman Bates's childhood in a long, tidy speech, and critics have long found it the weakest scene in the film. *Citizen Kane* (1941) builds its whole investigation around Kane's last word, "Rosebud", and then doubts it: in the final scene the reporter Thompson tells the others he doesn't think any single word can explain a man's life, just before the camera finds the burning sled. The film gives us the wound and refuses to let it be the answer.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Write each major character's want as something they could get by the final scene, and their super-objective as the drive beneath it. Then give the actor the wound as given circumstances, not as a speech. On stage, a confessed backstory tends to stop the play.
- **Screen:** Put want and need on one index card per lead. Find the scene where they collide, usually the climax, and check that the character must give up one for the other. If they can have both, the ending is probably too easy.
- **Journalism:** Ask what your subject wanted, in their own words, at each stage. That's reportable. Their "need" isn't: it's your interpretation, and readers will take it as fact. Don't diagnose a wound from the outside, and don't trim a life to fit a redemption arc. If someone hasn't changed, that's the story.

## The pushback

- **The trauma plot.** In "The Case Against the Trauma Plot" (*The New Yorker*, December 2021), the critic Parul Sehgal argued that trauma has become fiction and television's default shortcut to depth. A character's past injury is withheld like a mystery and revealed as the key to everything, and the result, she argues, reduces a person to a symptom. Her essay set off a long argument; some writers replied that trauma is real and deserves form (see *Charles Baxter*).
- **Explanation kills mystery.** The more completely a wound explains someone, the less room they have to surprise us. *E. M. Forster*'s test of a round character is that they can surprise convincingly; a backstory that accounts for everything can make them flat.
- **One shape among many.** The need-and-revelation model assumes a Western, individual, therapeutic arc. *Kishōtenketsu*, Chekhov's ensembles and much comedy don't run on it.
- **Mechanical use.** Filled in like a form, want, need and wound produce characters who behave like worksheets. They're diagnostic questions, not ingredients.

## Try it

Take your protagonist, or the lead of a film you know well.

1. Write the want in one line, with a deadline. Write the need in one line, in words the character wouldn't use about themselves.
2. Find the scene where the two collide. If there isn't one, write a moment where getting the want would mean betraying the need.
3. Write the wound in a sentence. Then cut every line in the draft that explains it, and leave one image or action that hints at it.

- **Stage:** give the wound to the actor as given circumstances and see whether the scene plays without anyone mentioning it.
- **Screen:** check where the wound is revealed. If it's in the first act, try moving it past the midpoint.
- **Journalism:** list what your subject said they wanted, with the source for each. Strike out anything you've inferred about their need.

**Check:** At the climax, does your character have to choose between what they want and what they need?

## Check yourself

Q: What's the difference between a character's want and need?
A: The want is the conscious, outward goal that drives the plot. The need is the inner change they must make, usually without knowing it, which gives the story its meaning.

Q: What happens to want and need at the climax of *Casablanca*?
A: Rick gives up his want (Ilsa) for his need (to rejoin the world and its fight), putting her on the plane with her husband.

Q: What is a flat arc?
A: A shape, described by K. M. Weiland, in which the protagonist already knows the truth and doesn't change; the story tests their belief and changes the people around them instead.

Q: What did Parul Sehgal argue in "The Case Against the Trauma Plot"?
A: That trauma has become a default shortcut to depth, withheld and revealed as the key to a character, and that it flattens people into symptoms.

Q: Why should a journalist be careful with want and need?
A: A subject's stated wants can be reported, but their "need" is the writer's interpretation. Imposing an arc or diagnosing a wound turns a real person into a character.
