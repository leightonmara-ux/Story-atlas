## Where it comes from

Every story is told from somewhere. Someone sees, someone speaks, and the reader or audience only ever gets what passes through them. Writers have always known this, but three thinkers gave us the most useful tools for talking about it.

**Wayne C. Booth** (1921–2005), a literary critic at the University of Chicago, published ***The Rhetoric of Fiction*** in 1961. He argued that fiction is a kind of rhetoric: every choice an author makes, including the choice of narrator, is a way of guiding the reader's judgment. A second edition (1983) added a long afterword answering his critics.

**Mikhail Bakhtin** (1895–1975), a Russian thinker who worked for much of his life in obscurity, published a book on Dostoevsky in 1929 and a much-revised version, ***Problems of Dostoevsky's Poetics***, in 1963. Western readers mostly met it through Caryl Emerson's English translation (1984).

**James Wood**, a critic for *The New Yorker*, wrote ***How Fiction Works*** (2008), a short, lively book whose best chapters are about how a narrator's voice and a character's voice blend.

## The big ideas

### The implied author

Booth's most lasting idea is the **implied author**: the version of the writer that a reader builds up from the work itself. It isn't the real person, who may be kinder, crueler or more confused than their book. It's the sensibility that seems to have made every choice: what to show, what to leave out, whom to sympathize with.

This matters because it gives you a fixed point for measuring the narrator. A narrator is a character the implied author has created, and the two may not agree.

### Reliable and unreliable narrators

Booth defined a narrator as **reliable** when they speak and act in line with the norms of the work, that is, with the implied author's values. When they don't, they're **unreliable**, and the reader is invited to see past them.

Unreliability isn't just lying. A narrator can be mistaken, naive, self-deceived, or morally blind. Huck Finn misjudges his own goodness; Kazuo Ishiguro's butler in *The Remains of the Day* (1989) can't admit what he has lost. The pleasure comes from a kind of secret communication: the implied author and the reader understand something together, behind the narrator's back.

### Showing and telling, reconsidered

By the mid-20th century, "show, don't tell" had hardened into a rule, partly through Percy Lubbock's *The Craft of Fiction* (1921), which drew on Henry James. Booth pushed back hard. He argued that telling, a narrator's direct comment, summary and judgment, is a legitimate tool, and that even the most "objective" showing is a set of authorial choices. Fielding and George Eliot tell constantly, and well. The real question isn't "show or tell?" but "what effect does this choice have on the reader?" (*Janet Burroway* gives the practical, workshop version of the debate.)

### Polyphony

Bakhtin argued that Dostoevsky invented a new kind of novel. In most fiction, he said, characters' voices are finally contained by the author's: the author knows best and has the last word. That's **monologic**. Dostoevsky's novels are **polyphonic**: characters are full, independent consciousnesses whose views the author doesn't overrule.

> A plurality of independent and unmerged voices and consciousnesses, a genuine polyphony of fully valid voices is in fact the chief characteristic of Dostoevsky's novels.
> — Mikhail Bakhtin, *Problems of Dostoevsky's Poetics* (trans. Caryl Emerson)

Behind this is a bigger idea, **dialogism**: every utterance answers earlier ones and anticipates replies. Words come to us already used, already loaded with other people's intentions. A writer who hears that can let a story argue with itself instead of delivering a verdict.

### Free indirect style

Wood's favorite tool is **free indirect style**: third-person narration that slips into a character's way of thinking and speaking without quotation marks or "she thought". His example is simple. In "Ted watched the orchestra through stupid tears", the word *stupid* is Ted's, not the narrator's; we're inside his embarrassment while still outside him.

The technique was developed by novelists such as Jane Austen and Gustave Flaubert. Its power is double vision. The narrator can be close enough to share a character's feelings and far enough away to let us see around them, often ironically. Most contemporary "close third person" is free indirect style.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Theatre has no narrator by default, but it has its own devices: the soliloquy and direct address (Richard III confiding his plans to us), the onstage narrator (Tom in Tennessee Williams's *The Glass Menagerie*, who tells us it's a memory play, or the Stage Manager in Thornton Wilder's *Our Town*). Each changes whose version of events we trust. An unreliable narrator onstage is especially powerful because the audience can see what he describes.
- **Screen:** The camera always takes someone's eyes, or chooses not to. A point-of-view shot puts us literally in a character's sight line; restricted narration, as in Hitchcock's *Rear Window* (1954), keeps us with one character's knowledge throughout. Voice-over can be as unreliable as any first-person novel. Ask in each scene: whose experience is the camera organized around, and what are we kept from seeing?
- **Journalism:** The reporter's presence is a point-of-view decision. First person can be honest about how you know what you know, and it was central to *Tom Wolfe & the New Journalism*; it can also crowd out the subject. Third person suggests neutrality, but someone still chose every detail. Writing from inside a subject's head, a kind of free indirect style, demands reporting that justifies it.

## The pushback

- **Is the implied author needed?** Narratologists such as Gérard Genette argued the concept is redundant: there's the real author, the narrator and the text, and nothing more. Others defend it as the only way to describe a work's values.
- **Unreliability depends on the reader.** What counts as "the norms of the work" is itself an interpretation. Later critics argued that readers decide a narrator is unreliable by bringing in their own assumptions, so one reader's unreliable narrator is another's honest one.
- **Bakhtin's drama problem.** Bakhtin claimed drama is by nature alien to genuine polyphony, because a play's world is built from a single piece. Many theatre scholars disagree, arguing that a stage full of embodied, contending voices is dialogic by nature.
- **Polyphony can excuse mush.** "Let the voices argue" is good advice, but a story still needs a shape. Bakhtin was describing a rare achievement, not a license to avoid choices.

## Read next

- Wayne C. Booth, *The Rhetoric of Fiction* (2nd ed., 1983).
- Mikhail Bakhtin, *Problems of Dostoevsky's Poetics*, trans. Caryl Emerson (1984). Dense; chapter 1 lays out polyphony.
- James Wood, *How Fiction Works* (2008), especially the chapters on narrating and free indirect style.
- Kazuo Ishiguro, *The Remains of the Day* (1989), a masterclass in an unreliable, self-deceiving narrator.

## Try it
Take a scene from your work-in-progress written in third person, about a page long.
1. Underline every word of judgment or feeling in the narration, such as *stupid*, *finally*, *of course*.
2. For each, ask whose word it is: the narrator's, or the character's? Mark it N or C.
3. Rewrite the page twice: once pulling the narration close, so most judgments are the character's (free indirect style); once pulling it back, so the narrator judges openly, Booth-style telling.
4. Note one thing the reader knows in the distant version that the character doesn't.

- **Stage:** Give the same scene a narrator or a soliloquy. What can the audience now see that the speaker can't?
- **Screen:** List the scene's shots and mark whose eyes each one takes. Recut on paper so one character owns every point-of-view shot.
- **Journalism:** Draft one paragraph of a feature in first person and in third. Which reveals how you know what you know?

**Check:** Can you say, for every judgment on the page, whose it is?

## Check yourself

Q: What does Booth mean by the implied author?
A: The version of the writer a reader infers from the work: the sensibility behind all its choices, distinct from both the real person and the narrator.

Q: When is a narrator unreliable, in Booth's sense?
A: When they don't speak or act in line with the work's norms, so the reader is invited to see past them. They may be lying, but also naive, mistaken or self-deceived.

Q: What did Booth say about "show, don't tell"?
A: He rejected it as a rule. Telling is a legitimate tool, and even showing is a set of authorial choices; what matters is the effect on the reader.

Q: What is a polyphonic novel?
A: Bakhtin's term for a novel, like Dostoevsky's, in which characters' voices are independent, fully valid consciousnesses that the author doesn't overrule.

Q: What makes free indirect style distinctive?
A: Third-person narration takes on a character's own words and attitudes without quotation marks, so we're inside their view and outside it at once.
