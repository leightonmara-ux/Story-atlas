## Who and when

Kristin Thompson is a film historian and scholar, long associated with the University of Wisconsin–Madison. With David Bordwell she wrote *Film Art*, the standard introductory film textbook, and with Bordwell and Janet Staiger *The Classical Hollywood Cinema* (1985), a large study of how Hollywood films told stories from the silent era to 1960.

*Storytelling in the New Hollywood: Understanding Classical Narrative Technique* (1999) carried that work forward. It asked a question the screenwriting manuals rarely did: if you actually watch Hollywood films closely and time them, what structure do you find?

She's the only person in the Screen track who isn't selling a method. She's describing what's there.

## The big ideas

### The argument: classical storytelling didn't die

By the 1990s many critics claimed that Hollywood had abandoned careful storytelling. In this view, the "New Hollywood" of blockbusters, high concept and franchises had replaced plot with spectacle and fragmented narrative.

Thompson argued the opposite. Close analysis of popular films from the 1970s to the 1990s showed that the classical techniques of the studio era were alive and well: clear goals, tight causality, careful motivation, **dangling causes** (threads left open to be picked up later), deadlines, and a great deal of redundancy so the audience never gets lost. Big special-effects films and personal comedies alike were built in the classical way.

### Four parts, not three

{{diagram:thompson}}

Thompson's most influential finding is about large-scale structure. Looking at feature films of around two hours, she found that most break into **four large parts**, often of roughly equal length, separated by turning points. A short **epilogue** usually follows.

1. **Setup:** introduces the characters and their world and establishes the protagonist's goals, often more than one.
2. **Complicating action:** a turning point creates a new situation. The goals may be redefined, or a new obstacle changes how the protagonist must go after them. This part often works almost like a second setup.
3. **Development:** the protagonist struggles toward the goals. Here the story can mark time with delays, obstacles, subplots and character moments, before a turning point pushes toward the end.
4. **Climax:** the action builds to the moment where the goals are clearly achieved or clearly lost.
5. **Epilogue:** a brief coda that shows the new stability and ties off subplots.

The parts are defined by **what the protagonist is trying to do**, not by page numbers. A turning point between parts is a moment where the goals or the situation shift.

### Why this matters for the "three acts"

The standard manual, following *Syd Field*, says a feature has three acts in a 1–2–1 ratio: a quarter, a half, and a quarter. Thompson's timings suggest that what writers call "Act II" is usually two parts, of about the same length as the others, divided by a significant turning point near the middle of the film.

That helps explain something. Many manual writers, Field included, ended up adding a **midpoint** to their three-act model, because the long second act clearly needed one. Thompson's analysis suggests the midpoint isn't an add-on but a full structural break, as important as the others.

She also found that length matters. The number of parts depends on the running time: in a feature of around two hours, four is typical, while an unusually long film may have more. The parts seem to want to stay a manageable size.

### Goals, plural

Thompson stresses that classical protagonists usually pursue **two or more goals**, often one in work or adventure and one in love. The two lines intertwine and are often resolved together near the climax. Compare *Blake Snyder*'s A and B stories, which meet at Break into Three.

### Watching like a scholar

Her method is part of the lesson. Thompson works from the films, timing scenes and charting where goals change, rather than from theory. She tested the manuals against the movies and found the manuals partly right, partly wrong. It's a good habit for any writer: before accepting anyone's structure, time a few films you love and see what's really there.

## Use it

- **Screen:** If your script's middle sags, try outlining it as four parts instead of three. Ask what new situation begins the complicating action, and what turning point at the halfway mark changes the protagonist's plan. Then check your parts are roughly balanced.
- **Stage:** Thompson studied films, not plays, so her timings don't carry over directly. But her method does: time a production you admire and mark where the characters' goals change. Two-act plays often have their own big turn at the interval.
- **Journalism:** Long-form audio and video documentaries face the same timing problem as films. Planning four balanced parts, each with its own situation and a clear turn between them, helps listeners keep their bearings over an hour.

## The pushback

- **Descriptive, not a method.** Thompson tells you how successful films are built, not how to write one. Writers looking for a step-by-step process will find more help in the manuals she critiques.
- **A particular kind of film.** Her sample is mainstream Hollywood. Art cinema, many non-Western traditions and the films in the *Counterpoints* track often don't follow the pattern, and weren't meant to.
- **Counting is interpretation.** Where one part ends and the next begins can be debated, and other analysts timing the same films sometimes place turning points differently. "Roughly equal" means roughly.
- **Three or four is partly naming.** Defenders of three-act thinking say a three-act structure with a strong midpoint is really the same thing described differently. Thompson would agree the shapes overlap. Her point is that the middle break is as strong as the others, and the parts are balanced.

## Read next

## Check yourself

Q: Name Thompson's four large parts, plus the coda that usually follows.
A: Setup, complicating action, development, climax, followed by a short epilogue.

Q: What defines where one part ends and the next begins?
A: A turning point where the protagonist's goals or situation change, not a page number.

Q: What does her finding suggest about the "second act" of the manuals?
A: That it's usually two parts of about normal length, split by a major turning point near the middle of the film.

Q: What larger argument was Thompson making about New Hollywood?
A: That classical Hollywood storytelling hadn't been abandoned for spectacle. Popular films of the 1970s to 1990s still used clear goals, causality and careful structure.

Q: How is Thompson's approach different from Field's or Snyder's?
A: She describes what films actually do, from close analysis and timing, rather than offering a method for writers to follow.
