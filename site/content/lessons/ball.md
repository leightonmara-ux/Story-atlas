## Who and when

David Ball's *Backwards and Forwards: A Technical Manual for Reading Plays* was published in 1983 by Southern Illinois University Press. It's under a hundred pages long, and it has been a standard text in theatre programs ever since, assigned to directors, actors, designers and dramaturgs as well as playwrights.

Ball came out of professional and university theatre, with roles at the Guthrie Theater and Duke University's drama department. He later became a well-known trial consultant, applying theatre craft to how lawyers tell stories to juries.

The book's subject is **reading**, not writing: how to take a script apart to see how it works before you put it on stage. But a reader's tool is also a writer's diagnostic. If your play doesn't survive Ball's reading, it probably won't survive rehearsal either.

His main example throughout is *Hamlet*.

## The big ideas

### Action: trigger and heap

Ball defines an **action** precisely. Something happens that causes or permits something else to happen. The first event is the **trigger**; the event it produces is the **heap**.

That's all an action is: two linked events. A ghost tells Hamlet he was murdered (trigger), so Hamlet swears revenge (heap). The pair is the basic unit of a play, the way a beat is for *Robert McKee*.

The crucial step is the next one. Every heap becomes the trigger for the next action. The play is a chain, like dominoes falling, from the first event to the last. Sometimes one trigger produces several heaps at once, and the chain branches.

### Read it backwards

Here's the book's signature move. Reading forwards, you feel the story the way an audience does: you're carried along, and gaps in logic slip past. So, Ball says, read it again **backwards**.

Start with the last event. Ask what caused or allowed it. Find that trigger, then ask what caused *it*. Keep going, event by event, until you reach the opening. Now you have the play's real spine, every link checked.

Reading backwards also corrects lazy assumptions. It's tempting to say the play ends with Hamlet killing Claudius "because the ghost told him to". Ball points out that the immediate cause is what happened a moment before: the poisoned sword, the dying Laertes revealing the plot. Big themes don't cause events. Specific events do.

### Stasis and intrusion

Every play, in Ball's account, begins in some kind of **stasis**: a state of balance, not necessarily a happy one. Something **intrudes** and upsets it. That intrusion releases forces that collide, and the play runs until those forces are no longer in conflict and a **new stasis** is reached. Then the play ends.

In *Hamlet*, the stasis is a grieving, depressed prince in a court that has moved on; the intrusion is the Ghost. Compare the disruption in *The shared core*: the same idea, seen by a theatre practitioner reading a script.

### Obstacles and conflict

Characters want things, and a play needs things in the way. Ball asks readers to identify each character's wants and the specific obstacles they meet, because conflict lives in those collisions, not in arguments for their own sake.

### Forwards

The second half of the title is about the audience. Ball calls an audience hungry for "next": a good play constantly makes you want to know what happens, or how, or when. **Forwards** are the devices that create that appetite: questions raised and not yet answered, promises, threats, plans announced, hints of a secret. A scene that only resolves and opens nothing new stalls the play.

### Exposition as action

Ball treats exposition, the information about the past, as something characters need and use, not a lecture for the audience. Good exposition is delivered because someone in the scene wants something from it.

### Character, image and theme come later

Ball deliberately starts with the mechanics. Character, he argues, is revealed through action. Images recur and gather meaning. Theme is what the whole chain adds up to, so it can only be understood after you've traced the chain, not used as a shortcut around it.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Write your play's events as a numbered chain, then read it backwards. Every heap should have a trigger in the line before. A link you can't name is a weak spot an actor will find in week two of rehearsal.
- **Screen:** The same check works for screenplays, where cause-and-effect plotting is the norm. Ball's "forwards" are what screenwriters call hooks and dangling causes: every scene should leave something unresolved that pulls you into the next.
- **Journalism:** Reconstructing how something happened is reading backwards: start from the outcome (the collapse, the conviction) and trace each immediate cause. It's a good discipline against vague explanations like "a culture of neglect" when a specific decision did the damage.

## The pushback

- **Built for causal plays.** The trigger-and-heap model fits *Hamlet*, Ibsen and most realist drama beautifully. It fits less well with plays built on repetition, image or association, like those of Beckett, Fornés or Suzan-Lori Parks (see *Suzan-Lori Parks*).
- **Mechanics first, meaning later.** Some teachers find the method too mechanical, especially for poetic or political theatre where the ideas are the structure.
- **A reading tool.** It diagnoses; it doesn't generate. You can't write a first draft by drawing dominoes, though many writers use it in revision.
- **Slim by design.** The book's brevity is part of its appeal, but it leaves out much: genre, language, staging, and the history of dramatic form.

## Read next

- David Ball, *Backwards and Forwards: A Technical Manual for Reading Plays* (1983). Read it with a copy of *Hamlet* open.
- William Shakespeare, *Hamlet*, the book's running example.
- Eric Bentley, *The Life of the Drama* (1964), a richer, more literary companion on how plays work.

## Check yourself

Q: What, for Ball, is an action?
A: A pair of events: a trigger, something that happens, and a heap, something it causes or permits to happen.

Q: Why read a play backwards?
A: Reading forwards, you're carried along and miss gaps. Reading from the last event back to the first forces you to name each immediate cause and reveals the true chain.

Q: What's wrong with saying Hamlet kills Claudius "because the Ghost told him to"?
A: It skips the chain. The immediate trigger is what happens just before: the revelation of the poisoned sword plot. Specific events cause events.

Q: What are stasis and intrusion?
A: Stasis is the balance at the start of a play; intrusion is the event that upsets it and releases the conflicting forces. The play ends when a new stasis is reached.

Q: What are "forwards"?
A: Devices that make the audience hungry for what comes next: unanswered questions, promises, threats, plans, secrets.
