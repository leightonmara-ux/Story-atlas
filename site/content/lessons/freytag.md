## Who and when

Gustav Freytag was a German novelist, playwright and journalist. He co-edited the political weekly *Die Grenzboten*, wrote a successful comedy about newspapermen, *Die Journalisten* (1852), and a best-selling novel, *Soll und Haben* (1855). In 1863 he published *Die Technik des Dramas*, a practical study of how serious plays are built. An English translation by Elias J. MacEwan, *Freytag's Technique of the Drama*, appeared in the 1890s.

His models were Greek tragedy, Shakespeare, and the German classics of Lessing, Goethe and Schiller. The book is long and detailed, but almost everyone remembers it for one drawing: a line rising to a peak and falling away again. That is Freytag's pyramid, and it is probably the most reproduced diagram in the history of writing instruction.

The irony is that the version most of us learned at school isn't quite his.

## The big ideas

### Five parts and three moments

Freytag divides a drama into **five parts**, joined by **three moments** (he calls them crises or forces) that tip the action from one part into the next. In MacEwan's English, the full sequence runs:

1. **Introduction:** the situation, the place, the people, the mood. Who is who before anything happens.
2. **Exciting force:** the moment that sets the action going, where the hero's will or an opponent's first stirs.
3. **Rise:** the action builds, with complications and growing tension.
4. **Climax:** the high point, where the hero's fortunes or power stand at their peak, or where the decisive deed is done.
5. **Tragic force:** the moment just after the climax that starts the downward turn.
6. **Return or fall:** the counter-forces gather and the hero's fortunes decline.
7. **Force of final suspense:** a last flicker of hope, a moment where escape seems possible, before the end.
8. **Catastrophe:** the downfall, usually the hero's death, which closes the play.

### The climax is in the middle

This is the single most important thing to understand about Freytag, and the thing most often garbled. **His climax sits at the center of the play**, roughly the third act of five, not near the end.

In a tragedy, the climax is where the hero's rise peaks: the moment of greatest power, or the fateful act that will undo him. Everything after is the fall. Think of *Macbeth*: the murders that make him king lie in the first half; the second half is the long, inevitable collapse. The play's most violent ending, the catastrophe, is a separate part entirely.

So Freytag's pyramid is symmetrical: up for half the play, down for the other half. When modern textbooks put "climax" near the end and call everything after it "falling action" or "denouement", they are describing a different shape, closer to the comedy or the Hollywood film, and borrowing Freytag's name for it.

### Two halves, two movements

Freytag saw a drama as built from two opposed halves: the **play** of the hero's will pushing outward, and the **counter-play** of the forces pushing back. In the first half the hero drives the action; in the second, the action drives the hero. He noted that either can lead: some plays open with the hero on the attack, others with forces pressing in.

This is an early version of the "midpoint turn" that later screenwriting gurus made famous: halfway through, the direction of the story reverses.

### Why the fall is hard

Freytag was frank that the second half is the harder one to write. Once the high point has passed, the audience can see where things are heading, and interest can sag. That's why he valued the **force of final suspense**: a late moment of uncertainty, a reprieve or a chance of rescue, that renews the question before the catastrophe answers it.

Every writer who has struggled with a saggy third act is wrestling with Freytag's problem.

### Five acts, not a formula

The five parts map loosely onto the five-act form of the plays he studied, but Freytag didn't insist that every part fill exactly one act. He offered the scheme as a way to see how great plays were built and to diagnose weak ones. Like *Aristotle*, he was describing what he admired more than legislating.

## The pyramid you learned at school

The classroom version has five labels, usually **exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution** (or denouement). It's a useful simplification, but notice what it does:

- It drops his three moments, including the exciting force, which is close to what we now call the inciting incident.
- It often moves the climax toward the end.
- It swaps tragedy's "catastrophe" for a neutral "resolution", so the shape can fit comedies, novels and films.

That adapted shape is fine to use. Just don't attribute it to Freytag, and don't be surprised when his own "climax" turns up in the middle.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Freytag's home ground. For tragedy and serious drama, ask where the hero's fortunes peak and whether the fall has its own momentum. Many full-length plays still turn on a central reversal.
- **Screen:** The midpoint of a feature film is Freytag's climax in a new job: a high or low that reverses the direction of the story. His force of final suspense survives as the last-minute hope before the end.
- **Journalism:** Profiles of rise and fall (a career, a company, a scandal) often follow his symmetrical shape: the climb, the peak, the turn, the long decline. Placing the peak in the middle of the piece can give a feature a satisfying balance.

## The pushback

- **Built for tragedy:** the pyramid describes five-act plays ending in a death. Comedies, modern plays and most films don't have a long symmetrical fall.
- **Modern stories peak late:** audiences now expect tension to keep rising almost to the end, with a short aftermath. Freytag's long fall can feel slack to them, as he himself feared.
- **One shape for everything:** critics of the pyramid as taught in schools argue that it trains students to see all stories as one curve, and pushes aside forms such as Kishōtenketsu, episodic narrative or the braided structures of many novels.
- **A name on someone else's diagram:** the school version is so far from Freytag's own that some teachers prefer to call it simply the "dramatic arc".

## Check yourself

Q: Where does Freytag's climax fall in a five-act tragedy?
A: In the middle, around the third act. It is the hero's high point, after which the action falls toward the catastrophe.

Q: Name Freytag's five parts in order.
A: Introduction, rise, climax, return or fall, catastrophe.

Q: What are his three moments, and where does each come?
A: The exciting force, between introduction and rise; the tragic force, just after the climax; the force of final suspense, late in the fall, just before the catastrophe.

Q: Why did Freytag value the force of final suspense?
A: After the climax the audience can see where things are going and interest can flag. A last moment of hope renews the question before the end.

Q: How does the school "plot pyramid" differ from Freytag's?
A: It drops his three moments, usually moves the climax toward the end, and replaces the tragic catastrophe with a neutral resolution or denouement.
