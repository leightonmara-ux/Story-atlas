## Who and when

Vladimir Propp was a Russian folklorist who taught at Leningrad University. In 1928 he published *Morphology of the Folktale*, a study of one hundred Russian wonder tales (the fairy tales of witches, dragons and magic gifts) from Alexander Afanasyev's great nineteenth-century collection.

"Morphology" is a word from botany: the study of the forms of plants and how their parts relate. Propp wanted to do the same for stories, to describe them by their parts rather than by their subjects. For three decades the book was little known outside Russia. Its English translation in 1958 changed that, and it became a founding text of structuralism and narratology, the study of how narratives work.

Propp wasn't trying to teach anyone to write. But writers, game designers and media scholars have mined his book ever since, because he did something no one had done so rigorously: he showed that hundreds of different-looking tales were built from the same small set of moves.

## The big ideas

### Functions, not characters

Earlier folklorists had sorted tales by their content: tales about animals, tales about magic objects, and so on. Propp saw that this didn't work. In one tale a king gives the hero an eagle that carries him to another kingdom; in another an old man gives him a horse; in another a princess gives him a ring. The names and objects change. What stays the same is the **act**: someone gives the hero a magical means of getting somewhere.

Propp called these constant acts **functions**: what a character does, defined by its significance for the course of the action. The same act can mean different things in different places, and different acts can serve the same function. What counts is the job it does in the story.

### Four claims

Propp summed up his findings in four bold theses:

1. Functions are the stable, constant elements of a tale, whoever performs them and however they're performed.
2. The number of functions in the wonder tale is limited.
3. The sequence of functions is always the same.
4. All wonder tales are of one type in their structure.

The third is the most surprising. Not every tale contains every function, but the ones that appear always come in the same order.

### The 31 functions

After an **initial situation** (the family is introduced, or the hero), Propp found 31 functions. You don't need to memorize them all, but the run of them is worth seeing, because it's a whole story in miniature:

- **Preparation:** someone in the family goes away (*absentation*). The hero is warned not to do something (*interdiction*). The warning is broken (*violation*). The villain scouts, is given information, tries to deceive, and the victim is fooled.
- **Complication:** the villain harms a member of the family (*villainy*), or someone simply lacks something, such as a bride or a magic object (*lack*). The misfortune becomes known and the hero is sent or decides to act. The hero leaves home (*departure*).
- **The donor:** the hero is tested by a **donor**, reacts, and receives a magical agent. The hero is led to the object of the search.
- **The struggle:** hero and villain fight (*struggle*). The hero is marked (*branding*). The villain is defeated (*victory*). The original misfortune or lack is put right.
- **Return:** the hero returns, is pursued, and is rescued.
- **The second move:** in many tales the hero comes home unrecognized, a **false hero** makes false claims, a **difficult task** is set and solved, the hero is recognized, the false hero is exposed, the hero is given a new appearance, the villain is punished, and the hero marries and takes the throne (*wedding*).

Propp noticed that many tales are made of more than one **move**, sequences that run from a villainy or lack through to its resolution, and that one move can be nested inside another. That's how a tale can seem to end and then start again.

### Seven roles

The functions are shared out among a small cast. Propp calls them **spheres of action**, and there are seven:

- **The villain:** causes the harm, fights the hero, pursues.
- **The donor (or provider):** tests the hero and supplies the magical agent.
- **The helper:** the magical helper who moves, rescues, solves tasks.
- **The princess (a sought-for person) and her father:** sets difficult tasks, recognizes the true hero, exposes the false one. Propp treats them together, since in the tales they aren't always clearly separate.
- **The dispatcher:** sends the hero off.
- **The hero:** either a seeker who goes out to put something right, or a victim who is carried off.
- **The false hero:** claims the hero's success.

One person can fill several roles (a witch might test the hero and then help him), and one role can be shared among several people. Again, the job matters, not the name.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Pantomime and fairy-tale theatre follow Propp's sequence almost exactly. For other plays, his roles are a quick way to check a cast: who is the donor here? Is someone set up as a false hero?
- **Screen:** Fantasy and adventure films often line up well with Propp's sequence, and the donor's test is everywhere: the hero must prove worthy before receiving the sword, the map or the skill. The false hero who claims credit is a durable source of late-act tension.
- **Journalism:** No reporter should cast real people as villains and heroes. But it helps to notice when a story is being shaped into Propp's mold, by you or by your sources: a villainy, a lack, a rescuer, a reward. Asking "who has been cast as the false hero here?" is a useful check on spin.

## The pushback

- **One corpus.** Propp studied one hundred Russian wonder tales. He didn't claim his sequence held for all stories, and it often fails for other genres or traditions. Later writers who stretched it into a universal formula went further than he did.
- **Form without meaning.** The anthropologist Claude Lévi-Strauss, while admiring the book, criticized it in 1960 for separating form from content and ignoring what tales mean in their cultures. Propp replied sharply.
- **The fixed order is less rigid than it sounds.** Propp himself had to allow for nested moves, doubled functions and some exceptions.
- **Formula risk.** Applied to modern writing, the scheme can turn into fill-in-the-blanks plotting with stock villains and princesses as prizes, a pattern many contemporary writers set out to subvert.

## Try it

Look at your story the way Propp looked at a tale: by what each character does, not who they are.

1. List the main acts in your story and give each one a role: villain, donor, helper, sought-for person, dispatcher, hero or false hero. One person may play several roles, and one role may be shared.
2. Look for gaps. Is there a donor's test before the hero gets what they need? Would a false hero add tension late on?
3. Then take one function, such as the donor's test, and write it with completely new surface: a setting, a tester and a gift Propp never saw. Same job, new clothes.

- **Stage:** check your cast list by role. Two characters doing the same job may be one character.
- **Screen:** in a fantasy or adventure script, make sure the hero earns the magical agent by passing a test, rather than simply being handed it.
- **Journalism:** don't cast real people. Instead, list how your sources are casting each other: who is called the villain, who the rescuer, who the false hero claiming credit?

**Check:** For each character, can you name the job they do for the action?

## Check yourself

Q: What is a function, in Propp's sense?
A: An act of a character, defined by what it does for the course of the action, regardless of who performs it or how.

Q: Of Propp's four theses, which is the most surprising?
A: That the sequence of functions is always the same. Not every tale contains every function, but the ones that appear come in the same order.

Q: Name the seven spheres of action.
A: Villain, donor, helper, princess (and her father), dispatcher, hero and false hero.

Q: A witch tests the hero and then gives him a magic ring. Which role is she playing?
A: The donor, who tests the hero and supplies the magical agent.

Q: What was Lévi-Strauss's main criticism?
A: That Propp separated form from content, describing structure while ignoring what the tales mean in their culture.
