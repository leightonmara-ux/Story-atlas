## Where it comes from

A side character usually gets a few minutes of screen time, a scene or two on stage, a paragraph in a feature. In that space they have to be recognizable, do their job in the story, and ideally stick in the memory. Writers have worked out how to do this for centuries, and the masters of it left plenty of evidence.

The great example is **Charles Dickens** (1812–1870), whose novels are crowded with minor people that readers still quote. The great defence is **E. M. Forster**'s, in *Aspects of the Novel* (1927), where he split characters into flat and round and refused to look down on the flat ones (see the *E. M. Forster* lesson).

The practical toolkit comes from writing manuals and writers' rooms. The word **tag**, for a quick identifying trait, is common in genre-fiction manuals; Dwight V. Swain used it in *Techniques of the Selling Writer* (1965). The maxim that "everyone is the hero of their own story" is repeated by writing teachers everywhere, and has no single clear source.

## The big ideas

### Flat is a tool, not a failure

Forster's flat character is built around a single idea or quality. His point, worth repeating, is that flat characters are useful: we recognize them the moment they reappear, and we remember them afterward. A story with a large cast can't make everyone round, and shouldn't try. The question for a side character is not "is this person deep?" but "is this person *vivid*?"

### One vivid trait

The fastest way to make a minor character memorable is to give them one strong, specific thing the audience can hang them on. A tag can be:

- **A way of speaking.** A catchphrase, a verbal tic, a habit of formality or slang.
- **An object.** Something they carry, wear or fuss over.
- **A behaviour.** A gesture they repeat, a ritual, a way of entering a room.
- **An attitude.** A single fixed stance toward the world, played to the hilt.

The trick is specificity. "Nervous" is not a tag. A man who keeps checking that the stove is off, in a story where nobody is cooking, is.

### Dickens, the master

Look at how Dickens does it, and you can borrow the method.

- **Mr. Micawber** (*David Copperfield*, 1850) is always broke and always cheerful, forever expecting that something will "turn up". He even gives young David a little lecture on money: spend slightly less than you earn and you're happy; slightly more, and you're miserable. A whole philosophy, voiced by a man who never follows it.
- **Miss Havisham** (*Great Expectations*, 1861) was jilted on her wedding day and stopped time. She still wears the yellowing dress, the clocks are stopped, the wedding cake is rotting on the table. One image tells you her whole history and her obsession.
- **Uriah Heep** (*David Copperfield*) keeps insisting how "umble" he is, while wringing his hands. The tag is also a warning: his humility is a performance.
- **Mr. Jaggers** (*Great Expectations*), the lawyer, washes his hands with scented soap after seeing clients, as if to rinse off their crimes.

Notice that the best tags aren't random decorations. Each one expresses who the person is, or what they're hiding.

### Give them their own want

A side character who exists only to serve the hero feels like furniture. The cure is to give them something they want, even if we only glimpse it. The waiter wants his shift to end. The witness wants to be believed. The hero's sister wants, for once, not to be the sensible one.

Tom Stoppard took this idea to its limit in *Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead* (first staged 1966), which puts two minor characters from *Hamlet* at the center and leaves Hamlet in the margins. Jean Rhys did the same in *Wide Sargasso Sea* (1966), which gives the "madwoman in the attic" from *Jane Eyre* a life and a story of her own. Both are reminders that every walk-on is the lead somewhere.

Dickens's **Wemmick**, Jaggers's clerk in *Great Expectations*, shows the payoff. At the office he is dry and wooden, always advising people to collect "portable property". At home in Walworth he lives in a tiny mock castle, complete with drawbridge, and tenderly looks after his deaf old father. The second side makes him unforgettable.

### The entrance

A side character often gets one shot to register, so make their first moment count. In *Jaws* (1975), the shark hunter Quint first appears at a town meeting by dragging his fingernails down a blackboard to silence the room. Before he says a word, we know he's abrasive, theatrical and fearless. The lesson *Introducing a character* goes further into first impressions.

### The side character who changes the hero

Some minor characters exist to turn the story. In Victor Hugo's *Les Misérables* (1862), the Bishop of Digne takes in Jean Valjean, a released convict. Valjean steals his silver; the police drag him back; and the bishop tells them the silver was a gift, then hands Valjean his candlesticks too. The bishop is barely in the book after that, but his act of mercy drives the rest of Valjean's life. A good catalyst character is small on the page and huge in effect.

In *Casablanca* (1942), Captain Renault plays a different role: the cynic who mirrors the hero's own cynicism. He gets one of the most famous minor-character moments in film, declaring himself "shocked, shocked" to find gambling in Rick's café, just as he's handed his winnings. One line, and his whole charming corruption is on display.

### Tokens and stereotypes

The danger of the one-trait method is that the trait becomes the character's identity, group or background. A side character who exists only to be "the gay friend", or the wise elder from another culture who helps the hero and then fades, is a token.

Critics have named some of these patterns. In 2007 the critic Nathan Rabin coined "manic pixie dream girl" in an *A.V. Club* review of *Elizabethtown*, for a quirky young woman who exists to teach a gloomy hero to enjoy life. He later said he regretted how the phrase was used, but it caught on because it named something real. The test is the same as before: does this person want anything for themselves? The lesson *Who gets to be the subject* explains why the single story about a group does harm.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Actors need something to play, so a minor role with a want and one clear physical habit is a gift. In a big-cast play, distinct tags help the audience track who's who, especially when actors double.
- **Screen:** Give every speaking part a want, even for one line. Write the tag into the action line where we first see them, and pay it off once later.
- **Journalism:** The minor source can be rendered in one telling detail, what Tom Wolfe called *status details* (see *Tom Wolfe & the New Journalism*): what they wear, carry or do. Gay Talese's "Frank Sinatra Has a Cold" (*Esquire*, 1966) is full of minor figures caught this way, including the woman whose job is to look after Sinatra's hairpieces. The detail must be true and fair, not a sneer.

## The pushback

- **Tags become tics.** Repeat the catchphrase too often and it stops being character and becomes a gimmick. Dickens's critics accused him of caricature even in his own time.
- **Not everyone should be memorable.** Some characters need to stay in the background so the story can focus. A bus driver with a tragic backstory can pull a scene off course.
- **Flatness can be lazy.** Forster defended flat characters, but a flat character drawn from a stereotype rather than observation is just a cliché.
- **The hero's own story can drown.** Giving every minor character a want is a good discipline, but the main line still has to dominate.

## Try it

Pick three minor characters from your draft, or from a film or play you know well.

1. Write each one's want in a sentence, even if the story never states it.
2. Give each a single tag: a way of speaking, an object, a behaviour or an attitude. Make it specific enough to draw.
3. Check that each tag says something about who they are or what they hide, as Jaggers's hand-washing does.
4. Rewrite each character's first moment so the tag shows up straight away, and plan one later moment to repeat or twist it.

- **Stage:** Give each minor role one physical action the actor can repeat, and one line that reveals their want.
- **Screen:** Put the tag in the character's introduction line, then pay it off in one later scene.
- **Journalism:** For each secondary source in a draft, replace their job title or adjective with one accurate, observed detail.

**Check:** Could a reader describe each minor character in one vivid phrase, and say what that person wants?

## Check yourself

Q: Why did Forster defend flat characters?
A: They are easy to recognize when they reappear and easy to remember, and a large cast can't make everyone round.

Q: What's a tag?
A: A single, specific identifying trait, such as a way of speaking, an object, a behaviour or an attitude, that makes a minor character recognizable.

Q: What makes Dickens's best tags work?
A: They're not random: each expresses who the character is or what they hide, like Miss Havisham's stopped clocks or Uriah Heep's false humility.

Q: How do you stop a side character feeling like furniture?
A: Give them a want of their own, even if we only glimpse it.

Q: What's the test for whether a minor character has become a token?
A: Whether they want anything for themselves, or exist only to serve the hero and stand for a group.
