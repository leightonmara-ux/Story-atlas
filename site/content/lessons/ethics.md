## Where it comes from

Every other lesson in this track teaches you to shape facts into a story. This one asks what that shaping costs, and where it must stop. Two writers frame the debate.

**John Hersey** wrote *Hiroshima* (1946), the account of six survivors of the atomic bomb that filled an entire issue of *The New Yorker* and is often cited as a founding work of narrative journalism. In 1980, in an essay in *The Yale Review* titled "The Legend on the License", he took aim at writers who, in his view, had blurred the line between fact and fiction, among them Truman Capote, Norman Mailer and Tom Wolfe.

**Janet Malcolm**, a longtime *New Yorker* writer, published *The Journalist and the Murderer* first in that magazine in 1989 and as a book in 1990. It examines the relationship between the writer Joe McGinniss and Jeffrey MacDonald, a former Army doctor convicted of murdering his wife and two daughters, about whom McGinniss wrote the bestseller *Fatal Vision* (1983).

Hersey's question is about the reader: what can you do to the facts? Malcolm's is about the subject: what are you doing to the people?

## The big ideas

### Hersey's rule: the writer must not invent

Hersey drew a sharp line. A novelist may borrow from real life as much as they like. A journalist may not borrow from imagination at all. The reader of journalism has to be able to trust that what's presented as fact happened. His image was of a license, like a driver's license, that every journalist carries, bearing a single legend: nothing in this was made up.

The problem Hersey saw in some New Journalism was invented or imagined material passed off under journalism's authority: thoughts attributed to people that no one could know, scenes heightened beyond the record. Mixing even a little invention into fact, he argued, makes it impossible for readers to trust any of it.

Note what Hersey *didn't* forbid. *Hiroshima* itself reconstructs scenes from long interviews with survivors. Reconstruction from evidence is reporting. Invention is not.

### Malcolm's charge: the confidence trick

*The Journalist and the Murderer* opens with one of the most quoted sentences in modern journalism:

> Every journalist who is not too stupid or too full of himself to notice what is going on knows that what he does is morally indefensible.
> — Janet Malcolm, The Journalist and the Murderer

Her case: McGinniss had been given close access to MacDonald's defense during the trial and, in letters, had kept expressing sympathy and friendship. The book he published presented MacDonald as guilty. MacDonald sued McGinniss for fraud, and the case was settled in 1987.

Malcolm's larger claim is that this isn't an aberration but the structure of the job. Subjects talk to journalists hoping to be understood on their own terms. The journalist encourages that hope, because it produces material, and then writes the story *they* see. The shaping that makes a narrative good, choosing a protagonist, a complication, a meaning, is exactly what the subject didn't consent to.

You don't have to accept her absolute verdict to take the point: narrative structure is imposed on real people, and they may not recognize themselves in it.

### The techniques in question

Most ethical debates in narrative nonfiction come down to a short list of practices.

| Technique | What it means | Mainstream view today |
|---|---|---|
| **Composite characters** | Combining several real people into one figure | Not acceptable in journalism without clear disclosure, and many editors forbid it outright |
| **Reconstructed scenes** | Writing a scene the writer didn't witness, from interviews and documents | Accepted when carefully sourced, and ideally signaled to readers |
| **Interior monologue** | Saying what someone thought or felt | Only when the person told you, and you're confident of it |
| **Compression** | Merging several conversations or days into one scene | Contested; at minimum, disclose it in a note |
| **Rearranged chronology** | Telling events out of order | Fine as structure, as long as the reader isn't misled about what happened when |
| **Invented quotations, events or details** | Making things up | Not acceptable. This is Hersey's line |

### The reader's contract

Behind all of these is an idea often called the **reader's contract**: the unstated agreement about what a label like "nonfiction" or "journalism" promises. Different forms carry different contracts. A newspaper story promises literal accuracy. A memoir is widely understood to rely on memory and reconstruct dialogue. A play "based on a true story" promises very little.

Most ethical failures are breaches of that contract: giving readers less than the form promised, without telling them. Hence the importance of **disclosure**: an author's note explaining what was reconstructed, compressed or changed lets the reader adjust their trust.

### Why the stakes are high

When invention is exposed, the damage spreads. In 1981 Janet Cooke of *The Washington Post* returned a Pulitzer Prize after admitting that the boy at the center of her story about a child heroin addict didn't exist. In 1998 *The New Republic* found that Stephen Glass had fabricated material in many of his articles. Each case made readers more suspicious of every vivid true story.

## Use it

- **Journalism:** Before publishing a narrative, mark every sentence that isn't directly witnessed or documented. For each one: how do you know? If the answer is "I imagined it", cut it. Consider an author's note for reconstructions and compressions. And think about Malcolm: what have you led your subject to expect?
- **Screen:** "Based on a true story" carries a looser contract, but audiences still feel betrayed when they learn key events were invented. Composites and compressions are normal in fact-based films; consider whether the invention changes the meaning of what happened.
- **Stage:** Verbatim and documentary theater promise real words. If you edit or combine testimony, the audience needs to know. For plays about real living people, Malcolm's question about consent is as sharp as in print.

## The pushback

- **Malcolm overstates.** Many journalists reject her opening as a sweeping generalization. Good reporters, they argue, can be honest with subjects about what they're doing. Critics also noted that Malcolm was herself in a long libel suit, brought by Jeffrey Masson over quotations in her reporting, which she ultimately won.
- **Hersey's line is blurrier than it looks.** All writing involves selection, and selection shapes meaning. Some argue that reconstruction and compression are already a kind of invention.
- **Emotional truth.** Some essayists and memoirists argue that changing small facts can serve a deeper truth. The debate between the writer John D'Agata and his fact-checker Jim Fingal in *The Lifespan of a Fact* (2012) dramatizes this view and its critics.
- **Different traditions.** Standards vary by country, period and form, and what counts as acceptable shaping has changed over time.

## Check yourself

Q: What was Hersey's rule for journalism?
A: The writer must not invent. A novelist may borrow from reality, but a journalist may not borrow from imagination.

Q: Does Hersey's rule forbid reconstructed scenes?
A: No. Reconstruction from careful interviews and documents is reporting; *Hiroshima* itself is reconstructed. Invention is what's forbidden.

Q: What is Malcolm's central charge?
A: That journalists win subjects' trust in order to get material, then write the story they see, which the subject never agreed to. She treats this as built into the job.

Q: What is the reader's contract?
A: The unstated agreement about what a form, such as news, memoir or "based on a true story", promises about accuracy. Ethical failures are breaches of it.

Q: A writer merges three interviews into one scene. What should they do?
A: Compression is contested. At minimum, disclose it, for example in an author's note, so readers aren't misled.
