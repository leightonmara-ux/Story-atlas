## Who and when

Blake Snyder (1957–2009) was a working Hollywood screenwriter who made his name selling spec scripts, mostly family comedies. His produced credits include *Stop! Or My Mom Will Shoot* (1992) and *Blank Check* (1994). He knew the business from the selling end: how a script gets pitched, read, and bought.

His book *Save the Cat! The Last Book on Screenwriting You'll Ever Need* (2005) came from a small publisher of film books, Michael Wiese Productions, and spread largely by word of mouth. It's breezy, funny and very practical, and it became one of the best-selling screenwriting books ever. Snyder died suddenly in 2009, but the brand carried on, including spin-offs for novelists.

## The big ideas

### Start with the logline

Snyder's first chapter isn't about structure at all. It asks: *what is it?* Before writing, you should be able to pitch your movie in one or two sentences, a **logline**, that makes someone want to see it. He wanted a logline to have irony, a clear picture of the movie, a sense of audience and cost, and a killer title.

His reasoning was commercial and blunt. If you can't say what your movie is, a buyer can't sell it, and you probably don't know what it is either.

### Save the cat

The book's title names a scene: early on, the hero does something, like saving a cat, that makes us like them and want to follow them. It doesn't have to be literal or even nice. It's any moment that gives the audience a reason to root for the hero, even a difficult one.

### The beat sheet

{{diagram:snyder}}

Snyder's core tool is the **Blake Snyder Beat Sheet**: fifteen beats, each with a target page in a 110-page script.

1. **Opening Image** (p. 1): a snapshot of the hero's world before the story.
2. **Theme Stated** (p. 5): someone, often not the hero, poses the question the movie will answer.
3. **Set-Up** (pp. 1–10): the hero's world, and what's missing from their life.
4. **Catalyst** (p. 12): the event that knocks the hero's life off course.
5. **Debate** (pp. 12–25): the hero hesitates. Should I go?
6. **Break into Two** (p. 25): the hero chooses to enter a new world. Snyder stresses that it must be a choice.
7. **B Story** (p. 30): a second story, often a love story, which usually carries the theme.
8. **Fun and Games** (pp. 30–55): "the promise of the premise", the scenes the trailer is made from.
9. **Midpoint** (p. 55): a false victory or a false defeat, and the stakes are raised.
10. **Bad Guys Close In** (pp. 55–75): outside pressure grows and the hero's team falls apart.
11. **All Is Lost** (p. 75): the low point, often with what he calls a "whiff of death".
12. **Dark Night of the Soul** (pp. 75–85): the hero wallows before finding the way forward.
13. **Break into Three** (p. 85): A and B stories meet, and the hero finds the solution.
14. **Finale** (pp. 85–110): the hero applies what they've learned and the old world is transformed.
15. **Final Image** (p. 110): the opposite of the opening image, proof that change has happened.

The opening and final images work as a pair: put them side by side and you should see the whole story.

### Ten genres

Snyder argued that comedy, thriller and romance are too vague to help a writer. Instead he sorted films into ten story types by their underlying shape, such as **Monster in the House** (a monster, a confined space, and someone who brought the danger in), **Golden Fleece** (a road trip or quest), **Dude with a Problem**, **Rites of Passage**, **Buddy Love**, **Whydunit**, **The Fool Triumphant**, **Institutionalized**, **Out of the Bottle** and **Superhero**.

The point is to know what your audience has enjoyed before in your story type, so you can give them the same thing "only different".

### The board

To plan a script, Snyder used a corkboard with four rows (Act One, the first half of Act Two, the second half of Act Two, and Act Three) and about forty index cards, one per scene. Each card should show a change of emotion from start to end, marked with **+/−**, and the conflict in the scene, marked **><**. It's a tidy, physical version of the scene-turn test in *Robert McKee*.

### The rules of thumb

Snyder liked naming things. A few of his rules are now part of industry slang:

- **The Pope in the Pool:** bury exposition inside something entertaining.
- **Double Mumbo Jumbo:** audiences will accept one piece of magic per movie, not two.
- **Laying Pipe:** too much set-up before the story starts.
- **Covenant of the Arc:** every main character should change over the story, not just the hero.

## Use it

- **Screen:** The beat sheet's real value is diagnostic. If your Act Two drags, check for a Midpoint that raises the stakes. If the ending feels unearned, look for a missing Dark Night of the Soul. Treat page numbers as rough, especially for television.
- **Stage:** The page targets mean little for a play, but the Theme Stated beat and the paired opening and final images transfer well. Many plays open and close on a mirrored image.
- **Journalism:** You can't arrange a real person's catalyst on page 12. But the logline discipline is gold: if you can't pitch your story in two sentences, you haven't found it yet, which is *Jon Franklin*'s point too.

## The pushback

- **Every movie the same.** The best-known critique came in 2013, when Peter Suderman argued in *Slate* that big Hollywood films were following Snyder's beats almost to the minute, and that this was why so many blockbusters felt alike. Snyder's defenders reply that the beats describe what worked before the book, not a new formula.
- **The page numbers.** Snyder's fixed pages are more precise than anyone else's in this course. Critics say precision invites box-ticking; supporters say it's a useful check on pacing, not a law.
- **Likability isn't everything.** "Save the cat" can be read as a rule that heroes must be nice. Many great films and plays center on people we don't like at all, and hold us through fascination rather than sympathy.
- **Commercial by design.** The book is openly about selling scripts. That's its honesty and its limit: it says little about films that set out to break the pattern, the territory of the *Counterpoints* track.

## Try it

Run four of Snyder's quick tests on a script you're writing, or on a film you know well.

1. Write a logline in one or two sentences and say it aloud to someone. Do they want to see the movie?
2. Write your Opening Image and Final Image side by side. Are they opposites that show the whole change?
3. Find your Break into Two and your Midpoint. Is the break the hero's own choice? Is the midpoint a false victory or false defeat that raises the stakes?
4. Pick five scene cards and mark each with its emotional change (+/−) and its conflict (><). Rework any card that has neither.

- **Stage:** forget the page numbers and keep the mirrored images. Write the first and last stage pictures of your play as a pair.
- **Screen:** compare your pages with the beat sheet as a pacing check, not a rule, especially for television.
- **Journalism:** pitch your story to an editor in two sentences. If you can't, keep reporting until you can.

**Check:** If someone saw only your opening and final images, would they know what changed?

## Check yourself

Q: What is a "save the cat" moment?
A: An early scene where the hero does something that makes the audience want to root for them.

Q: What happens at Snyder's Midpoint?
A: A false victory or false defeat, and the stakes are raised. Fun and Games ends and the story gets serious.

Q: How should the Opening Image and the Final Image relate?
A: As opposites, or mirror images, so that together they show how much has changed.

Q: Why does Snyder want writers to sort their story into one of his ten genres?
A: So they know what the audience has enjoyed before in that kind of story and can give it to them "the same, only different".

Q: What's the Break into Two, and what must it be?
A: The hero entering the new world of Act Two, around page 25. It must be the hero's own choice, not something that just happens to them.
