## Who and when

Two critics, writing seventeen years apart, gave us the most influential accounts of why endings matter so much.

**Frank Kermode** (1919–2010) was a British critic, a scholar of Shakespeare and modern poetry, and a longtime contributor to the *London Review of Books*. ***The Sense of an Ending: Studies in the Theory of Fiction*** (1967) grew out of lectures he gave at Bryn Mawr College in 1965. It ranges across apocalyptic prophecy, theology and the modern novel. (Julian Barnes borrowed the title for his 2011 novel.)

**Peter Brooks** (born 1938) is an American critic who taught for many years at Yale. ***Reading for the Plot: Design and Intention in Narrative*** (1984) combines close readings of nineteenth-century novels with Freud's psychoanalysis to explain what drives readers through a story.

## The big ideas

### Kermode: we live in the middle

Kermode starts from a simple condition. We are born into the middle of things and die in the middle of things. We never see the beginning or the end of the world. So we make fictions that give the span of a life, or of history, a beginning and an end that make sense of the middle. Apocalyptic prophecy, which predicts the end of the world, is the extreme case. Novels are a gentler one.

What we want, he argues, is **concord**: a satisfying fit between beginning, middle and end, so that the end confirms what the beginning set going.

### Tick-tock

His most famous illustration is the ticking of a clock. The clock actually makes the same sound twice, but we hear *tick-tock*, a beginning and an end, with a meaningful interval between.

> Tick is a humble genesis, tock a feeble apocalypse.
> — Frank Kermode, *The Sense of an Ending*

The interval between tick and tock is organized time. The interval between tock and the next tick is just waiting. A plot, for Kermode, is a vastly extended tick-tock: it turns mere duration into time charged with meaning.

### Chronos and kairos

Kermode borrows two Greek words for time, familiar from New Testament theology. **Chronos** is passing time, one thing after another. **Kairos** is time filled with significance, a moment that matters because of its relation to the end. Fiction converts chronos into kairos. A scene in a novel matters not because time passed but because it bears on how things will come out.

### Peripeteia: the end by an unexpected road

If the end were simply what we predicted, the story would be dull. Kermode reinterprets Aristotle's *peripeteia*, the reversal (see *Aristotle*), as the falsification of our simple expectations. We want the end to come, but by a route we didn't foresee. The more daring the reversal, he suggests, the more a fiction respects our sense that reality is complicated, provided it still arrives at a concord we accept.

### Fictions, not myths

Kermode warns that a fiction can harden into a **myth** when people forget it's a fiction and take it as literal truth about the world. His example is the apocalyptic thinking of totalitarian politics. Good fictions are held knowingly: we use them to make sense of time while remembering we made them.

### Brooks: reading for the end

Brooks starts from the reader's experience. We read forward, driven by what he calls **narrative desire**: the wish to reach the end and find out what it all means. But meaning comes only in retrospect. He calls reading an **anticipation of retrospection**: we read now in expectation of the moment when we'll look back and understand. The end is what gives meaning to the middle.

### Freud's masterplot

To explain this pull, Brooks turns to Freud's ***Beyond the Pleasure Principle*** (1920). Freud suggested that living things seek to return to a state of rest, but each in its own way and by its own path, not by any short cut. Brooks reads this as a model of plot:

- The **beginning** arouses energy, a tension that wants discharge.
- The **middle** is a detour. It delays the end, and through repetitions, returns and doublings it binds that energy so it can be mastered rather than spent at once.
- The **end** is the discharge, the rest the story was seeking.

The danger in the middle is the **short circuit**: reaching the end too soon, or reaching the wrong end. A story that resolves before its energy has been worked through feels cheap. A good middle is the right length of detour.

### Winnett's challenge

In "Coming Unstrung" (*PMLA*, 1990), the critic **Susan Winnett** praised Brooks's account but argued that its model of arousal, build-up and discharge mirrors male sexual experience, and so describes what men have wanted from narrative. She asked what a theory of plot would look like if it started from women's experience, in which pleasure and significance can lie in beginnings and continuations rather than in a climactic end.

## Use it

- **Screen:** Brooks's short circuit names a common note: "it's resolved too early." Kermode's peripeteia names another: "I saw the ending coming." Aim for an end that is surprising in route and satisfying in fit.
- **Stage:** Kermode's tick-tock is a test for scenes as well as whole plays. Does the end of the scene answer its beginning, or does it just stop?
- **Journalism:** Real events don't end tidily. Kermode suggests why readers want them to, and why that wish should make reporters careful: the end you choose (an arrest, a verdict, a quote) gives the whole piece its meaning. Choose the ending knowing it's a choice, as *Hayden White & Joan Didion* would remind you.

## The pushback

- **The novel-centered view.** Both critics build on the Western realist novel. Forms like serial television, *kishōtenketsu*, or *Le Guin*'s carrier bag don't hinge on a climactic end in the same way.
- **Freud as foundation.** Brooks's model depends on a speculative essay by Freud that many psychologists no longer accept. Readers can treat it as a metaphor rather than science.
- **Gender.** Winnett's critique questions whether arousal-and-discharge is a universal shape or one experience presented as everyone's.
- **Modernism.** Kermode himself notes that modern fiction often resists concord. Open endings are a response to his argument, not a refutation of it.

- Susan Winnett, "Coming Unstrung: Women, Men, Narrative, and Principles of Pleasure", *PMLA* 105 (1990).

## Try it

Write the last paragraph or final scene of a piece you're working on, then put it beside the opening.

1. List three things the opening sets going: a question, an image, a desire.
2. Check whether your ending answers each one. That's Kermode's concord.
3. Mark the point in the draft where a reader could first guess the ending. If it's early, add a reversal: keep the destination, change the road.
4. Find any moment where tension is released before the end. Is it a short circuit, or a necessary pause?

- **Stage:** Make the final image echo the first. Tick, then tock.
- **Screen:** Write two endings, one predictable and one reached by a reversal. Which earns its fit?
- **Journalism:** List three possible final paragraphs. Ask what meaning each one imposes on the whole piece.

**Check:** Reading your ending first, then the opening, does the opening now mean more?

## Check yourself

Q: What does Kermode's tick-tock illustrate?
A: That we impose a beginning and an end on mere sequence. A plot is an extended tick-tock: time organized between a beginning and an end so the interval means something.

Q: What is the difference between chronos and kairos?
A: Chronos is passing time; kairos is time charged with meaning by its relation to an end. Fiction turns the first into the second.

Q: How does Kermode reinterpret peripeteia?
A: As the falsification of simple expectation: we want the end, but reached by a route we didn't foresee.

Q: What does Brooks mean by the "anticipation of retrospection"?
A: We read forward expecting the moment when the end will let us look back and understand what came before.

Q: What is a short circuit, for Brooks?
A: Reaching the end too soon or the wrong way, before the middle's detours have worked through the story's energy.
