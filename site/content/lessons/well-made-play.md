## Where it comes from

The *pièce bien faite*, or "well-made play", was the most successful commercial formula of the nineteenth-century stage. Its master was the French dramatist **Eugène Scribe** (1791–1861), who turned out several hundred plays, comedies, vaudevilles and opera libretti, many of them with collaborators, as if from a workshop. His best-known works include *Le Verre d'eau* (*The Glass of Water*, 1840), in which great affairs of state turn on tiny accidents, and *Adrienne Lecouvreur* (1849, with Ernest Legouvé).

His successor was **Victorien Sardou** (1831–1908), author of *Les Pattes de mouche* (1860, known in English as *A Scrap of Paper*) and *Tosca* (1887), which Puccini turned into the opera. Sardou's plays toured the world, often as vehicles for Sarah Bernhardt.

Scribe never wrote a manual. The "rules" were reconstructed later by critics who noticed how reliably his plays ran. The most widely cited summary is the scholar Stephen Stanton's list of seven features, from 1957.

The form matters to you because almost everything later playwrights did either borrows its machinery or rebels against it, and much of what screenwriting manuals call "setup and payoff" started here.

## The big ideas

### A machine for suspense

A well-made play is built so that the audience always knows a little more than some characters and a little less than they want. Its parts:

- **Exposition up front.** The first act lays out the situation, often through servants or old friends catching each other up. The past is the fuse.
- **A secret.** Something the audience knows, or half-knows, is hidden from a key character: a compromising letter, a hidden parent, a forged signature. The whole play is the slow burn toward its exposure.
- **Ups and downs.** The hero's fortunes swing back and forth, each swing sharper than the last. Letters go astray, people arrive at the wrong moment, a misunderstanding (*quiproquo*) makes characters act on false beliefs.
- **The obligatory scene.** The confrontation the audience has been waiting for, where the secret comes out in front of the person who most needs to hear it.
- **A logical dénouement.** Every thread is tied off; nothing is left unexplained.
- **The same pattern in miniature.** Each act has its own rising action and curtain hook, so the audience comes back after the interval.

### The obligatory scene

The French critic **Francisque Sarcey** gave this its name, the *scène à faire*: literally the scene that has to be done. Once a play has promised a confrontation, the audience will feel cheated if it doesn't arrive. The British critic William Archer, in *Play-Making* (1912), translated it as the **obligatory scene**, and the term passed into English-language playwriting and screenwriting teaching.

It's a useful idea in any medium. Ask what your story has promised the audience, even without saying so, and check that you deliver it on stage, not offstage or in summary.

### Props as plot

Scribe and Sardou loved objects that carry the plot: a letter, a fan, a glass of water. An object planted early and used decisively later is the ancestor of every "plant and payoff" in modern film. The connection to what we now call Chekhov's gun is real, though Chekhov used it to make a very different kind of play (see *Anton Chekhov*).

### Ibsen takes over the machine

**Henrik Ibsen** learned the form from the inside. From 1851 he worked at the Norwegian theatre in Bergen, where the repertoire leaned heavily on French plays, Scribe's among them. When he came to write his modern prose dramas, he kept the machinery and changed what it was for.

*A Doll's House* (1879) is a well-made play almost to the letter. There's an old friend for exposition, a secret (Nora once borrowed money by forging her father's signature), a blackmailer, a damning letter locked in a letterbox, and a final act in which the husband reads it. Then, at the point where Scribe would tie everything up, Ibsen turns the play inside out. The crisis passes, Torvald is ready to forget it all, and Nora sits him down to talk about their marriage. Then she leaves.

### Shaw's attack: "Sardoodledom"

**George Bernard Shaw**, reviewing for the *Saturday Review* in 1895, coined the word **Sardoodledom** to mock Sardou's ingenious, empty contraptions: plots that run like clockwork and say nothing about life.

In a chapter added to *The Quintessence of Ibsenism* in 1913, Shaw put his case in structural terms. The old play, he argued, ran *exposition, situation, unravelling*. Ibsen's new play ran *exposition, situation, **discussion***, and the discussion was the point. Shaw built his own plays around argument rather than intrigue.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Most of the form's devices still work, and some modern hits are well-made plays in all but name. Use the secret and the obligatory scene, but make sure the exposure means something beyond the gasp. Ibsen's lesson: the machine is a means, not the goal.
- **Screen:** The thriller and the courtroom drama are its direct heirs. The obligatory scene is the showdown you've been promising; withholding it reads as a cheat.
- **Journalism:** An investigative narrative often has a well-made shape: a hidden fact, partial revelations, a confrontation (the official finally asked on the record). Be careful not to manufacture suspense the facts don't support.

## The pushback

- **Ingenuity over truth.** Shaw's complaint still stands. Plots driven by intercepted letters and coincidental arrivals feel mechanical, and characters exist to serve the mechanism.
- **"Well-made" became an insult.** For much of the twentieth century, critics used the phrase to dismiss tidy commercial plays, and the avant-garde defined itself against it.
- **It's a reconstruction.** Stanton's features describe a pattern across many plays; Scribe didn't publish them as rules. Treat them as a toolbox, not a law.
- **But the craft is real.** Many teachers argue the form was unfairly maligned. Its tools, from clear exposition to a promised confrontation, are exactly what keeps an audience in its seats.

## Try it

Read *A Doll's House*, or take your own draft, and lay out its machinery.

1. Name the secret. Who knows it, who doesn't, and how much does the audience know?
2. List the swings of fortune in order. Does each one bring the exposure closer?
3. Name the obligatory scene: the confrontation the story has promised. Is it written in scene, in front of the person who most needs to hear it, or does it happen offstage or in summary?
4. Then ask Ibsen's question. After the exposure, what does it mean? Write the first few lines of the discussion that could follow.

- **Stage:** add one object, like a letter or a key, planted early and used decisively later.
- **Screen:** for a thriller or courtroom script, check that the showdown is on screen and not cut away from.
- **Journalism:** name the confrontation in your investigation, such as the official finally asked on the record, and check that the suspense you build comes from the facts, not from withholding.

**Check:** Does your obligatory scene change something beyond revealing the secret?

## Check yourself

Q: What drives the suspense in a well-made play?
A: A secret that the audience knows, or half-knows, and that some characters don't, combined with swings of fortune that bring its exposure closer.

Q: What is the obligatory scene (scène à faire)?
A: The confrontation the play has led the audience to expect and want, usually where the secret comes out in front of the person most affected. Sarcey named it; Archer translated it.

Q: How did Ibsen change the form in *A Doll's House*?
A: He kept the machinery (secret, letter, blackmail, exposure), but after the crisis passes he replaces the tidy unravelling with a serious discussion between husband and wife, and an ending that settles nothing comfortably.

Q: What did Shaw mean by "Sardoodledom"?
A: Clever, mechanical plotting with nothing to say about life, the kind he saw in Sardou's plays.

Q: Did Scribe write down the rules of the well-made play?
A: No. They were reconstructed by later critics, such as Stephen Stanton, from the pattern of his plays.
