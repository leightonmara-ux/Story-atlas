## Who and when

Ingmar Bergman (1918–2007) was a Swedish director who worked in theatre all his life and in film for about forty years. He ran the city theatre in Helsingborg in his twenties, directed at Gothenburg and then Malmö, and headed the Royal Dramatic Theatre in Stockholm from 1963 to 1966. He staged Strindberg, Ibsen and Shakespeare again and again, and once compared the theatre to a faithful wife and film to a demanding, costly mistress.

His films include *The Seventh Seal* and *Wild Strawberries* (both 1957), *Persona* (1966), *Cries and Whispers* (1972), the television series *Scenes from a Marriage* (1973), *Autumn Sonata* (1978) and *Fanny and Alexander* (1982). He wrote almost all of them himself. For his own account of how he worked, read three sources: his introduction to *Four Screenplays of Ingmar Bergman* (1960), his memoir *The Magic Lantern* (1987) and *Images: My Life in Film* (1990), where he goes back through his films one by one, sometimes correcting what he'd said about them in earlier interviews.

## The big ideas

### Start from an image, not a plot

Bergman rarely began with a story. He described films starting as a picture, a mood or a fragment of dream that kept coming back until he had to find out what it meant.

*Cries and Whispers* is his best-documented case. In *Images* he traces it to a picture that wouldn't leave him: a room all in red, with women dressed in white whispering to one another. The finished film is four women in a manor house, one of them dying. Its rooms are red, and its scenes end not in fades to black but in fades to red.

*Wild Strawberries* opens with a dream. The old professor Isak Borg (Victor Sjöström) walks through empty streets in bright light. A clock has no hands. A hearse lurches, a wheel comes off, the coffin falls into the road and the corpse inside is Isak, who reaches up and grabs his own hand. Only then does the plot begin, a car journey to collect an honorary degree. The dream has already stated the film's subject: an old man facing death and the coldness of his life.

For writers, the lesson is a way of generating material. If you have an image that keeps returning, don't force it into a premise yet. Ask who is in the room, why they're whispering, and what has to happen for that picture to occur.

### Film is closer to music than to literature

In his introduction to *Four Screenplays* (1960), Bergman argued that film has little to do with literature, and that the screenplay is a very imperfect technical basis for a film, because so much of what matters can't be written down. A film, he said, works on feeling directly, not through the intellect, and the art it most resembles is music:

> Film is mainly rhythm; it is inhalation and exhalation in continuous sequence.
— Ingmar Bergman, introduction to *Four Screenplays of Ingmar Bergman* (1960)

That's practical advice: think about a scene's tempo, its loud and quiet passages, where it breathes out. *Cries and Whispers* has a moment that makes the point: two estranged sisters, Karin and Maria, finally talk tenderly, touching each other's faces, but we don't hear the words. Bergman lays a Bach sarabande for solo cello over the scene, and their faces carry it. (Later we learn the reconciliation doesn't last.) See *Music and silence* for how scoring and its absence shape feeling.

### The human face

Bergman said many times, in interviews and writings, that cinema's great originality is its ability to come close to the human face. Theatre can't do that; a play is always seen from the stalls. His films lean in.

- ***Winter Light* (1963).** Märta (Ingrid Thulin) has written the pastor a letter. As he reads it, she appears speaking it straight to the camera, in one long, nearly unbroken close-up lasting several minutes. Nothing moves but her face.
- ***Persona* (1966).** An actress, Elisabet (Liv Ullmann), has stopped speaking; a nurse, Alma (Bibi Andersson), cares for her in a summer house and talks to fill the silence. Late on, Alma delivers a long speech about Elisabet's hidden feelings toward her son. Bergman plays it twice: first on Elisabet's face as she listens, then again, word for word, on Alma's as she speaks. It ends with half of one face joined to half of the other in a single image.
- ***Autumn Sonata* (1978).** A concert pianist, Charlotte (Ingrid Bergman), visits the daughter she neglected, Eva (Liv Ullmann). Eva plays Chopin's Prelude in A minor for her, and the camera holds on Charlotte listening, her face showing what she won't say. Then Charlotte plays it herself, explaining how it should go. The night-long confrontation that follows is largely two faces in close-up.

These films work as *The face and the cut* describes: the meaning of a face depends on what surrounds it. But Bergman added patience. He'd hold on a face long after another director would cut, until the performance stopped being "acting" and became exposure. *The camera's eye* covers how shot size sets that intimacy.

### The chamber film

In 1907 August Strindberg wrote a group of short, intense **chamber plays**, including *The Ghost Sonata*, for his small Intimate Theatre in Stockholm, borrowing the idea of chamber music: few players, close listening, no spectacle. Bergman directed *The Ghost Sonata* several times across his career.

His films of the early 1960s apply the same discipline, and he likened them to chamber music himself:

- ***Through a Glass Darkly* (1961):** four people (a young woman slipping into mental illness, her father, husband and younger brother), one island, about a day.
- ***Winter Light* (1963):** a doubting pastor across one Sunday, from a morning service to an evening one with almost nobody in the pews.
- ***The Silence* (1963):** two sisters and a small boy in a hotel in a foreign city whose language they can't speak.

The three are often called a trilogy. Bergman used the label himself early on, then later disowned it as something applied after the fact. Either way, they show the form clearly.

A chamber piece has nowhere to hide. With four characters, each must pull against the others; *The cast as a system* explains why. With one location, pressure builds instead of leaking into new settings. With little plot, the drama is in small shifts between people. Playwrights know this ground (see *Stage forms*); screenwriters can learn that a small cast in one house can grip like a chase.

### Rules from a working director

In the essay usually published in English as "Each Film Is My Last" (translated in 1966), Bergman set out three "commandments" for himself, here paraphrased. First, always be entertaining: he insisted this was a moral duty, because audiences give you their time and money. Second, obey your artistic conscience. Third, make each film as if it were your last. Remember the first whenever "serious" starts meaning "boring".

### Writing between stage and screen

Bergman's screenplays read more like short novels than industry scripts, with moods and inner states described directly in prose. He published them as books, and late in life wrote some for others to direct, such as *The Best Intentions* (filmed by Bille August, 1992). Material moved between media, too: *Scenes from a Marriage*, made for television, became a much-staged play.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Bergman is a model of the chamber play's strengths: a handful of characters, one location, small shifts carrying the drama. Give your audience a "close-up" by stilling everything else on stage while one face listens.
- **Screen:** Write the face. Give the listener, not just the speaker, a line of action at the key moment, and consider letting one long speech play on a single face. Think in rhythm: mark where each sequence speeds up, slows down, or falls silent.
- **Journalism:** The image-first method suits features. Start your notes from the picture you can't shake from your reporting, and ask what it means before deciding your angle. In video and audio, letting a subject's silence run is the documentary version of Bergman's held close-up, provided you're not exploiting a vulnerable person.

## The pushback

- **Solemnity.** Bergman became a byword for Nordic gloom: God's silence, death, cruelty within marriage. Parodies followed, from the short *De Düva* (1968) to Death being challenged to board games in *Bill & Ted's Bogus Journey* (1991). Woody Allen, a lifelong admirer, borrowed his imagery for comedy in *Love and Death* (1975). The charge overlooks his comedies, though, above all *Smiles of a Summer Night* (1955), which became Stephen Sondheim's musical *A Little Night Music* (1973).
- **Self-absorption.** Critics have called the films private myths about one man's father, faith and guilt, and his memoirs freely link them to his life.
- **Treatment of others.** Bergman was famously demanding and could be harsh. In 1969 he struck a newspaper critic at a Royal Dramatic Theatre rehearsal and was fined, and Jane Magnusson's documentary *Bergman: A Year in a Life* (2018) gathers accounts of his temper and cruelty. Against that, actors including Liv Ullmann, Bibi Andersson and Max von Sydow worked with him for decades.

## Try it

Take a scene from your work-in-progress where something important is said.

1. Cut it down to two or three characters and one place. Remove anyone who isn't pulling against someone else.
2. Find the line that lands hardest. Rewrite the moment from the listener's side: what does their face do, and how long do we stay on it before anyone speaks again?
3. Mark the scene's rhythm in the margin, as *fast*, *slow* or *silent*, beat by beat. If it's all one tempo, change one passage.

- **Stage:** stage the key line so the audience watches the listener, with everything else on stage still.
- **Screen:** write one speech to be held on a single face, then write the same speech held on the listener, and compare.
- **Journalism:** in a profile, find the moment your subject went quiet, and write it so the reader stays in that silence for a sentence or two.

**Check:** If the dialogue were muted, would the faces and the rhythm still tell us what the scene is about?

## Check yourself

Q: Where did Bergman say *Cries and Whispers* began?
A: With a recurring image of a red room and women in white whispering, which he described in *Images*; the plot grew out of the picture.

Q: Why did Bergman compare film to music rather than literature?
A: Because, as he wrote in *Four Screenplays*, film acts on feeling directly and is mainly rhythm, so the screenplay can only be an imperfect basis for it.

Q: What is a chamber film, and what does it borrow from Strindberg?
A: A film with a small cast, few locations and little plot, where drama comes from close pressure between people, like Strindberg's 1907 chamber plays and chamber music.

Q: How does *Persona* use faces in Alma's long speech about Elisabet's son?
A: It plays the speech twice, first on Elisabet listening and then on Alma speaking, and ends by merging the two faces into one image.

Q: What was surprising about Bergman's first "commandment"?
A: That a director known for gloom insisted on always being entertaining, as a duty to the audience.
