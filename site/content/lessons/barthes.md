## Who and when

Roland Barthes (1915–1980) was a French critic and teacher, and one of the central figures of structuralism and then of what came after it. He wrote about wrestling, advertising, photography, fashion and love as well as literature, and he had a gift for turning a dry theory into a sentence people remembered.

Three works matter here:

- "**Introduction to the Structural Analysis of Narratives**" (1966), published in the French journal *Communications*, an attempt to describe the grammar shared by all stories.
- "**The Death of the Author**" (1967), a short polemic first published in English in the American magazine *Aspen*, then in French in 1968.
- ***S/Z*** (1970), a book-length, phrase-by-phrase reading of Balzac's novella "Sarrasine" (1830).

The movement between them is part of the story. The 1966 essay looks for a universal structure; *S/Z* gives up on that and follows one text closely, showing the many codes running through it at once.

## The big ideas

### Nuclei and catalyzers (1966)

In the structural analysis essay, Barthes sorts the units of a narrative by what they do. Some actions are **cardinal functions**, or **nuclei**: real hinges, where the story could go one way or another. Others are **catalyzers**: the smaller actions that fill the space between hinges. A phone rings (nucleus); the character crosses the room, puts down a cup, lights a lamp (catalyzers); she answers or doesn't (nucleus).

Remove a nucleus and the story changes. Remove a catalyzer and it survives, though its rhythm and texture change. The American critic Seymour Chatman later adapted this as **kernels and satellites** in *Story and Discourse* (1978); see *Story vs. telling*. If you meet "kernels and satellites" in a craft book, the idea comes from Barthes, with Chatman's names.

### The five codes (S/Z, 1970)

In *S/Z* Barthes cuts "Sarrasine" into 561 short units he calls **lexias** and shows that every stretch of a story is woven from five codes:

- **Hermeneutic code:** the enigmas. Questions raised, kept open, and finally answered.
- **Proairetic code:** sequences of actions. We recognize "a stroll", "a seduction", "a murder" because we've read thousands of them, so the first step tells us there will be more.
- **Semic code:** the connotations that cluster around a character or place and build up into a personality.
- **Symbolic code:** the large oppositions that organize meaning, such as inside and outside, warm and cold, male and female.
- **Cultural (referential) code:** appeals to shared knowledge, the "as everyone knows" of a period.

Only the first two run in time and drive the reader forward. The other three are more like a web of meaning laid over the whole.

### The hermeneutic code: a question held open

This is Barthes's most useful idea for working writers, and the theory behind the fourth element in *The shared core*, *a question held open*. "Sarrasine" opens at a lavish Paris party, where a strange, ancient man moves among the guests. Who is he? Where does the family's fortune come from? The story makes readers wait for the answers.

Barthes shows that a narrative doesn't just ask and answer. It works hard to **delay** the answer, with a whole set of devices: snares (deliberate false leads), equivocations (mixtures of truth and evasion), partial answers, suspended answers and jammings (admissions that it can't be answered yet). The truth, when it comes, closes the story. The middle is all delay.

### Readerly and writerly

*S/Z* also introduces a pair of terms. A **readerly** text (*lisible*), like most classic realist fiction, gives the reader a finished meaning to consume. A **writerly** text (*scriptible*) makes the reader a producer of meaning, with no single way through. Barthes clearly prefers the second, though *S/Z* itself shows how plural even a "readerly" story can be.

### The death of the author

The 1967 essay argues against explaining a text by its author's life and intentions. A text, Barthes says, isn't a message from one god-like mind but a fabric of language and quotation drawn from the whole culture. Its unity lies not in its origin but in its destination: the reader.

> The birth of the reader must be at the cost of the death of the Author.
> — Roland Barthes, "The Death of the Author" (trans. Stephen Heath)

It's a polemic, not a literal claim that authors don't matter. It belongs to the same moment as Michel Foucault's lecture "What Is an Author?" (1969), and the two are often confused.

## Use it

- **Screen:** List every open question in your script, scene by scene, and mark where each is raised, delayed and answered. Thrillers and mysteries live on the hermeneutic code; a script where questions get answered the moment they're raised has no pull. The proairetic code reminds you that audiences recognize sequences from the first step, so you can play with their expectations.
- **Stage:** The nucleus/catalyzer split is a cutting tool. In a long scene, find the hinges. Then ask whether each catalyzer earns its time by building texture, character (the semic code) or tension.
- **Journalism:** A narrative feature runs on the hermeneutic code too. The nut graf can promise an answer; the body delays it with scenes, partial answers and complications. The ethical limit: you can delay an answer, but you can't plant a snare that misleads readers about facts.

## The pushback

- **Jargon.** Barthes's terms are elegant but overlapping, and critics have found the five codes hard to apply consistently. Several codes could claim the same sentence.
- **The author isn't dead.** Many readers and writers find intention and biography meaningful, and "death of the author" is often used loosely to mean "anything goes", which goes beyond Barthes's essay.
- **Elitism.** The preference for the writerly can sound like a dismissal of popular storytelling, even though Barthes himself delighted in mass culture.
- **Not a craft manual.** These are tools for reading. Writers have to translate them into practice themselves.

## Try it

Take the first ten pages of a script or the first 800 words of a draft. Highlight in one color every question the reader would be asking, and in another every place a question is answered. Then list the questions down the margin and, for each one, note where it's raised, how it's delayed (false lead, partial answer, postponement), and where it's closed.

- **Stage:** Find a question that's answered in the same scene it's raised. Can you hold it until the next act?
- **Screen:** Mark your nuclei. Is there a catalyzer between every two hinges, or do the hinges pile up with no room to breathe?
- **Journalism:** Find the enigma your lede raises. Make sure the piece answers it, and that no delay misleads readers about the facts.

**Check:** At every point in the draft, is there at least one question the reader wants answered?

## Check yourself

Q: What is the difference between a nucleus and a catalyzer?
A: A nucleus (cardinal function) is a hinge where the story could go different ways; remove it and the story changes. A catalyzer fills the space between hinges; remove it and the story survives with a different texture.

Q: What does the hermeneutic code do?
A: It raises enigmas, delays their answers with snares, partial answers and postponements, and finally discloses the truth.

Q: Which of the five codes drive the reader forward in time?
A: The hermeneutic code (questions) and the proairetic code (sequences of actions).

Q: Where do "kernels and satellites" come from?
A: Seymour Chatman's *Story and Discourse* (1978), adapting Barthes's nuclei and catalyzers from his 1966 essay.

Q: What is Barthes's main claim in "The Death of the Author"?
A: That a text shouldn't be explained by its author's intentions or life; its meaning is made in the reader.
