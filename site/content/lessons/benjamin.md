## Who and when

Walter Benjamin (1892–1940) was a German-Jewish critic and philosopher, a writer of essays rather than systems. He wrote about Baudelaire, Kafka, Proust, translation, cities, toys and the shopping arcades of Paris. He fled the Nazis in 1933 and died in 1940 at Portbou on the Spanish border, while trying to escape occupied France.

"**The Storyteller**" (*Der Erzähler*) was published in 1936. It is nominally an essay on the Russian writer **Nikolai Leskov** (1831–1895), whose tales draw on oral and folk traditions, but Leskov is mostly the occasion. Benjamin's real subject is a kind of telling he thought was dying out. Most English readers know it from Harry Zohn's translation in *Illuminations* (1968), the collection edited by Hannah Arendt.

It was written in the same years as his better-known "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction", and the two essays share a question: what happens to old forms of art and experience under modern conditions?

## The big ideas

### Storytelling is coming to an end

Benjamin opens with a striking claim: the art of storytelling is disappearing, and with it the ability to exchange experiences. His emblem is the soldiers of the First World War, who came home silent. They had lived through more than almost any generation before them, yet had less they could pass on. Experience, he says, has fallen in value.

### Two kinds of storyteller

He sketches two old types. One is the person who stays home, the farmer who knows the local tales and traditions. The other is the traveller, the trading seaman who comes back with news of far places. The medieval craft workshop brought them together: the settled master and the wandering journeymen. Storytelling, for Benjamin, grows out of that world of handwork, where people sit together at a slow task and a tale can be told and retold.

### Information versus story

This is the idea journalists keep coming back to. **Information**, the stuff of the modern newspaper, has to be checkable, has to make sense on its own and comes already explained. Its value lasts only as long as it's new. Yesterday's news is worth nothing.

A **story** works differently. It doesn't use itself up. It keeps its force and can release it long after it was first told. Benjamin's example comes from the Greek historian Herodotus. The Egyptian king Psammenitus, defeated and captured by the Persian king Cambyses, watches without a sign of emotion as his daughter is paraded as a servant and his son is led to execution. But when he sees an old servant of his among the prisoners, he breaks down and weeps. Herodotus doesn't say why. That's exactly why, Benjamin argues, the story still makes readers think thousands of years later.

> It is half the art of storytelling to keep a story free from explanation as one reproduces it.
> — Walter Benjamin, "The Storyteller" (trans. Harry Zohn)

The storyteller reports the extraordinary and the strange, but leaves the reader free to interpret it. Information explains everything and so leaves nothing behind.

### Experience passed on

The storyteller takes what he tells from experience, his own or other people's, and makes it the experience of those who listen. A good story is easy to remember and to retell, and it carries **counsel**: not an answer to a problem, but a suggestion about how a story might go on. Like a potter's handprints on a pot, the teller's traces stay on the tale.

### The novel and solitude

Benjamin contrasts the story with the **novel**, which he sees as born of the solitary individual: a writer alone, writing for a reader alone, about a person who can no longer give or receive counsel. The novel reader, he suggests, reads to find the meaning of a life, and only a finished life, closed by death or by the end of the book, can show its meaning. (Compare *Frank Kermode & Peter Brooks* on why endings matter.)

### Authority from death

In older storytelling, Benjamin writes, death was a public part of life, and the dying passed on what they had lived. The storyteller borrows his authority from death: a life seen whole becomes transmissible. Modern society has hidden death away, and with it some of that authority.

## Use it

- **Journalism:** Benjamin's distinction is a direct challenge to the newsroom. Most daily copy is information: verified, explained, disposable. Narrative journalism at its best is closer to story: it reports something strange and true, and resists tying it off with an explanatory paragraph. The test is whether readers would still retell your piece a year from now. You still have to verify everything; Benjamin's point is about how much you explain, not whether you check.
- **Stage:** Theatre is one of the few places where people still gather to hear a story told in a shared room. Benjamin's ideal of the unexplained tale suits plays that let a central act stay mysterious, as Herodotus does with the weeping king.
- **Screen:** Voice-over and exposition often do what Benjamin calls explanation. Try cutting the line that tells us why a character did something and see if the scene gets stronger.

## The pushback

- **Nostalgia.** The essay can read as a lament for a lost, idealized world of craftsmen and villages. Benjamin himself complicates this: he says the decline of storytelling isn't simply decay, and that it lets us see a new beauty in what is vanishing.
- **Too sharp a split.** Critics point out that oral storytelling never disappeared, and that novels and good journalism can carry experience as well as any folk tale.
- **The novel's defenders.** Many novelists would reject the idea that the novel is a form of isolation; reading can be its own kind of communal experience.
- **A theory of decline, not a method.** Benjamin gives you a standard and a warning, not a structure.

## Try it

Pick a true story you know well, something you witnessed or reported. Tell it aloud to someone in under three minutes with no explanation: no "because", no "I think this shows", no moral. Then write it down the same way, in 300 words. Finally, write a second version that explains everything, and compare.

- **Stage:** Turn the unexplained version into a monologue. Let the strangest detail sit without comment.
- **Screen:** Find the line of dialogue or voice-over in your script that explains a character's motive. Cut it and replace it with an action.
- **Journalism:** Take a feature draft and find the paragraph that tells readers what it all means. Try ending one paragraph earlier, on a fact.

**Check:** Would a listener want to retell your unexplained version to someone else tonight?

## Check yourself

Q: What is the difference between information and story, for Benjamin?
A: Information must be verifiable and explained, and loses its value once it's no longer new. A story isn't used up; it keeps its force and can provoke thought long after it's told.

Q: Why does he admire Herodotus's story of Psammenitus?
A: Because Herodotus doesn't explain why the king weeps for an old servant after showing no emotion for his own children. The unexplained story keeps readers thinking for millennia.

Q: Where does the storyteller's material come from?
A: Experience, his own or other people's, which the telling then makes the experience of the listeners.

Q: How does Benjamin contrast the novel with the story?
A: The novel is born of the solitary individual, writing and read alone, who can no longer give or receive counsel. The story is told in company and passed on.

Q: What does the essay suggest for narrative journalists?
A: Verify everything, but resist over-explaining. Report the strange and true, and leave readers room to interpret.
