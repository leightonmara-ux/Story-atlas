## Who and when

Bill Nichols is an American film scholar, long at San Francisco State University, and a leading figure in documentary studies since the field took shape in universities. His first major book on the subject, *Representing Reality* (1991), proposed a set of "modes" of documentary. He expanded and renamed them over the following decade, and in ***Introduction to Documentary*** (2001; later editions 2010 and 2017) he settled on six. That book became the standard textbook for film students.

Nichols writes about documentary as a kind of argument about the world: not a window onto reality but a voice, made from real sounds and images, that proposes a way of seeing.

The word "documentary" itself is usually credited to the Scottish filmmaker and producer **John Grierson**, who used it in a 1926 review of Robert Flaherty's *Moana*. Grierson is also credited with defining documentary as "the creative treatment of actuality". The phrase is always attributed to him and dated to his writing of the early 1930s, but its exact first appearance is hard to pin down, so treat it as his summary of the idea rather than a quotation with a page number.

## The big ideas

### Creative treatment of actuality

Grierson's phrase holds the central tension of the form. *Actuality*: the material is real, filmed, not invented. *Creative treatment*: it's selected, framed, ordered and cut, and those choices make an argument. Flaherty's *Nanook of the North* (1922), often called the first feature documentary, staged scenes and reconstructed older ways of life for the camera. From the start, documentary was shaped.

### Documentary as voice

Nichols's key move is to stop asking "is it objective?" and ask "what is its **voice**?" Every documentary takes a perspective through its choices: what to film, whom to interview, what music to use, what order to put things in. Even a film with no narration has a voice, carried by its editing.

### Six modes

Nichols sorts documentaries by how they make that voice heard. The modes aren't stages in a history or a ranking, and most films mix several. But each names a distinct set of choices.

| Mode | What it does | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Poetic | Arranges images by rhythm, association and mood, not argument | Joris Ivens, *Rain* (1929) |
| Expository | Addresses the viewer directly, often with narration, to make a case | Frank Capra, *Why We Fight* (1942–45) |
| Observational | Watches events unfold without narration or interviews | Frederick Wiseman, *Titicut Follies* (1967) |
| Participatory | The filmmaker interacts with subjects on camera, through interviews or presence | Jean Rouch and Edgar Morin, *Chronicle of a Summer* (1961) |
| Reflexive | Draws attention to how documentaries are made and how we read them | Dziga Vertov, *Man with a Movie Camera* (1929) |
| Performative | Stresses subjective, emotional experience, often the filmmaker's own | Marlon Riggs, *Tongues Untied* (1989) |

Nichols's earlier term for participatory was "interactive", and poetic was the last mode he added. Notice that the reflexive example is from 1929, older than many observational films: the modes overlap in time.

### What each mode promises

The useful thing about the modes for a working filmmaker is that each makes a different **promise** to the audience. Expository promises a clear account and asks you to trust the narrator. Observational promises that you're seeing life as it happened, with the camera a fly on the wall, which makes any staging a betrayal. Participatory admits the filmmaker's presence and lets the encounter itself become the story. Reflexive makes the viewer question the film. Performative asks you to feel what it was like rather than to know the facts.

Mixing modes is normal, but switching promises midway without telling the audience is where trust breaks.

### Structure in the edit

Fiction films are written before they're shot. Documentaries are usually found afterward. A typical documentary shoots many hours of footage for every minute used, and the story is built in the cutting room: finding the protagonist, the question, the turning points, the ending.

This is where the tools from the rest of this course come in. Editors look for the same things a screenwriter would: someone who wants something, obstacles, a change. They build sequences, decide when to reveal information, and choose where to start and stop. *The shared core* applies; the difference is that you can only use what really happened, and you can't reshoot life.

### Ethics shared with journalism

Nichols devotes real attention to ethics, and the questions overlap closely with journalism's (see *Janet Malcolm & John Hersey*). Has the subject given informed consent, and do they understand how they'll appear? Does a reconstruction or reenactment clearly declare itself? Does the edit change what someone meant, or the order of events in a way that misleads?

Two famous cases show the stakes. Errol Morris's *The Thin Blue Line* (1988) used stylized reenactments, openly and reflexively, and helped free Randall Adams, a man wrongly convicted of murder. Michael Moore's *Roger & Me* (1989) was criticized by the critic Harlan Jacobson and others for rearranging the chronology of events to sharpen its argument. Both reshaped reality; only one hid it.

## Use it

- **Screen:** Before you shoot, decide which mode or modes you're working in and what that promises the audience. In the edit, write a paper edit, a transcript-based outline of the story, before cutting, and ask where your protagonist, question and turns are.
- **Stage:** Verbatim and documentary theatre face the same choices: whether to present testimony "straight" (observational), to put the playwright onstage interviewing (participatory), or to expose the making of the play (reflexive). Each makes a different contract with the audience.
- **Journalism:** The modes map neatly onto audio and video journalism: the explanatory narrated piece is expository, the fly-on-the-wall feature is observational, the reporter-led podcast is participatory. Audio documentary in the style of *This American Life* is largely participatory and performative.

## The pushback

- **Categories leak.** Critics point out that nearly every film is a hybrid, so the modes can become a filing exercise. Nichols agrees that films mix modes; the categories are lenses, not boxes.
- **A historical story after all.** Although Nichols warns against seeing the modes as a progression, his books have often presented them roughly in the order they emerged, and readers have taken them as an evolution. They aren't one.
- **Too film-centered.** The modes were built around cinema and some critics find them less useful for interactive, web and virtual-reality documentary, or for documentary podcasts.
- **"Creative treatment" can excuse too much.** Grierson's phrase is sometimes used to justify staging and manipulation. The ethical line, what you can rearrange and what you must not, still has to be drawn case by case.

## Check yourself

Q: What does "the creative treatment of actuality" capture about documentary?
A: The material is real, but it's selected, ordered and shaped, and those choices make an argument. The phrase is attributed to John Grierson, from the early 1930s.

Q: Name Nichols's six modes.
A: Poetic, expository, observational, participatory, reflexive and performative.

Q: What's the difference between observational and participatory documentary?
A: Observational watches events unfold with the filmmaker unobtrusive. Participatory puts the filmmaker's interaction with subjects, such as interviews and presence, on screen.

Q: Are the modes a historical sequence, from primitive to advanced?
A: No. They overlap in time, most films mix them, and Nichols warns against reading them as an evolution.

Q: Why did *The Thin Blue Line*'s reenactments raise fewer ethical objections than *Roger & Me*'s chronology?
A: Morris's reenactments were openly stylized and declared themselves. Moore was accused of rearranging events without telling the audience.
