## Who and when

Jane Alison is a novelist, memoirist and translator of Ovid who teaches creative writing at the University of Virginia. ***Meander, Spiral, Explode: Design and Pattern in Narrative*** (2019) is her craft book, and it starts from a frustration many writers share: why should every story have to rise to a peak and fall?

Her answer is to look at nature. Water ripples and meanders, shells spiral, cells divide, branches network, things burst. These are patterns that organize energy and matter without a single climax. Alison asks what fiction would look like if it borrowed *those* shapes.

The book is short, playful and full of close readings, ranging across writers from Nabokov and Robbe-Grillet to Raymond Carver and Susan Minot. It's become a favorite in MFA programs looking for alternatives to the arc.

## The big ideas

### The arc is one shape among many

Alison doesn't say the dramatic arc is wrong. She says it's **one pattern**, treated as if it were the only one. The rise, the climax, the fall: she notes, with some mischief, that it has a suspiciously particular shape, a swelling and a release, and asks why every narrative should be modelled on it.

Many of the books we love most, she points out, don't move that way. They pull us along by other means: by repetition and variation, by circling back, by patterns we recognize and want to see completed.

### A vocabulary of patterns

The heart of the book is a set of shapes, each borrowed from nature:

- **Wavelets:** instead of one big wave, many small rises and falls. The energy is in the ripple of scene after scene, each with its own small tension and release.
- **Meander:** a narrative that wanders like a river, looping and digressing, but always moving downstream. The pleasure is in the turns.
- **Spiral:** a story that keeps returning to the same center, a memory, an event, an image, but each time at a different level, so that the return brings deeper understanding.
- **Radial:** pieces arranged around a central point, like spokes around a hub. Each section shows the center from a different side.
- **Network and cells:** narratives built from modules, chapters or fragments that link to each other in many directions, or sit side by side as discrete units that add up to a larger organism.
- **Explosion:** a story that bursts from a single moment and scatters its fragments, leaving the reader to reconstruct the event from what flies outward.

These aren't rigid categories. A single novel can meander at the level of the sentence and spiral at the level of the whole book.

### Design, not just plot

Alison's key move is to shift attention from **plot** to **design**: the visible, felt shape of a text. Repetition, motif, juxtaposition, the length and arrangement of sections, even white space on the page: all of these create patterns that move readers. Plot tension is one source of momentum. Pattern is another.

A useful exercise in her spirit: draw your draft. Not a plot graph, but a picture of how its parts relate. Does it circle one moment? Branch from a single event? Ripple along in small units? The drawing often shows you a shape you didn't know you were writing, and which parts fight it.

She draws on the idea that readers are pattern-seekers. Show us a shape and we'll want to follow it, anticipate it, and feel the click when it completes or breaks. That pull can be as strong as "what happens next?"

### Close reading as craft

Much of the book is Alison taking a passage and showing how it's built, how sentences, images and sections form a pattern that carries the reader. The lesson for writers isn't "choose a shape and fill it in". It's "notice the shapes your material already wants to make, and design for them."

## Use it

- **Stage:** Plays with episodic, circular or fragmentary structures, from Pinter's *Betrayal* (told largely backwards) to plays built of short, interlocking scenes, can be read through Alison's patterns. A spiral play returns to the same moment, a radial one circles one event from several characters' perspectives.
- **Screen:** Anthology films and series, nonlinear features and mosaic narratives are networks and radials. Wavelets describe much long-form television, where episodes ripple rather than build to a single peak.
- **Journalism:** Long-form features often spiral, returning to a central scene with new information each time, or work radially, circling one event through several people's accounts. Alison gives you names for structures many feature writers already use.

## The pushback

- **Metaphors, not mechanics.** The natural images are evocative, but critics note they don't always tell you *how* to build such a story, or how to sustain a long work without plot tension.
- **Readers still need a reason to turn the page.** Pattern can carry a short story or a lyrical novel. Over three hundred pages, or a two-hour film, most writers find that some form of tension, desire or question is still needed. Alison's patterns work best *alongside* it.
- **The arc caricature.** Defenders of conventional structure point out that good arcs already contain wavelets, motifs and returns. The choice may be less either/or than the book's framing suggests.
- **A literary bias.** Her examples lean toward experimental and literary fiction; genre writers may find less to use directly.

## Read next

- Jane Alison, *Meander, Spiral, Explode: Design and Pattern in Narrative* (2019).
- Ursula K. Le Guin, "The Carrier Bag Theory of Fiction" (1986), the essay many readers pair with Alison (see *Ursula K. Le Guin*).
- Kurt Vonnegut's lecture on the shapes of stories, for a playful counterpoint: he graphs stories as curves, while Alison asks for shapes that aren't curves at all.

## Try it

Take a story or script you're drafting and try it in a shape other than the arc.

1. Draw the draft as it stands: not a plot graph, but a picture of how its parts relate.
2. Choose one of Alison's patterns: a spiral (return to the same moment three times, each at a deeper level), a radial (show one event from three different sides), or wavelets (several small rises and falls instead of one peak).
3. Write a one-page sketch of the piece in that shape, using material you already have.
4. Draw the new version and compare. Which parts fit the pattern, and which fight it?

- **Stage:** try a spiral play that keeps returning to the same moment, or a radial one that circles an event through three characters.
- **Screen:** outline an episode as wavelets, with several small tensions and releases.
- **Journalism:** restructure a feature to spiral back to its central scene, with new information each time.

**Check:** Does the pattern itself pull the reader on, making them want to see it complete or break?

## Check yourself

Q: What is Alison's basic complaint about the dramatic arc?
A: Not that it's wrong, but that it's one pattern among many, treated as if it were the only possible shape for a story.

Q: Where does she look for alternatives?
A: To patterns in nature: wavelets, meanders, spirals, radial forms, networks and cells, explosions.

Q: What's the difference between a spiral and a radial structure?
A: A spiral returns again and again to the same center, deepening each time. A radial structure arranges pieces around a center, each showing it from a different side.

Q: What does she mean by shifting from plot to design?
A: Paying attention to the felt shape of a text, its repetition, motifs, juxtapositions and arrangement of parts, as a source of momentum alongside or instead of plot tension.

Q: What is a common criticism of the book?
A: Its natural images are suggestive but don't always explain how to build or sustain a long work without some kind of tension.
