## The short answer

The deep rules are shared. Every form needs desire and resistance, cause and effect, escalation, an open question and change (see *The shared core*). What differs is the **medium**, and above all two things:

1. **Who controls the audience's attention.**
2. **Whether you invent the events or have to find them.**

Nearly every difference in the theories follows from those two.

| | Screen | Stage | Journalism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who controls attention | The camera and the editor choose what you see | The audience chooses where to look in one shared space | The reader, who can leave at any moment |
| Main tool | Images and action | Words and live bodies; dialogue *is* action | Facts, and how they are chosen and ordered |
| Time | Cut freely: years in a second | Mostly continuous within a scene | Whatever order serves the reader |
| How strict the form is | Very strict: page counts, act breaks on set pages, beat sheets | Looser, more open to experiment | Strict up top, freer in long features |
| Where structure comes from | You invent it | You invent it | You **find** it in what you reported |

## Screen: the camera decides

A screenplay is a blueprint for pictures. Convention says one page is roughly one minute of screen time, which is why screenwriting gurus can speak of the inciting incident "around page 12" and the end of Act One "around page 25". No other form is measured so precisely, partly because film is expensive and the industry reads hundreds of scripts looking for a recognizable shape.

What this means for structure:

- **Show it.** If a character's change can't be seen or heard, it barely exists on screen. Inner life has to be turned into behavior.
- **Cut ruthlessly.** You can leave a scene late and jump years in a cut, so scenes start as close to their turn as possible.
- **Momentum matters.** Film moves at a fixed speed, and the audience can't slow down or re-read. Clarity of want and obstacle carries a lot of weight.

## Stage: the audience decides

In a theater everything happens in one room, in front of people who are watching everyone at once. A film tells you where to look; a play can't.

What this means for structure:

- **Words carry the action.** Characters fight, seduce and deceive through talk. That's why Stanislavski taught actors to play an *objective* in every line: on stage, speech is something you *do* to someone.
- **Time and place are scarce.** Each location change costs a set, each time jump costs a convention. So plays often compress: one room, one night, one family. Pressure builds because no one can leave.
- **Theatricality is allowed.** Characters can talk to the audience, time can loop, one actor can play five parts. Theater tolerates formal experiment (Brecht, Beckett, Caryl Churchill) far more readily than commercial film.
- **The audience is in the room.** Intermissions, laughter and silence are part of the structure. A first act usually needs a turn strong enough to bring people back after the interval.

## Journalism: the facts decide

Here the rules change most, because **you can't invent anything**. The events, the people and the ending are what they are. Structure becomes the art of *finding* the story in your reporting and *ordering* it. Journalism uses three main shapes:

- **The inverted pyramid** (hard news): the most important facts first, then the less important. A reader can stop anywhere and still have the key facts, and an editor can cut from the bottom. It barely resembles a dramatic arc.
- **The feature**: open with an anecdote or scene, then the *nut graf* (what this is about and why it matters), then the body, then a *kicker* to end on. The dramatic question is mostly "what's going on here?"
- **Narrative nonfiction** (Jon Franklin, Jack Hart): a full dramatic arc with a real character, complication and resolution. But you can only write it when reality has supplied one, so reporting comes before structure.

Two more differences:

- **The reader can leave at any time.** There's no darkened room and no ticket paid for. Journalism front-loads its promise, which is why the nut graf usually arrives within the first few paragraphs.
- **The change is often in understanding.** The person at the center may not change at all. The reader comes away seeing the world differently.

## Use it: one event, three forms

Take a single real situation, for example a family fighting an eviction from a rent-stabilized apartment, and sketch it three ways:

1. **Screen:** What's the image that tells us everything in the first minute? What happens around page 12 that forces the family to act?
2. **Stage:** Which room do we stay in? Who's trapped in it together, and what does each person want from the others, right now, in this scene?
3. **Journalism:** What's the news (the lede)? What's the nut graf, meaning why should a stranger care? And has the situation resolved, so a narrative is possible, or is it still open?

You'll find the same skeleton under all three, dressed differently each time.

## Check yourself

Q: What two differences between the media explain most of the differences in their theories?
A: Who controls the audience's attention (camera, audience or reader), and whether the events are invented or must be found.

Q: Why can screenwriting theory specify beats by page number while playwriting theory rarely does?
A: The one-page-per-minute convention and the film industry's standardized reading make the form measurable. Theater varies more in running time and tolerates more formal experiment.

Q: Why does a nut graf usually arrive within the first few paragraphs?
A: A reader can leave at any moment, so journalism has to state its promise, what the story is and why it matters, early.

Q: Why do so many plays take place in one room, over a short span of time?
A: Locations and time jumps are costly on stage, and confinement builds pressure because the characters can't escape each other.
