## Where it comes from

Every story exists twice. There are the **events**, in the order they happened. And there is the **telling**, in the order and at the speed and from the angle the teller chooses. The gap between the two is where a great deal of a writer's craft lives.

Three bodies of work gave us the vocabulary:

- The **Russian Formalists**, a group of critics in the 1910s and 1920s, chief among them **Viktor Shklovsky**, drew the basic distinction between *fabula* and *syuzhet*.
- The French critic **Gérard Genette**, in *Narrative Discourse* (1972, English 1980), took it apart with precision, using Proust's *In Search of Lost Time* as his test case.
- The American critic **Seymour Chatman**, in *Story and Discourse* (1978), put the two in plain English and applied them to film as well as fiction.

The terminology differs from one theorist to the next, but the core idea is simple and enormously useful, especially for journalists, who can't change the events but always choose the telling.

## The big ideas

### Fabula and syuzhet

The **fabula** is the raw sequence of events in chronological and causal order: what happened, and when. The **syuzhet** is the way those events are actually presented: what we're shown first, what's withheld, what's skipped.

Shklovsky was fascinated by books that made the gap obvious. His best-known example was Laurence Sterne's *Tristram Shandy*, a novel that digresses, doubles back and delays so much that it takes volumes for its narrator even to be born. Shklovsky called it the most typical novel in world literature, because it lays bare the device that every novel uses: the art lies not in the events but in how they are arranged.

This connects to his other famous idea, **defamiliarization** (*ostranenie*, "making strange"). Art, he argued, exists to make us see the familiar afresh, and the rearrangement of events is one of its main tools.

### Order

Genette's first category is **order**: the relation between the sequence of events and the sequence of their telling. Any departure from chronology he calls an **anachrony**, and there are two kinds:

- **Analepsis:** a flashback. The telling jumps back to something that happened earlier.
- **Prolepsis:** a flash-forward. The telling jumps ahead to something that hasn't happened yet.

Starting *in medias res*, in the middle of things, and then filling in the past is the oldest anachrony of all: Homer does it.

### Duration

**Duration** (sometimes called speed) is the relation between how long events take and how much text or screen time they get. Genette identifies four basic speeds:

- **Scene:** telling time roughly equals event time, as in dialogue.
- **Summary:** a long stretch of events told quickly. "For the next ten years she worked nights."
- **Ellipsis:** events skipped entirely. The cut, the white space.
- **Pause:** the events stop while the telling continues, as in a description.

A fifth, often added, is **stretch**: telling slower than the event itself, as in slow motion.

### Frequency

**Frequency** is how many times something happened versus how many times it is told:

- **Singulative:** told once, happened once. The default.
- **Repetitive:** told several times, happened once. *Rashomon* tells one crime from four perspectives.
- **Iterative:** told once, happened many times. "Every Sunday they walked to the river." Genette showed how much of Proust is written this way.

### Focalization

Genette also separated two questions that are easy to blur: **who speaks?** and **who sees?** The narrator is whoever tells the story. The **focalizer** is the character whose perception the telling follows at a given moment. A third-person narrator can still restrict us to what one character knows (*internal* focalization), roam freely (*zero* focalization), or show only external behavior, like a camera (*external* focalization).

### Story and discourse

Chatman's terms are the plainest. **Story** is the *what*: events, plus the characters and settings he calls existents. **Discourse** is the *how*: the means by which the story is communicated. Because the same story can be told in a novel, a film or a ballet, Chatman argued, story is a structure independent of any medium.

He also distinguished **kernels** from **satellites**, adapting Roland Barthes. Kernels are the branching points where choices are made; remove one and the logic of the story breaks. Satellites fill in, elaborate and delay; remove one and the story survives, though it may be poorer.

## Use it

- **Journalism:** This is the reporter's core skill. You find the fabula by reporting; you write the syuzhet. The inverted pyramid ignores chronology altogether and orders by importance. The feature opens with a scene, jumps back to explain (analepsis), and saves a moment for the end. The iterative ("each morning at six, the line forms...") lets you describe a pattern you can verify rather than inventing one dramatic day. And because you choose order, not events, order is where honesty lives: rearranging is fair, implying a false sequence is not.
- **Screen:** Film has the most vivid tools: the flashback, the flash-forward, the jump cut. *Citizen Kane* tells a life out of order through witnesses. *Memento* runs much of its story backward. *Pulp Fiction* reorders so a dead man walks out alive at the end. Ask what each anachrony buys you: suspense, irony or a new meaning for what came before.
- **Stage:** Theatre usually runs forward in real time, so any rearrangement stands out. Harold Pinter's *Betrayal* (1978) plays most of an affair in reverse, so the audience watches each scene already knowing how it ends. Duration matters too: an interval can skip years in a single breath.

## The pushback

- **A tidy fabula is a fiction.** Critics such as Jonathan Culler have argued that we don't always know the events before the telling. Sometimes the discourse seems to produce the event: in *Oedipus*, the crime is "established" because the story needs it, not by evidence. The hierarchy can run both ways.
- **No story without a version.** Barbara Herrnstein Smith argued that there is no basic story underneath all versions, only more versions. This is especially pointed for journalism, where the "events" are themselves reconstructed from accounts.
- **Terminology chaos.** Fabula, story, *histoire*, *récit*, plot, syuzhet, discourse: the words overlap and conflict between theorists, and even Forster's story and plot don't line up neatly with them.
- **Analysis, not recipe.** These are tools for seeing what a telling does. They won't tell you which order is best for a particular story.

## Try it

Separate the events from the telling in your own draft.

1. List the events of your story in the order they happened, and number them. That's your fabula.
2. Now list your scenes or sections in the order you tell them, using those numbers. Every jump back is an analepsis, every jump ahead a prolepsis. Beside each, note what it buys you.
3. Mark the speed of each section: scene, summary, ellipsis or pause. Is your most important moment in scene?
4. Try one different order, opening on a later event, and write just the first page of that version.

- **Stage:** borrow the trick of *Betrayal* on paper: swap two scenes and ask what the audience now knows going in.
- **Screen:** mark each ellipsis in your outline. Could a scene start later, cutting more?
- **Journalism:** compare your draft against the fabula, and check that no rearrangement implies a false sequence. Then look for one pattern you can tell iteratively ("every morning...").

**Check:** For each departure from chronology, can you say what it does: suspense, irony or new meaning?

## Check yourself

Q: What's the difference between fabula and syuzhet?
A: The fabula is the events in chronological and causal order. The syuzhet is how they're presented: the order, emphasis and omissions of the telling.

Q: What are analepsis and prolepsis?
A: A flashback and a flash-forward: the telling jumps back to something earlier or ahead to something later.

Q: "Every summer the family drove to the coast." Which kind of frequency is this?
A: Iterative: told once, but it happened many times.

Q: What's the difference between who speaks and who sees?
A: The narrator speaks; the focalizer is the character whose perception the telling follows. A third-person narrator can be focalized through one character.

Q: Why is this distinction especially important to journalists?
A: They can't change the events, only the telling. Order, speed and perspective are their main structural tools, and choosing them honestly is an ethical matter.
