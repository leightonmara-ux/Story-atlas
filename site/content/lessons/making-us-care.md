## Where it comes from

Every writer runs into the same problem sooner or later: the reader doesn't care. The plot works, the stakes are real, and still nobody minds what happens to the person at the center. Writers have always solved this by instinct. In the last few decades, screenwriting teachers gave the tools names.

The most famous name is Blake Snyder's **save the cat**, from his book *Save the Cat!* (2005): an early moment where the hero does something that makes us want to follow them. The *Blake Snyder* lesson covers it, and his beat sheet, in full.

Before Snyder, the script consultant **Michael Hauge** had set out a fuller toolkit in ***Writing Screenplays That Sell*** (1988, revised in 2011). Hauge's word for the goal is **identification**: the audience has to step into the hero's shoes and experience the story through them. Without it, he argues, nothing else in the script lands.

A third source is short and quoted everywhere. In 2011 **Emma Coats**, then a story artist at Pixar, tweeted a series of story lessons she'd picked up there. Rule #1 is about exactly this problem (see *Pixar's story rules*).

The phrases **pet the dog** (a kind act that softens a hard character) and **kick the dog** (a cruel act that marks someone as a villain) are informal industry slang. Nobody knows for sure who coined them.

## The big ideas

### Caring is not the same as liking

Start here, because it saves a lot of bad writing. You don't need the audience to approve of a character, or want to have dinner with them. You need them to be *invested*: to want to know what happens to this person, and to feel it when it does. Liking is one route there. It isn't the only one, and it isn't always the strongest.

### Hauge's ways in

Hauge lists several ways to create identification. Each one works differently.

- **Sympathy through undeserved misfortune.** We side with people who are hurt through no fault of their own. Cinderella, made a servant in her own house. Harry Potter, sleeping in the cupboard under the stairs. The key word is *undeserved*.
- **Jeopardy.** Put someone in danger and we worry about them, almost regardless of who they are. Even a stranger clinging to a ledge gets our attention.
- **Likability.** A character who is good, generous or kind to others earns goodwill. This is Snyder's cat.
- **Humor.** We warm to people who make us laugh, especially if they can laugh at themselves.
- **Power or skill.** We're drawn to people who are very good at something. Competence is attractive in itself.

Hauge's practical advice is to combine several of these, and to do it early: the audience decides fast whom to attach to.

### The opening of Up

Pixar's *Up* (2009) is a masterclass in the first tool. In the first ten minutes or so, a wordless montage shows Carl and Ellie's whole marriage: their wedding, their plans to travel, the jar they keep saving in and keep breaking open for life's emergencies, the child they can't have, and finally Ellie's illness and death. By the time we meet Carl as a grumpy old man shutting out the world, we know exactly why. A character who would be merely irritating has become someone we ache for.

### Admire them for trying

Coats's rule #1 is the shortest summary of the whole topic:

> You admire a character for trying more than for their successes.
> — Emma Coats, rule #1

Effort makes us lean in. A character who wants something badly, and visibly works for it, gives us something to hope with. This is the engine of the **underdog**. In *Rocky* (1976), Rocky Balboa is a small-time boxer who works as muscle for a loan shark. Early on he's sent to break a man's thumb over a debt and doesn't, and he talks to his pet turtles in a shabby apartment. Those are small pet-the-dog touches. What really binds us to him is that he is given an impossible chance and decides, against all sense, to go the distance.

### Competence

Watch how often a story introduces a hero by letting them be brilliant. In Arthur Conan Doyle's *A Study in Scarlet* (1887), Sherlock Holmes meets Watson and, within moments, tells him he has been in Afghanistan. Holmes is cold, odd and rude. We follow him anyway, because watching someone be superb at something is a pleasure. Competence carries many difficult characters: surgeons, detectives, con artists, killers.

### Why we follow people we don't like

Some of the most gripping characters are ones we would cross the street to avoid. Three things hold us:

1. **Fascination.** We want to see what this person will do next. Shakespeare's Richard III opens his play alone, confiding his plans to us. We become his accomplices before we've decided whether we approve.
2. **Competence.** As above. Tom Ripley, in Patricia Highsmith's novels, keeps readers turning pages because he is so good at getting away with it.
3. **Worse people around them.** We judge on a curve. In *The Godfather* (1972), the Corleones are criminals, but the rival gangsters and the corrupt police captain around them are colder and crueller, so we take the family's side.

The tools lesson *Whose side are we on?* explains this more precisely, with Murray Smith's distinction between understanding a character and siding with them.

Notice too how stories spend sympathy. *Breaking Bad* opens with Walter White humiliated: an underpaid chemistry teacher, working a second job at a car wash, then diagnosed with cancer. That undeserved misfortune buys a lot of credit, and the series spends it, episode by episode, until we are watching a monster we once rooted for.

### Pet the dog, kick the dog

These are the quick, cheap versions. **Pet the dog** gives a hard character one moment of tenderness, which tells us there's more to them. **Kick the dog** gives a villain one moment of gratuitous cruelty, so we know where we stand: Cruella de Vil wants to make coats out of puppies.

Used once and well, both work. Used lazily, they're visible from the back row. The best versions grow out of the character's want: Rocky not breaking the thumb isn't decoration, it tells us he isn't really cut out for the life he's living.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Plays often can't show a backstory montage, so lean on wanting and trying. Give your protagonist a clear, urgent want in the first scene, and let us watch them work for it. On stage, direct address (as in *Richard III*) is a powerful way to make an unlikable character an intimate.
- **Screen:** Check your hero's first five minutes against Hauge's list. Can you name at least two of his tools at work? If your hero is hard, decide what holds us instead: fascination, skill, or worse people nearby.
- **Journalism:** You earn a reader's care for a real person by specificity, not adjectives. Words like "tragic" and "heartbreaking" ask the reader to feel; concrete detail and the person's own want make them feel. Show what the person is trying to do and what stands in their way. Chekhov gave a version of this advice to a fellow writer: when you write about unhappy people, be colder, and the reader will feel more. *Jon Franklin*'s "Mrs. Kelly's Monster" is a model of restraint about a patient facing a dangerous operation.

## The pushback

- **Sympathy can be manipulative.** A dead spouse or a sick child can feel like a lever pulled on the audience. Viewers notice, and resent it, when misfortune is there only to buy their tears.
- **Likable isn't the goal.** Read too literally, save the cat turns every hero nice and every story bland. Many great works keep us at a deliberate distance, and *Brecht* built a whole theatre on refusing easy identification.
- **Identification is not universal.** Who finds a character easy to identify with depends on who is watching. Stories that assume a default viewer can leave many people outside (see *Who gets to be the subject*).
- **One moment isn't enough.** A single kind act in act one can't carry a character who is hollow everywhere else. Care is built across a whole work.

## Try it

Take your protagonist's first appearance, or the first ten minutes of a film you love.

1. List every moment that might make us care, and label each with one of Hauge's tools: misfortune, jeopardy, likability, humor, skill.
2. Write one sentence for what the character wants, and point to where we see them trying for it.
3. If your character is unlikable, name what holds us instead: fascination, competence or worse people nearby.
4. Add or sharpen one pet-the-dog moment that grows out of the character's want, rather than decorating it.

- **Stage:** Rewrite your protagonist's first entrance so we see them trying for something within a minute.
- **Screen:** Do it for the first five pages; aim for at least two tools before page 5.
- **Journalism:** Cut every adjective that tells the reader how to feel about your subject, and replace one with a concrete detail of what they're trying to do.

**Check:** Could a reader say, after one scene, what this person wants and why they'd mind if they didn't get it?

## Check yourself

Q: What does Michael Hauge mean by identification?
A: The audience stepping into the hero's shoes and experiencing the story through them. He sees it as the foundation of everything else.

Q: Name four of Hauge's ways to create it.
A: Any four of: sympathy through undeserved misfortune, jeopardy, likability, humor, and power or skill.

Q: What does Emma Coats's rule #1 say?
A: We admire a character for trying more than for their successes.

Q: Name three things that can make us follow an unlikable character.
A: Fascination, competence, and being surrounded by people who are worse.

Q: What's the difference between "pet the dog" and "kick the dog"?
A: Pet the dog softens a hard character with one kind moment; kick the dog marks a villain with one cruel one.
