## Where it comes from

***Bildungsroman*** is German: *Roman* means novel, and *Bildung* means formation, cultivation, education in the widest sense, the shaping of a whole person. So: the novel of formation. In English it usually travels as the **coming-of-age story**.

The word was coined by **Karl Morgenstern**, a professor of classics at the University of Dorpat (now Tartu, in Estonia), in lectures given in 1819 and 1820. It stayed obscure until the philosopher **Wilhelm Dilthey** used it in his 1870 biography of Friedrich Schleiermacher and in later essays, which made it standard. Dilthey is often credited with inventing it; Morgenstern got there first.

Both men had the same model in mind: **Goethe**'s ***Wilhelm Meister's Apprenticeship*** (1795–96). Wilhelm, a merchant's son, runs off to join a travelling theatre company, believing the stage is his calling. (Along the way he directs and plays Hamlet, and his reflections on the play became famous in their own right.) He learns that the theatre isn't his life after all, and discovers that a secret society, the Society of the Tower, has been quietly watching and guiding his education. The novel ends with him ready to take a useful place in the world, and engaged to marry.

## The big ideas

### What the form promises

A bildungsroman makes the reader three promises:

1. **Formation.** We will watch a young person become someone: their character, values and sense of their place in the world, built over years rather than days.
2. **Education.** Not only school. The hero learns from mistakes, mentors, love, work, cities and failure. The story is a curriculum.
3. **An ending that answers the question "who will they be?"** Classically, that means **accommodation**: the young person makes peace with society, takes a role, often marries. But a strong strand ends in **rupture**: the hero refuses the role on offer and leaves, or is broken by it.

The critic Franco Moretti, in *The Way of the World* (1987), argued that this tension is the form's real subject. Modern societies prize both freedom and belonging, and youth is where the two collide. Every bildungsroman stages a negotiation between becoming yourself and fitting in.

### The English line

The English novel took to the form. Charlotte Brontë's ***Jane Eyre*** (1847) follows its narrator from an unloved childhood through a brutal school, a post as governess, love, a terrible discovery and flight, to an ending where she returns on her own terms, independent, to marry Rochester. Accommodation, yes, but bargained for.

Dickens wrote two. ***David Copperfield*** (1849–50) is the warm version: an orphaned boy grows into a successful writer, and the book is close to Dickens's own life. ***Great Expectations*** (1860–61) is the bitter one. Pip, a blacksmith's boy, comes into money from a secret benefactor and becomes a snob. The education he gets is learning that his "expectations" were the wrong ones: the benefactor is the convict he once helped, and the people he looked down on were the ones worth having. Dickens rewrote the ending at a friend's urging, and readers still argue over which is truer.

### The künstlerroman

A branch of the form follows an artist's growth: the ***künstlerroman***, or artist novel. Its ending is usually rupture, because the artist must break with home to make art. The great example is James Joyce's ***A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man*** (1916). Stephen Dedalus breaks in turn with family, Church and nation, and in the book's last pages leaves Ireland, determined, in his famous phrase, "to forge in the smithy of my soul the uncreated conscience of my race."

### Whose formation?

The classic form assumed a young man who could leave home, choose a profession and roam. A young woman in 1800 could do few of those things. In *The Voyage In: Fictions of Female Development* (1983), Elizabeth Abel, Marianne Hirsch and Elizabeth Langland argued that women's novels of development often take other shapes: growth that is delayed until adulthood, or turned inward, or blocked, ending not in a career but in marriage, madness or death. George Eliot's *The Mill on the Floss* (1860), where Maggie Tulliver drowns, is a standard case.

Critics have made similar arguments for writers of color and colonized peoples, whose heroes are formed by societies that don't offer them a full place to accommodate to. For them, rupture may be the only honest ending.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Theatre struggles with years of growth in two hours, so stage coming-of-age stories usually compress it into a crisis. Frank Wedekind's *Spring Awakening* (written 1891, first staged in 1906 by Max Reinhardt in Berlin) shows teenagers destroyed by adults who won't tell them the truth about sex: a bildungsroman ending in rupture and death. Duncan Sheik and Steven Sater's 2006 musical adaptation won the Tony for Best Musical. Pick the one season where formation happens fastest.
- **Screen:** Film can show time passing on a face. Truffaut's *The 400 Blows* (1959) ends with Antoine Doinel escaping a reform school and running, in a long tracking shot, to the sea he has never seen; he turns toward the camera and the image freezes. Nothing is resolved: who he'll become is left to us. Richard Linklater's *Boyhood* (2014) was shot over twelve years with the same young actor, Ellar Coltrane. Greta Gerwig's *Lady Bird* (2017) ends with its heroine in New York, leaving a message for her mother, using her given name, Christine, after a film spent insisting on the one she chose. Barry Jenkins's *Moonlight* (2016) tells its hero's formation in three chapters, each named for what he's called at that age: Little, Chiron, Black.
- **Journalism:** The coming-of-age feature follows a young person through a threshold: a first year at college, a first season in a job, an immigrant teenager's first year at school. The best ones report over time and let the subject change between visits. Michael Apted's *Up* documentary series, revisiting the same British children every seven years from *Seven Up!* (1964) onward, is the form's longest experiment.

## The pushback

- **Too broad to mean much.** Any story with a young protagonist gets called a bildungsroman. Some critics argue the term should be kept for novels in the German tradition, about inner cultivation, not every high-school movie.
- **A conservative engine.** The classic ending rewards adjustment: settle down, marry, take your place. Critics on the left have read the form as teaching readers to accept the society they're given.
- **Built for the privileged.** As above, the model assumes freedoms that most people, historically, didn't have.
- **Growth isn't a straight line.** Real people regress, repeat and change in loops. A story of formation can flatten into a tidy lesson learned; compare *Want, need and wound* on character arcs, including the negative and flat ones.

## Try it
Take your work-in-progress, or a coming-of-age film you know well.
1. Write the protagonist's answer to "Who am I, and where do I belong?" at the start, in their own voice. Then write it as they'd say it at the end.
2. List three teachers the story gives them: a person, a mistake, a place. What does each teach?
3. Decide the ending's mode: accommodation (they take a place), rupture (they refuse it or are broken), or open. Write the final image so it shows that mode without anyone stating it.

- **Stage:** Compress the formation into one season or one night. Which single event forces the change?
- **Screen:** Find a way to show time on the character: a name, a costume, a body, a place they return to.
- **Journalism:** Plan two visits to your young subject, months apart. What will you ask both times?

**Check:** Is the person at the end recognizably formed by these particular lessons, and not just older?

## Check yourself

Q: What does *Bildung* mean, and who coined *bildungsroman*?
A: Formation or cultivation, the shaping of a whole person. Karl Morgenstern coined the term in 1819–20 lectures; Wilhelm Dilthey popularized it from 1870.

Q: Which novel is the form's model?
A: Goethe's *Wilhelm Meister's Apprenticeship* (1795–96).

Q: What are the two classic kinds of ending?
A: Accommodation, where the young person makes peace with society and takes a place in it, and rupture, where they refuse it, leave, or are broken.

Q: What's a künstlerroman?
A: A novel of an artist's growth, like Joyce's *A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man*; it usually ends with the artist breaking away to make art.

Q: What did critics of the female bildungsroman argue?
A: That the classic form assumed a young man's freedoms, and that women's novels of development often take other shapes: delayed, inward or blocked growth, ending in marriage, madness or death.
