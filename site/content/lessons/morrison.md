## Who and when

Toni Morrison (1931–2019) was born Chloe Wofford in Lorain, Ohio. She worked for years as an editor at Random House while writing her first novels, *The Bluest Eye* (1970), *Sula* (1973) and *Song of Solomon* (1977). *Beloved* (1987) won the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction, and in 1993 she won the Nobel Prize in Literature.

Her clearest statement on craft is a short talk, "The Site of Memory". It was part of a lecture series on memoir, and William Zinsser collected it in *Inventing the Truth: The Art and Craft of Memoir* (1987; later editions followed). It is reprinted in her late essay collection *The Source of Self-Regard* (2019). Morrison wrote it while finishing *Beloved*, and it explains where that novel came from. But its method works for anyone who builds a story out of a record with holes in it: a historical playwright, a screenwriter adapting real events, a reporter.

## The big ideas

### What the record left out

Morrison begins with the slave narratives, the autobiographies of formerly enslaved people such as Frederick Douglass and Harriet Jacobs (who wrote as "Linda Brent"). These books were written to change minds. Their first readers were largely white, and the narratives came wrapped in prefaces by white sponsors vouching that they were real. So the writers were careful. They kept to facts that would persuade, avoided anything that might offend, and stayed quiet about much of what they felt.

Again and again, Morrison notes, a narrator reaches something unbearable and stops, saying in effect that a veil should be drawn over it. What the narratives rarely show, she argues, is the **interior life** of the people who lived through slavery: their private thoughts, desires and grief. The record is real, but it was shaped by who it was written for, and the most important part is missing. Her task, as she put it:

> My job becomes how to rip that veil drawn over "proceedings too terrible to relate."
— Toni Morrison, "The Site of Memory" (1987)

For a writer of any kind the lesson is a habit of mind. Every document was written by someone, for someone. Ask who the audience was and what the writer couldn't afford to say. The gap often marks where your story is.

### Literary archaeology

How do you write what nobody wrote down? Morrison calls her method **literary archaeology**. An archaeologist goes to a site, looks at what remains (a wall, a few pots, some bones) and works out what kind of world those remains imply. Morrison does the same with the past. She starts from some information and a little guesswork, travels to the "site" of memory, and reconstructs a world that fits what she found.

Two things keep this from being mere invention. The remains are real, so the reconstruction has to be consistent with them. And the imagining is disciplined: it fills gaps, it doesn't overwrite evidence.

*Beloved* is the model. Its seed was the true story of Margaret Garner, who escaped slavery in Kentucky in 1856 and, when slave catchers found her in Ohio, killed her daughter rather than see her taken back. Morrison came across a newspaper account while editing *The Black Book* (1974), an anthology of documents from Black American history. She has said she deliberately didn't research Garner further: the novel invents Sethe's inner life, the very thing the record didn't keep.

### Fact versus truth

The talk's most quoted distinction is not between fact and fiction:

> the crucial distinction for me is not the difference between fact and fiction, but the distinction between fact and truth. Because facts can exist without human intelligence, but truth cannot.
— Toni Morrison, "The Site of Memory" (1987)

A fact is a date, a dollar figure, a court record. It stays true whether or not anyone thinks about it. Truth, in Morrison's sense, is what those facts meant to the people inside them, and it only exists when a mind makes sense of them. A novel can invent facts and still tell the truth. A meticulously sourced article can get every fact right and miss it.

This sits close to *Vivian Gornick*'s situation and story, and to *Hayden White & Joan Didion* on how facts don't arrive with their meaning attached. Morrison adds a moral urgency: the truth of some lives was left out of the record on purpose.

### Start from the image

Morrison says her most trustworthy route into a book runs from a picture to the words, not the other way round. She doesn't begin with a theme or a thesis. She begins with an image from memory, a remembered object, a person, a place, and asks what it is about; the text grows from there. Ideas arrived at first tend to produce illustrations of the idea. An image arrived at first carries feeling that the writer hasn't yet explained, so the writing has somewhere to go.

She also describes memory itself as active, closer to a flood returning than to a filing cabinet. Writers, she suggests, are drawn back to where they were the way a straightened river keeps flooding its old bed.

### Language as an act

Two other sources round out her craft. In her Nobel Lecture (1993), she told a parable: young people try to trick a blind, wise old woman by asking whether the bird in their hands is alive or dead. She answers that she doesn't know, but that it is in their hands. Morrison reads the bird as language, and the writer as responsible for whether it lives. She argues that oppressive language doesn't just describe violence; it *is* violence. For a writer that means every word choice is a choice about whose reality gets to count.

In *The Paris Review*'s "Art of Fiction" interview (No. 134, 1993), she describes drafting by hand in pencil, revising a great deal, and recognizing a point where revision turns into fretting that only wears the work down.

## Use it

- **Stage:** History plays and plays drawn from real events (see *Lynn Nottage*) face Morrison's problem directly: the archive has the trial transcript, not what anyone felt that night. Find the moment your sources go silent and stage it. Theatre is especially good at interiority made visible: a ghost, a memory walking onstage, as Beloved herself does.
- **Screen:** Adaptations of true stories are often built from public records. Before writing, list what the records were for (a court case, a sale, a news story) and who they were meant to persuade. Then let a key scene grow from a single image rather than a plot summary.
- **Journalism:** Morrison is writing fiction, and journalists can't invent an interior life (see *Janet Malcolm & John Hersey*). But her first step is pure reporting: read every document asking who wrote it, for whom, and what they left out. Go to the site. Then use interviews to recover, from the people themselves, what the record didn't keep. Her fact/truth distinction is a useful test of a finished piece: every fact checks out, but is it true?

## The pushback

- **Imagining others' interiors.** Literary archaeology fills silences with imagination. Some argue that's exactly what the archive's victims deserve; others worry that invented inner lives can replace real voices. The historian Saidiya Hartman's essay "Venus in Two Acts" (2008) names a related method, **critical fabulation**, and is candid about its limits: the archive's gaps can't truly be filled, only written against.
- **Facts still matter.** "Fact versus truth" is liberating in fiction and dangerous elsewhere. Writers who have invented in nonfiction often appealed to a deeper truth. Morrison's distinction doesn't license that; she was writing novels, and said so.
- **Reception.** *Beloved* was attacked as well as praised. The critic Stanley Crouch's hostile review in *The New Republic* (1987) accused it of melodrama and of exploiting suffering. Its standing has since grown, and it is now among the most widely taught American novels.

## Try it

Pick a document behind your project: a court record, a letter, an old article, a family photo, a police report.

1. Write down who made it, for whom, and what it was meant to do.
2. List three things it couldn't or wouldn't say.
3. Choose one silence and write the moment it hides, starting from a single concrete image (an object, a gesture, a sound), not from an idea.
4. Check every line against the document: does anything contradict the facts you have?

- **Stage:** write the silence as a short scene, or as a monologue spoken by someone the record mentions only in passing.
- **Screen:** write it as one wordless scene built around the image.
- **Journalism:** don't invent. Turn your list of silences into interview questions, and find who can answer them.

**Check:** Does your piece reveal something the record left out, without contradicting anything the record got right?

## Check yourself

Q: Why, according to Morrison, did the slave narratives leave so much out?
A: They were written to persuade a largely white readership, so their authors stuck to facts that would move readers and avoided what might offend, drawing a veil over the worst events and their own inner lives.

Q: What is literary archaeology?
A: Morrison's method: start from real remains (documents, fragments, memories), go to the site, and reconstruct the world those remains imply, imagining only what fits the evidence.

Q: What's the difference between fact and truth for Morrison?
A: Facts exist whether or not anyone thinks about them; truth needs a human mind to make sense of the facts. Fiction can invent facts and still be true.

Q: Why does Morrison prefer to start from an image rather than an idea?
A: An image carries feeling the writer hasn't yet explained, so the work can discover something; starting from an idea tends to produce illustration.

Q: How should a journalist use Morrison without breaking journalism's rules?
A: By reading the record for what it omits and why, then reporting the gaps through interviews and observation, not by imagining people's inner lives.
