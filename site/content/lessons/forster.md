## Who and when

E. M. Forster was the novelist behind *A Room with a View* (1908), *Howards End* (1910) and *A Passage to India* (1924). In 1927 he gave the Clark Lectures at Trinity College, Cambridge, and published them the same year as *Aspects of the Novel*.

The book still reads like a talk: relaxed, funny, opinionated, full of asides. Forster refuses to treat the novel historically. He imagines all novelists, from every period, sitting together in a circular room writing at the same time, and asks what they share. His answer is a set of "aspects": story, people, plot, fantasy, prophecy, and pattern and rhythm.

Two of his ideas escaped the book entirely and became part of how everyone talks about fiction: the difference between **story and plot**, and the difference between **flat and round characters**.

## The big ideas

### Story: "and then?"

Forster defines story at its most basic as a narrative of events arranged in time order. It can only have one merit, he says: making the audience want to know what happens next. And one fault: making them not want to.

His emblem is Scheherazade, who survives a thousand and one nights not because of her style or her characters but because she keeps the Sultan wondering what will happen next. Forster is famously grudging about this:

> Yes—oh, dear, yes—the novel tells a story.
> — E. M. Forster, Aspects of the Novel

He calls story the lowest and simplest of literary organisms, and wishes the novel could be something finer. But he admits that it is the backbone, and that no novel can do without it.

### Plot: "why?"

Plot is also a narrative of events, but with the emphasis on **causality**. This is where his famous example comes from, the one quoted in *The shared core*: "the king died and then the queen died" is a story, while "the king died, and then the queen died of grief" is a plot.

Forster pushes it one step further. "The queen died, no one knew why, until it was discovered that it was through grief at the death of the king" is a plot with a **mystery** in it, and mystery, he says, is essential to plot at a higher level. Time order is suspended; the reader has to hold a question open.

The difference shows up in the audience's question. A story makes us ask *and then?* A plot makes us ask *why?* And plot asks more of us: intelligence to notice causes, and memory to connect a clue from early on with what it finally means.

### Flat characters

A **flat** character, in its purest form, is built around a single idea or quality, and can often be summed up in one sentence. Forster's example is Mrs Micawber in Dickens's *David Copperfield*, who is entirely expressed by her insistence that she will never desert Mr Micawber.

Crucially, Forster doesn't sneer at flat characters. They are easy to recognize whenever they appear, and easy to remember afterward. They don't need introducing or developing. Dickens, he notes, filled his novels with flat people and yet gives a wonderful sense of human depth. A comic novel often needs them, and so does any story with a large cast.

### Round characters

A **round** character is one with enough dimensions to change and to surprise us. Forster's test is the most quoted line in the book:

> The test of a round character is whether it is capable of surprising in a convincing way.
> — E. M. Forster, Aspects of the Novel

Both halves matter. A character who never surprises is flat. A character who surprises *unconvincingly* is just badly written. The great round characters, for Forster, include many of Jane Austen's: even her minor people feel ready to step out of their roles.

### Bouncing the reader

On point of view, Forster takes a refreshingly practical line. Critics of his time worried about whether a novelist should stay inside one character's head. Forster says what matters is the writer's power to **bounce** the reader into accepting what is said. Dickens shifts viewpoint all over the place and gets away with it. If the reader is carried along, the rules don't matter; if not, following them won't help.

### Pattern and rhythm

In his final lecture Forster reaches past plot toward something more like music. **Pattern** is the overall shape a novel leaves in the mind, which he compares to an hourglass in one novel, where two characters' fortunes cross and swap places. **Rhythm** is subtler: an image or phrase that returns with variations, gathering meaning each time. His model is the little musical phrase that recurs in Proust. He calls it, in essence, repetition plus variation.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Flat and round characters are a casting and writing tool. A play with a small cast needs most people round. Farce and satire thrive on flat ones, whose single quality can be pushed to absurdity.
- **Screen:** "Why?" not "and then?" is the difference between an episodic script and a plotted one. Rewrite every "and then" in your outline as a cause. And use Forster's mystery: withhold the reason for an event and let the audience work for it.
- **Journalism:** News is often pure story: this happened, then this. Explanatory and investigative pieces are plot: they answer *why*. Forster's queen-with-a-mystery is the shape of many investigations: something happened, no one knew why, until the reporter found out.

## The pushback

- **The king and queen are less clear-cut than they look.** Narrative theorists have pointed out that readers tend to infer a cause even when none is stated. Read "the king died and then the queen died" and many people supply the grief themselves. The distinction is really about emphasis, not two different kinds of text.
- **Flat versus round is a spectrum.** Forster admits as much, but the terms are often used as a simple verdict, with "flat" meaning "bad". He meant nothing of the kind.
- **Casual by design.** The lectures are impressionistic and almost entirely about British, French and Russian novels. They offer memorable distinctions, not a system.
- **His disdain for story** strikes many writers, especially in film and journalism, as snobbish. Wanting to know what happens next is not a low appetite to be apologized for.

## Read next

- E. M. Forster, *Aspects of the Novel* (1927). Short, witty and widely available.
- *Story vs. telling*, the lesson on Shklovsky, Genette and Chatman, for the more rigorous theory of how event order differs from telling order.
- James Wood, *How Fiction Works* (2008), which argues with Forster's flat and round characters at length.

## Check yourself

Q: What question does a story make the audience ask, and what question does a plot make them ask?
A: A story makes them ask "and then?" A plot makes them ask "why?"

Q: How does Forster add mystery to the king and queen example?
A: "The queen died, no one knew why, until it was discovered that it was through grief at the death of the king." The cause is withheld and then revealed.

Q: What is Forster's test of a round character?
A: Whether it is capable of surprising in a convincing way.

Q: Does Forster think flat characters are a failure?
A: No. They're easily recognized and remembered and are often exactly what a novel needs. Dickens's flat characters are his example.

Q: What does Forster mean by "bouncing" the reader?
A: The novelist's power to carry the reader into accepting what's said. He thinks that matters more than keeping a consistent point of view.
