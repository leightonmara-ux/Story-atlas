## Where it comes from

Open almost any newspaper or magazine feature and you'll find the same shape. It starts with a person in a moment: a farmer staring at a dry well, a nurse on her third double shift this week. A few paragraphs later, the story pulls back and tells you what this is really about: droughts are emptying wells across the region, or hospitals can't keep staff. Then comes the evidence. Then, at the end, we often return to the farmer or the nurse.

This is usually called the **Wall Street Journal formula**, because the *Journal*'s front-page features, developed from the middle of the 20th century, made it famous and taught it to generations of reporters. It's also called the feature formula, the anecdotal-lede formula, or "specific to general to specific".

Its best-known teacher is **William E. Blundell**, a *Journal* reporter and editor who ran writing training for the paper. His book *The Art and Craft of Feature Writing* (1988) is subtitled "Based on The Wall Street Journal Guide". Interestingly, Blundell spends far more time on reporting and thinking than on the formula itself. More on that below.

## The big ideas

### The four parts

{{diagram:wsj-formula}}

1. **The anecdotal lede.** A short scene or story about one person or one moment that embodies the larger subject. It's chosen because it's vivid *and* typical. It makes an abstract issue human.
2. **The nut graf.** The paragraph that says what the story is about and why it matters now. It connects the anecdote to the bigger picture, and it's the promise the rest of the story has to keep. Editors sometimes call it the "so what" paragraph.
3. **The body.** The evidence and the explanation: numbers, experts, history, more examples, the other side. Good features organize the body by ideas, not by the order the reporter happened to learn things.
4. **The kicker.** The ending. Often it circles back to the person from the lede, so we see them again in light of everything we've learned. Sometimes it's a strong quotation or a telling final detail.

### Why the nut graf matters so much

The anecdote buys attention; the nut graf earns it. A reader who has enjoyed three paragraphs about a farmer will still leave if they don't soon learn why they're reading about him. As *Same skeleton, three media* puts it, readers can leave at any moment, so journalism has to state its promise early.

The commonest failure is a beautiful lede followed by a vague nut graf ("It's a story playing out across the country"). The nut graf should say something specific: what's happening, how big it is, and why now.

### Specific, general, specific

The formula alternates between the particular and the broad. A single person makes a subject feel real; numbers and context make it matter. A story that is all anecdote is a pleasant sketch with no point; a story that is all generalization is a report nobody finishes. The formula moves between the two on purpose.

### Choosing the right anecdote

The lede anecdote has to do two jobs: be interesting, and be *representative*. An unusual story can mislead if it suggests a typical case. Blundell-trained writers ask: does this person's experience really stand for the larger trend? If not, find one that does, or say plainly that it's an extreme case.

### Blundell's real lesson: think before you write

Blundell argued that most bad features fail before the writing starts, because the reporter hasn't figured out what the story is. He suggested that a feature about a trend or development usually needs to cover a set of dimensions, which he listed as:

- **History:** where did this come from?
- **Scope:** how big and widespread is it?
- **Reasons:** what's causing it?
- **Impact:** who is affected, and how?
- **Countermoves:** who's pushing back, and what's being done?
- **Future:** where is it heading?

These aren't sections to write in order. They're a checklist for reporting, so that by the time you sit down, you have a body worth organizing. The formula gives you the outside of the story; Blundell's dimensions fill the inside.

## How it relates to the dramatic arc

The feature formula isn't a full story structure in the sense of *Jon Franklin*. The anecdotal lede often contains a small arc of its own, and the kicker can feel like a resolution. But the main question the reader carries is "what's going on here?", not "what will happen to this person?"

That's why it's so flexible. It works when nothing has resolved, which is exactly when Franklin's narrative can't be used.

## Use it

- **Journalism:** The default shape for trend stories, explainers with a human face, and most magazine features. Try writing the nut graf first, in one or two sentences, before you choose a lede. If you can't write it, you need more reporting.
- **Screen:** Documentaries often use the same move: a cold open on one person, a pull-back to the issue, and a return to that person at the end.
- **Stage:** Less directly useful, but the principle that a single human case makes an abstract subject bearable applies to issue plays and verbatim theater.

## The pushback

- **It's a formula.** Readers can spot it, and a lede about "Jane Doe, 47" has become a cliché. Many editors now warn against the reflexive anecdotal lede, especially when a straight lede would be stronger.
- **The anecdote can mislead.** Picking the most vivid case to stand for a trend can exaggerate it, or turn a structural problem into a personal one.
- **The slow start.** On phones, some readers want the news immediately. A hybrid with the nut graf near the top, sometimes even first, is increasingly common.
- **The circle-back can feel neat.** Returning to the opening person can make a messy, ongoing story seem more resolved than it is.

## Try it

Take a feature you're working on, or a trend story you'd like to write.

1. Write the nut graf first, in one or two sentences: what's happening, how big it is, and why it matters now. If you can't, list which of Blundell's dimensions (history, scope, reasons, impact, countermoves, future) you still need to report.
2. Choose a lede anecdote and test it: is it vivid, and does this person's experience really stand for the larger trend?
3. Outline the body by ideas, not in the order you learned things.
4. Write a kicker that returns to the person from the lede, seen in the light of everything since.

- **Stage:** for an issue play or verbatim piece, choose the one human case that makes the abstract subject bearable.
- **Screen:** sketch a documentary as a cold open on one person, a pull-back to the issue, and a return to that person at the end.
- **Journalism:** also try a hybrid with the nut graf as the second paragraph, and see whether the lede still earns its place.

**Check:** After the first four paragraphs, could a reader say what the story is about and why it matters now?

## Check yourself

Q: What are the four parts of the feature formula?
A: An anecdotal lede, a nut graf, the body, and a kicker that often circles back to the opening.

Q: What job does the nut graf do?
A: It tells the reader what the story is really about and why it matters now, connecting the lede to the bigger picture.

Q: What two jobs must the lede anecdote do?
A: Be vivid enough to draw readers in and representative enough not to mislead them about the larger subject.

Q: What was Blundell's main point about feature writing?
A: Most features fail before the writing, in the reporting and thinking. He offered dimensions to cover, such as history, scope, reasons, impact, countermoves and future.

Q: Why can the feature formula be used where Franklin's narrative can't?
A: It doesn't need a resolution. Its question is "what's going on?", so it works for situations that are still unfolding.
