## Who and when

James Baldwin (1924–1987) grew up in Harlem, the eldest of nine children, and spent his early teens as a boy preacher in a Pentecostal church. In 1948 he moved to Paris, and France remained his base for most of his life; he died in Saint-Paul-de-Vence. He wrote novels, including *Go Tell It on the Mountain* (1953) and *Giovanni's Room* (1956), and two plays, but for many readers his essays are the heart of his work.

He's here for two questions: how do you put a real person on the page without shrinking them, and how do you turn your own life into testimony that matters to strangers?

## The big ideas

### Against the protest novel

Baldwin's first famous essay, "Everybody's Protest Novel", appeared in 1949 in *Zero*, a small Paris magazine, and was quickly reprinted in *Partisan Review*. It's an attack on Harriet Beecher Stowe's *Uncle Tom's Cabin* (1852), and, more shockingly at the time, on Richard Wright's *Native Son* (1940). Wright was the most celebrated Black novelist in America and had been Baldwin's mentor.

Baldwin's charge is that both books, for all their good intentions, do the same thing as the injustice they protest: they turn a human being into a category. Stowe's Uncle Tom is a symbol of saintly suffering; Wright's Bigger Thomas, a violent young man driven to murder, is in Baldwin's reading Uncle Tom's angry descendant, a figure defined entirely by what society has done to him. Neither is allowed to be a whole person. The essay's central sentence:

> The failure of the protest novel lies in its rejection of life, the human being, the denial of his beauty, dread, power, in its insistence that it is his categorization alone which is real and which cannot be transcended.
— James Baldwin, "Everybody's Protest Novel" (1949)

He also takes aim at **sentimentality**, which he describes as a showy parade of excessive, false feeling that is really a sign of dishonesty and an inability to feel. A sentimental book lets readers enjoy their own compassion and close the cover feeling virtuous, without ever meeting a person as complicated as themselves.

Baldwin isn't saying art should avoid politics; his own work is soaked in it. He's saying that a story which treats people as evidence for a cause fails twice, as art and as protest. Compare the single story in *Who gets to be the subject*, and *Making us care* on why caring isn't approving.

### Your own experience, wrung dry

"Autobiographical Notes", the short preface to *Notes of a Native Son*, is Baldwin's account of his job. He says a writer has only his own experience to work from, and that everything depends on how relentlessly he forces every last drop of meaning out of it, sweet or bitter.

He ends on his ambition, in one of his best-known lines: "I want to be an honest man and a good writer." The order matters. Honesty comes first, and the craft serves it.

### The essay as witness

The title essay of *Notes of a Native Son* opens with three facts. On 29 July 1943 his father died; a few hours later his father's last child was born; and his father's funeral fell on Baldwin's nineteenth birthday. The Harlem riot of August 1943 broke out in those same days, and the family drove to the cemetery through streets of smashed glass.

Baldwin braids these strands. The private story is a son trying to understand a bitter, proud father he couldn't love. The public story is the rage that Harlem's people carried, which the riot let loose. Between them sits a scene from his own year working in New Jersey, where, refused service at a restaurant, he threw a mug of water at a waitress and fled, frightened to discover he could have killed someone. The essay ends with an argument he has earned through the story: that one must accept life as it is, without bitterness, and at the same time never accept injustice as normal.

In *Vivian Gornick*'s terms, the situation is a death, a birth and a riot; the story is what hatred does to the person who carries it. (*The braided narrative* looks at the technique in long-form reporting.)

*The Fire Next Time* (1963) pushes further. A short open letter to his teenage nephew, for the hundredth anniversary of Emancipation, is joined to a long essay first published in *The New Yorker* in November 1962. That essay is memoir (his conversion at fourteen and years in the pulpit), reportage (a dinner in Chicago with Elijah Muhammad of the Nation of Islam, told scene by scene) and argument (America must face its history or face catastrophe). The title comes from the spiritual quoted at the end.

In a 1984 interview with Julius Lester in *The New York Times Book Review*, Baldwin drew the line that explains all of this: a spokesman presumes to speak for others; he saw himself instead as a **witness** to what he had seen.

### The artist as disturber

In "The Creative Process" (1962), written for the anthology *Creative America*, Baldwin argues that the artist's first distinction is the need to cultivate what most people avoid, the state of being alone with themselves. From that solitude the artist reports back what a society would rather not know about itself. That's why, he says, societies have always battled the artist as an "incorrigible disturber of the peace".

### At the desk

His 1984 *Paris Review* interview ("The Art of Fiction No. 78", with Jordan Elgrably and George Plimpton) is full of practical habits:

- **He worked at night.** He began when the household was asleep, a habit from years of day jobs and a crowded home.
- **Rewriting was where the truth came out.** He called revision painful, and said the hardest thing to achieve was simplicity, because it means stripping away disguises you didn't know you were wearing. The goal, he said, is a sentence as clean as a bone.
- **Learning to see.** He told how the painter Beauford Delaney, his friend and mentor, pointed at a puddle in a Greenwich Village street and said only "Look." Baldwin saw water; when he looked again he saw oil on it, and the city reflected in it. Look at what is actually in front of you.

### Writing for the stage

*The Amen Corner*, finished in 1954 and first staged at Howard University in 1955, centers on Sister Margaret, pastor of a Harlem storefront church, whose estranged husband, a jazz musician, comes home to die. The play sets church against family, and draws on Baldwin's own years in the pulpit.

*Blues for Mister Charlie* opened on Broadway in 1964 in an Actors Studio production. Loosely based on the 1955 murder of Emmett Till, it splits the stage by an aisle into Whitetown and Blacktown, and moves back and forth in time between a young Black man's murder and his killer's trial. In his preface, Baldwin wrote that his fear had been that he could never draw a valid portrait of the murderer. That's "Everybody's Protest Novel" applied to the stage: even the villain must be a person, or the play becomes a sermon.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Every character, especially the one the audience is meant to condemn, needs an inner logic they'd recognize. Baldwin's test for *Blues for Mister Charlie* is a good one: could you write a valid portrait of the person who did the worst thing in your play?
- **Screen:** "Issue" films fall into the protest-novel trap constantly: the victim who exists only to suffer, the bigot who exists only to be hateful. Give each a want, a contradiction and a moment that surprises us.
- **Journalism:** Baldwin is the model for the personal essay that does public work. Braid a private story with a public event so each explains the other. In profiles, don't let a subject become a symbol of their circumstances; report the beauty, dread and power. Be a witness, not a spokesman: report what you've seen rather than claiming to speak for a community.

## The pushback

- **Wright's answer.** Wright felt betrayed, and Baldwin later described their bitter confrontation in Paris in "Alas, Poor Richard" (in *Nobody Knows My Name*). Many readers think Baldwin misread *Native Son*, whose power lies partly in refusing to make Bigger likeable.
- **Howe and Ellison.** In "Black Boys and Native Sons" (*Dissent*, 1963), the critic Irving Howe defended Wright and argued that anger was the honest response, criticizing Baldwin and Ralph Ellison for retreating into art. Ellison answered with "The World and the Jug".
- **Did Baldwin follow his own advice?** Some reviewers called *Blues for Mister Charlie* propaganda, and critics have said his later essays slide toward the sermon. Others reply that his anger sharpened as the stakes did, and that he never stopped writing complicated people.
- **Attacks on the man.** Eldridge Cleaver's *Soul on Ice* (1968) attacked Baldwin in openly homophobic terms, and some Black Power writers dismissed him as too gentle. Most readers now think these attacks say more about the critics.

## Try it

Take a character, or a real person in a piece of reporting, who exists mainly to represent something: a victim, a villain, a problem.

1. Write one sentence saying what category they stand for.
2. Now list three things about them that the category can't explain: something they love, something they fear, a contradiction.
3. Rewrite one scene or paragraph so at least one of those things is visible on the page, without weakening the injustice they're part of.
4. If you're writing about yourself, find a public event happening at the same time as your private story, and write one paragraph where each sheds light on the other.

- **Stage:** do it for your antagonist. Write the speech they'd give in their own defense, and see what it changes.
- **Screen:** do it for the supporting character whose only job is to suffer or to be cruel.
- **Journalism:** do it for a profile subject or a person in a news feature who appears only as an example of a trend.

**Check:** Could a reader describe this person without using the category you started with?

## Check yourself

Q: What was Baldwin's main objection to *Uncle Tom's Cabin* and *Native Son* in "Everybody's Protest Novel"?
A: That they reduce people to categories defined by a social cause, denying their full humanity, and so fail both as art and as protest.

Q: Why does Baldwin distrust sentimentality?
A: He sees it as a showy display of excessive, false feeling that lets readers feel virtuous without ever confronting a real, complicated person. He calls it a mark of dishonesty.

Q: How is the title essay of *Notes of a Native Son* built?
A: It braids his father's death, the birth of his last sibling and his own nineteenth birthday with the 1943 Harlem riot, so that private bitterness and public rage illuminate each other, leading to an argument earned by the story.

Q: What's the difference between a spokesman and a witness, in Baldwin's sense?
A: A spokesman presumes to speak for others. A witness reports truthfully on what they have seen and lived.

Q: What did Baldwin tell *The Paris Review* was hardest to achieve in rewriting?
A: Simplicity, because it means stripping away disguises the writer didn't know they had; the goal is a sentence as clean as a bone.
