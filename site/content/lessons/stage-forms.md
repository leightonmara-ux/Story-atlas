## Where it comes from

Theatre's forms were named by the people who made and sold plays, long before critics theorized them. The editors of Shakespeare's First Folio (1623) sorted his plays into Comedies, Histories and Tragedies, which made "history" a category of drama. **Melodrama** was named in France around 1800, **farce** centuries earlier. Tennessee Williams labelled his own *The Glass Menagerie* (1944) a "memory play". **Verbatim theatre** got its name in the 1980s; the scholar Derek Paget's 1987 article "'Verbatim Theatre': Oral History and Documentary Techniques" gave the term wide currency.

The most important theorist here is **Peter Brooks** (born 1938), whose ***The Melodramatic Imagination*** (1976) took a despised popular form seriously. (He reappears in *Frank Kermode & Peter Brooks* with a later book on plot.)

Each form is a different promise to an audience: about time, about truth, and about how much feeling the stage is allowed to show. *Tragedy and comedy* covers the oldest pair, and *The well-made play* covers the nineteenth century's plot machine; this lesson takes the others.

## The big ideas

### Farce: the machine that speeds up

*Comedy structure* covers farce's mechanics: Feydeau's doors, the lie that needs another lie, the two people who must never meet. What matters here is the promise. Farce promises **acceleration**. Its characters are mostly flat (see *E. M. Forster*), each driven by one appetite or fear, so that the plot, not psychology, carries the evening. A farce usually starts calm and plausible, gets each secret into the house by the end of the first act, and spends the rest letting the pressure rise until everything collides.

Watch the second act of Michael Frayn's *Noises Off* (1982), which shows the same act of a bad farce from backstage, with the set turned around. The doors we watched in Act One are now seen from behind, and the real comedy is the actors' near-silent warfare as they run between them.

### Melodrama: virtue made visible

The word joins Greek *melos* (song) to drama: it began as spoken drama with music. Rousseau's *Pygmalion* (written 1762, staged 1770) set speech against orchestral passages, and in Paris around 1800 René-Charles Guilbert de Pixerécourt, often called the father of melodrama, made the form a popular hit with plays like *Cœlina* (1800). Thomas Holcroft's *A Tale of Mystery* (1802), adapted from it, brought the label to London, where only the licensed "patent" theatres could stage spoken drama until 1843, and plays with music were a way round the law.

Melodrama's shape is clear: innocent virtue at peace; a villain intrudes; virtue is persecuted and misjudged; then, in a great scene of recognition, virtue is revealed and the villain exposed. Music cued every emotion.

Brooks's argument is that this isn't just crude drama. After the French Revolution, he says, the old sacred order no longer guaranteed what good and evil meant, and melodrama answered by making moral forces **legible**, played out loud on bodies and faces. He calls this the **moral occult**: hidden moral truths that melodrama drags into the light. Characters say exactly what they feel; nothing stays subtext. Brooks also noted the form's love of muteness, gesture and tableau, moments where words fail and the body speaks.

Film inherited it. The scholar Linda Williams argued in 1998 that melodrama, not realism, is the basic mode of American cinema. A popular myth to drop: the villain tying a woman to the railway tracks is rarer in old melodrama than its fame suggests. In one early example, Augustin Daly's *Under the Gaslight* (1867), a man is tied to the tracks and the heroine saves him.

### The history play: the past rearranged

Shakespeare's ten English histories run from *King John* to *Henry VIII*. He drew on chronicles, especially Holinshed's, and reshaped them freely: he compressed years into scenes, made Hotspur Prince Hal's contemporary though the real man was much older, and invented Falstaff. The history play promises not the record but **a meaning for the record**: why power passes, and at what cost.

Its central freedom is invention in service of a question. Friedrich Schiller's *Mary Stuart* (1800) builds to a meeting between Mary and Elizabeth I that never happened, because the play needs the two queens face to face. Modern history plays keep the licence and often make it visible: Peter Shaffer's *Amadeus* (1979) is narrated by a bitter Salieri, and Lin-Manuel Miranda's *Hamilton* (2015) casts the founders with actors of colour, asking who gets to tell national history.

### The memory play: the past as felt

In his production notes, Williams wrote that *The Glass Menagerie*, being a memory play, could be staged "with unusual freedom of convention": dim lighting, music, projected titles, nothing naturalistic. Tom, the narrator, steps out of the action to tell us the play is memory, and that he gives "truth in the pleasant disguise of illusion."

The memory play's promise is **subjectivity**. We see the past as someone remembers it: certain scenes magnified, others missing, the remembered people slightly unreal. Its structure is a frame (the narrator now) around episodes (then), with a final turn back to the narrator, who can't escape what he's shown us. Tom's last speech, as Laura blows out her candles, is the model. Brian Friel's *Dancing at Lughnasa* (1990) uses a grown son to recall one summer, and Paula Vogel's *How I Learned to Drive* (1997) (see *Paula Vogel*) scrambles memory's order to approach what's hardest to say.

### Documentary and verbatim theatre

Documentary theatre puts real events and real words onstage. Peter Weiss's *The Investigation* (1965) drew on the Frankfurt Auschwitz trial. **Verbatim** theatre narrows the rule: the words are what real people said, usually in interviews.

**Anna Deavere Smith** made it a solo art. In *Fires in the Mirror* (1992), about the 1991 Crown Heights riots in Brooklyn, she performed some two dozen people she had interviewed, catching each one's exact rhythms, hesitations included. Moisés Kaufman and the Tectonic Theater Project's *The Laramie Project* (2000) came from hundreds of interviews in Laramie, Wyoming, after the murder of Matthew Shepard, and the company members appear as characters. **Alecky Blythe** has her actors wear earpieces and repeat the recorded interviews exactly as they hear them, every "um" intact; *London Road* (2011) set such speech to music.

The promise is **testimony**: you're hearing what was really said. That makes the editing the art and the ethics (compare *Janet Malcolm & John Hersey*): selection and order build an argument the speakers never made.

### The musical's book

A musical's **book** is its script and structure. In the integrated musical, a model often dated to *Oklahoma!* (1943), songs move the story. Early on, the hero usually sings the **"I want" song**, stating the desire that drives the show: "Wouldn't It Be Loverly" in *My Fair Lady*, "Part of Your World" in *The Little Mermaid*. It's the want of *Want, need and wound*, set to music.

### The monologue play

One speaker, one audience. Beckett's *Krapp's Last Tape* (1958) splits it into an old man and his recorded younger self; Phoebe Waller-Bridge's *Fleabag* (2013) began as one. The key choice is **who the audience is** to the speaker: confessor, jury, friend. Its structure is usually a story told with an avoidance at its centre, which the speaker finally reaches.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Choose the form for its promise. Need the audience to trust the words? Verbatim. Want the past distorted by feeling? A memory frame. Moral stakes made loud? Melodrama, used knowingly.
- **Screen:** Melodrama runs through film and soap opera; the memory frame becomes voice-over and flashback; the biopic is the history play's heir, with the same right to compress and the same duty to say so.
- **Journalism:** Verbatim theatre and oral history share methods. If you adapt interviews for the stage or audio, keep a record of every cut and reordering.

## The pushback

- **"Melodramatic" as an insult.** The word still means overwrought, and critics have long treated the form as lowbrow. Brooks's defence persuaded many scholars, but not every reader.
- **History's licence.** Every history play distorts. *Amadeus*'s idea that Salieri murdered Mozart is rumour, not fact. Audiences often remember the play, not the record.
- **Verbatim isn't neutral.** Real words, cut and arranged, can mislead as well as any invented script.

## Try it
Take a scene you've written, or one from a play you know, and recast it in two forms from this lesson.
1. As a memory play: add a narrator, looking back from years later, who opens the scene and interrupts it once. What does memory exaggerate, and what does it leave out?
2. As melodrama: make every character say exactly what they feel, and give the moral turn one silent gesture or tableau.
3. Compare the two drafts with the original. Which promise (felt truth, moral clarity, plain realism) does your material want?

- **Stage:** Try a verbatim version too: interview someone for ten minutes about the scene's subject, and use only their words.
- **Screen:** Rewrite the memory version with voice-over, and notice what the camera must do that the narrator once did.
- **Journalism:** Rewrite a news event as a short history-play scene, then list every invention you made.

**Check:** Can you name what each form let you show that the others couldn't?

## Check yourself

Q: What does farce promise its audience?
A: Acceleration: flat characters driven by one appetite or fear, secrets piling up, and pressure rising until everything collides.

Q: What's Peter Brooks's "moral occult"?
A: The hidden moral truths that melodrama drags into the open. After the old sacred order collapsed, melodrama made good and evil legible, played out loud on bodies and faces.

Q: What makes *The Glass Menagerie* a memory play?
A: A narrator, Tom, presents the past as he remembers it, so the staging can be non-realistic: dim light, music, magnified moments, and a frame of the present around it.

Q: What's the ethical risk in verbatim theatre?
A: The words are real, but selection and order build an argument the speakers may never have made.

Q: What is the "I want" song?
A: A song early in a musical in which the protagonist states the desire that drives the show, like "Part of Your World."
