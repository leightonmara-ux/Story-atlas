## Who and when

Lynn Nottage (born 1964) is a Brooklyn-born playwright who studied at Brown University, where *Paula Vogel* was among her teachers, and at the Yale School of Drama. She worked for Amnesty International before writing full time, and her early plays include *Crumbs from the Table of Joy* (1995) and *Intimate Apparel* (2003).

Two plays made her a model for research-driven drama. *Ruined* won the Pulitzer Prize for Drama in 2009, and *Sweat* won it in 2017, making her the first woman to win the drama prize twice. Both grew out of long periods of interviewing real people. Neither is verbatim theatre. That's what makes her useful: she shows how to turn listening into an invented play that still feels owed to the people who talked to you.

## The big ideas

### Listen first, then invent

*Stage forms* covers **verbatim theatre**, where every word onstage is something a real person said: Anna Deavere Smith, *The Laramie Project*, Alecky Blythe. Nottage does something different. She interviews for months or years, then writes fiction. Talking to *The Interval* in 2015 about *Sweat*, she said the characters were purely fictional but inspired by people she met, and that this gave her a freedom she wouldn't have had in a verbatim piece. Elsewhere she has described building **composites** out of several people she met.

The trade-off is worth naming. Verbatim theatre earns trust by fidelity to exact words. Nottage's method earns it by fidelity to experience. She keeps the textures she heard (jobs, money worries, how people talk about their town and each other), but she's free to compress years into a few scenes, invent a plot with a climax, and give characters secrets nobody would confess on tape.

### *Ruined*: when the source play didn't fit

*Ruined* began as an idea to adapt Bertolt Brecht's *Mother Courage and Her Children* (1939), a war play about a woman who profits from the fighting, to the wars in the Democratic Republic of Congo. In 2004 Nottage traveled to Uganda with the director Kate Whoriskey to interview Congolese women who had fled the conflict, many of whom had survived sexual violence used as a weapon of war.

The interviews changed the plan. Nottage has said that adapting Brecht wouldn't be enough: the women's stories didn't fit a European frame built around a single heroine. She kept a trace of *Mother Courage* in Mama Nadi, who runs a bar and brothel in a mining town and tries to stay neutral and in business while armies on both sides drink there. But the play centers on the young women who work for her, especially Sophie and Salima, and on what the war has done to them.

The craft lesson: research can overturn your premise. Go in with a frame if you need one, and be ready to drop it when the material won't fit.

### *Sweat*: two years in one town

For *Sweat*, Nottage looked for a place to write about American economic decline, and chose Reading, Pennsylvania, which the 2010 census had shown to be one of the poorest cities in the country. From 2011 she and Whoriskey made repeated trips there, interviewing a wide range of people. Accounts of how long it lasted range from about two years to nearly three; Nottage herself has spoken of two and a half years of going back and forth and listening.

One encounter, she told *Slate* (2016), changed the play: a group of steelworkers who had been locked out of their plant for 93 weeks. She has said that it was there she first felt real empathy for them, and the play's emotional center grew from it.

The finished play is invented. It's set mostly in a bar where friends who work at the same factory drink after shifts. Scenes in 2008, in which two young men, Jason and Chris, are just out of prison, frame a long run of scenes in 2000, as a lockout and the move of jobs abroad pull the group apart. Cynthia's promotion into management sets her against her best friend Tracey, and the bar's Colombian American barback, Oscar, takes a job crossing the picket line. The script ties scenes to the news of their moment, so private quarrels play against the national economy.

### Structure that builds pity, not just suspense

Opening in 2008 with Jason and Chris on parole gives the audience a piece of dramatic irony: we know something terrible happened, and we watch the years before it, as the friends make choices whose end we already half-know. The climax in 2000 is a violent fight in the bar. *Story vs. telling* explains the general device; Nottage's use of it is moral as well as structural. Knowing the outcome lets us stop asking *what happens* and start asking *how did these people get here*, which is the question her research was about.

### Dignity on the page

Nottage has said that the message of *Sweat* is that workers deserve to be treated with dignity, and in the *Slate* interview she was wary of what is often called "poverty porn": work that displays hardship for an audience's pity or thrill. In practice, dignity in *Sweat* comes from craft choices. The characters are funny and sharp. They argue about politics and money with real knowledge. Their worst actions grow from understandable fears, not from ignorance. Nobody exists only to suffer.

She has also described choosing music to match the mood of Reading and of individual characters while writing (*The Interval*, 2016), a way to stay inside the world between interviews.

### Giving the story back

Nottage didn't stop at the play. In 2017 she and Whoriskey made *This Is Reading*, a multimedia performance installation in Reading's long-empty Franklin Street railroad station, drawn from the interviews and performed with local residents among the cast. It turned the research back toward the community that provided it. She later returned to the world of *Sweat* in *Clyde's* (2021), set in a truck-stop sandwich shop near Reading whose cooks are formerly incarcerated.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Interview first, but decide early whether you're making verbatim theatre or fiction, and tell the people you interview which it is. Nottage has said she told the people she met that she was a storyteller.
- **Screen:** Films "inspired by" a community work the same way. Use interviews for texture: jobs, routines, jokes, the words people use for their own town. Then build a plot freely and keep composites honest.
- **Journalism:** The reporting is identical: long immersion, return visits, listening without an agenda. The difference is the output. A journalist can't make composites or invent a climax (see *Janet Malcolm & John Hersey*). But Nottage's patience, and her rule that nobody exists only to suffer, apply directly to writing about poor and working-class people.

## The pushback

- **Who speaks for whom?** An American playwright turning Congolese women's testimony into theatre for mostly Western audiences raises questions *Who gets to be the subject* explores. Nottage's defense is in her process: years of listening, and plays that center the women's own agency.
- **Endings.** Some critics found *Ruined*'s ending, a tentative romance for Mama Nadi, too consoling after what came before. Others read it as refusing to make the women only victims.
- **Fiction's freedom cuts both ways.** Because *Sweat* is invented, it can't be fact-checked like journalism, yet audiences often take it as a portrait of Reading.
- **The Trump frame.** *Sweat* reached Broadway in 2017 and was widely billed as explaining the 2016 election. The play was researched and written years before, and reading it only that way narrows it.

## Try it

Interview one person for 20 minutes about their work: what they do all day, what's changed, what they fear.

1. Write down five things you'd never have invented: a phrase, a routine, a joke, an object, a grievance.
2. Invent a character who is *not* that person, and write a one-page scene in which they want something urgently, using at least three of the five.
3. Read it back and ask whether the character has humor, knowledge and choices, or only hardship.

- **Stage:** set the scene in a shared space like a bar or break room, with a second character whose interests clash with the first.
- **Screen:** write it as a scene whose action is their actual work, done while they argue.
- **Journalism:** keep the real person, but write a scene from your notes that shows their expertise, not just their trouble.

**Check:** Would the person you interviewed recognize their world, and feel respected, without recognizing themselves?

## Check yourself

Q: How does Nottage's method differ from verbatim theatre?
A: Verbatim theatre uses only what real people said. Nottage interviews at length but writes fictional characters and plots, often composites, keeping fidelity to experience rather than to exact words.

Q: Why did *Ruined* stop being an adaptation of *Mother Courage*?
A: Interviews with Congolese women in Uganda convinced Nottage that Brecht's frame couldn't hold their stories; she kept an echo of it in Mama Nadi but centered the women.

Q: What does *Sweat*'s 2008 frame do for the audience?
A: It creates dramatic irony: we know something went badly wrong, so we watch the 2000 scenes asking how these people got there.

Q: What does writing working-class characters with dignity mean in practice?
A: Giving them humor, knowledge, arguments and choices, so that no one exists only to suffer or to be pitied.

Q: What was *This Is Reading*?
A: A 2017 multimedia installation in Reading's old railroad station, made from the *Sweat* interviews with local residents among the performers, giving the story back to the community.
