## Who and when

John Yorke spent most of his career commissioning and producing British television drama. He ran *EastEnders*, was head of drama at Channel 4, held senior drama posts at the BBC, and founded the BBC Writers Academy, which trains new writers on long-running shows. He has watched thousands of scripts succeed and fail, often on a weekly deadline.

*Into the Woods* came out in Britain in 2013 with the subtitle *A Five-Act Journey into Story*; the American edition (2014) is subtitled *How Stories Work and Why We Tell Them*. It's an ambitious book. Yorke reads the screenwriting manuals, Campbell, Freytag and Shakespeare side by side and asks a simple question: if all these models look so alike, what is the single shape underneath them, and *why* do we tell stories in that shape at all?

## The big ideas

### All stories are journeys into the woods

Yorke's governing image comes from fairy tales. A character lives in a familiar world, but something in them, or in their life, is lacking. They're pushed (or they leap) into the woods: an unfamiliar, dangerous place. There they confront what they've been missing. Then they come out again, changed.

The woods can be literal (a jungle, a war, outer space) or not (a new job, a love affair, a crime investigation). What matters is the movement: out of the known, into the unknown, and back with something learned.

### Change is the point

For Yorke, structure exists to dramatize **change**. A protagonist begins with a flaw, a lack or a false belief. The story confronts them with its opposite, and they have to absorb it. The cynic learns to trust, the coward finds courage, the controlling parent learns to let go. Or, in tragedy, they refuse, and are destroyed.

This is why he cares so much about the protagonist's inner journey matching the outer plot. The events of the story are the pressure; the change is the payoff.

### Five acts, not three

{{diagram:yorke}}

Yorke argues that the familiar three-act model is real but too coarse. Its second act is a long, shapeless middle. Divide that middle at two further turning points and you get **five acts**, the shape used by Renaissance dramatists and described by *Gustav Freytag*.

Roughly:

1. **Act One:** the world and the flaw, then the call to action, the inciting incident.
2. **Act Two:** first steps into the woods. Early progress, learning the rules, doubts overcome.
3. **Act Three:** the deepest part of the woods. At its center sits the **midpoint**.
4. **Act Four:** things begin to fall apart. Opposition mounts, the old self fights back, and the act ends in crisis, often the worst point.
5. **Act Five:** the final confrontation, and the way out of the woods.

He's clear that five acts are a refinement of three, not a rival. Five-act thinking is simply a more detailed map of the same ground.

### The midpoint

The midpoint is the hinge of Yorke's model. It's the moment, roughly halfway, when the protagonist reaches the heart of the woods. Often they get a glimpse of the truth they need, or win something that looks like what they wanted and turns out not to be enough. Either way, the direction of the story shifts: the first half moves toward something, the second half deals with the consequences.

Yorke also notices a **symmetry** around this hinge. The fourth act tends to mirror the second, and the fifth mirrors the first: lessons learned on the way in are tested on the way out, and the ending answers the opening.

### Fractal structure

Yorke's most original claim is that story structure is **fractal**: the same shape repeats at every scale. A whole story has a problem, a journey, a crisis and a resolution. So does each act. So does each scene, which has its own small inciting moment, its own escalation and turn. Even a single exchange of dialogue can carry the pattern.

For a working writer, this is practical. If a scene feels dead, ask whether it has the shape in miniature: a want, a push into difficulty, a turn. If an act sags, ask the same question one level up.

### Why we tell stories

The book ends where most manuals don't start: with the question of why the shape exists. Yorke argues that storytelling mirrors the way human minds make sense of experience. We meet something unfamiliar, struggle with it, and fold it into what we know. A story is that process acted out, which is why audiences find it satisfying and why the shape recurs across cultures he examines.

## Use it

- **Screen:** If your Act Two sags, try Yorke's two extra act breaks. Find a midpoint that changes the direction of the story, not just a set piece. In television, his fractal idea applies to the episode, the season and the whole series.
- **Stage:** The five-act shape is native to the theater, from Shakespeare to Ibsen. Even in a two-act play, the interval often sits at or near a Yorke-style midpoint.
- **Journalism:** The "into the woods" journey is a good test for narrative nonfiction: what familiar world did your subject leave, what did they find there, and what did they bring back? If you can't answer the last question, the story may not have resolved yet.

## The pushback

- **"All stories are the same" is a big claim.** Critics point out that the universal pattern is easier to find when you're looking for it. Ambiguous or open-ended work has to be stretched to fit, and some readers find his act divisions of famous films arbitrary.
- **More synthesis than discovery.** Much of the book restates Freytag, Campbell, Vogler and McKee. Yorke is candid about his sources; his own contributions are the fractal idea, the emphasis on symmetry and the attempt to explain *why*.
- **The five-act history is messier than it looks.** Five acts were a Roman and Renaissance convention, but many act divisions in printed Shakespeare were added by later editors.
- **Change isn't always the point.** Some of the best television characters, and many comic heroes, barely change at all; the world around them does.

## Check yourself

Q: What is Yorke's "journey into the woods"?
A: The archetypal story movement: a character leaves a familiar world for an unfamiliar, dangerous one, confronts what they lack, and returns changed.

Q: How does Yorke relate five-act structure to three-act structure?
A: Five acts are a refinement of three. Adding two turning points inside the long second act splits it into three, giving a more detailed map.

Q: What typically happens at Yorke's midpoint?
A: The protagonist reaches the heart of the woods, often glimpsing a crucial truth or winning something that proves insufficient, and the story changes direction.

Q: What does Yorke mean by calling structure "fractal"?
A: The same shape repeats at every scale: story, act, scene, even an exchange. Each has its own problem, escalation and turn.

Q: Which acts mirror each other in Yorke's account of symmetry?
A: The fourth mirrors the second and the fifth mirrors the first, around the midpoint in Act Three.
