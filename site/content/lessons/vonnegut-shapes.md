## Who and when

**Kurt Vonnegut** (1922–2007) was the American novelist of *Cat's Cradle* (1963) and *Slaughterhouse-Five* (1969). After serving in the Second World War, and surviving the firebombing of Dresden as a prisoner of war, he studied anthropology at the University of Chicago.

Vonnegut liked to say that his best contribution to culture was a master's thesis the university turned down: a theory that stories have shapes you can draw on graph paper. He tells it in his collection ***Palm Sunday*** (1981) and again, with hand-drawn graphs, in ***A Man Without a Country*** (2005). The way he tells it, the anthropology faculty rejected the idea because it looked too simple and too much fun.

The paper trail is messier than the anecdote. He left Chicago in 1947 without a degree, and accounts differ over which thesis proposal was rejected, when, and why. The university later awarded him the master's degree, in 1971 by most accounts, accepting *Cat's Cradle* in place of a thesis.

The idea reached most people through a short lecture clip, widely shared online, of Vonnegut at a blackboard drawing the curves and getting big laughs. It's a performance, and worth watching as one: he's a comedian teaching with a piece of chalk.

## The big ideas

### Two axes

Draw a horizontal line: the **beginning–end axis**, time running from the first page to the last. Draw a vertical line through its start: the **good–ill fortune axis**, with good fortune at the top, misery at the bottom, and ordinary life somewhere around the middle. A story becomes a line moving through that space, rising when things go well for the main character and dropping when they go badly.

That's the whole method. Vonnegut's claim is that once you plot stories this way, a few shapes keep turning up, and some of them are reliably loved.

### Man in a Hole

{{diagram:vonnegut-shapes}}

Somebody gets into trouble, gets out of it again, and ends up better off than before. The line starts a little above the middle, plunges, and climbs back past where it began. Vonnegut joked that people never tire of this one. Think of almost any survival story, from a rescue at sea to *The Martian* (2015): a stranded astronaut, a long climb out, an ending in a better place than the start.

### Boy Meets Girl

It needn't be about a boy or a girl. Someone ordinary, on an ordinary day, comes upon something wonderful; loses it; and gets it back for good. The line rises, falls and rises again. It's the skeleton of most romantic comedy, but also of any story about a job, a friend or a home found, lost and regained.

### Cinderella

The shape Vonnegut spends longest on. The line starts at the bottom: a girl whose mother has died and whose stepfamily treats her as a servant. Then it climbs in steps, each gift from the fairy godmother lifting her higher, to the ball. At midnight it drops, but not as low as before: she's had the night. Then the slipper fits and the line shoots up off the top of the graph into lasting happiness. It's the shape of countless stories, and Vonnegut clearly relished it.

### The Kafka shape

Now start low: a young man who isn't attractive or well liked, stuck in a dull job that barely pays. That's Gregor Samsa in Kafka's *The Metamorphosis* (1915). He wakes up as a giant insect, and the line goes down from there, off the bottom of the graph. Vonnegut used it to show that a story can be famous and great without the climb back.

### Hamlet: the flat line

His best joke is also his most serious point. Hamlet starts in a bad place, like Cinderella: his father dead, his mother remarried to his uncle. Then a ghost tells him his uncle was the murderer. Good news or bad? We can't tell. Each event in the play is like that: we don't know where to plot it. So the line stays flat.

Vonnegut's conclusion: *Hamlet* is a masterpiece partly because Shakespeare doesn't pretend to know what's good news and what's bad. Real life rarely does either. Simple shapes are popular; the greatest works may refuse to have one.

### The computers arrive

Vonnegut half-joked that the shapes of stories were simple enough to feed into a computer. In 2016, a team at the University of Vermont's Computational Story Lab, Andrew Reagan, Lewis Mitchell, Dilan Kiley, Christopher Danforth and Peter Sheridan Dodds, tried it. Their paper, "The emotional arcs of stories are dominated by six basic shapes," appeared in *EPJ Data Science*.

They took a large collection of English-language fiction from Project Gutenberg (a filtered set of 1,327 works in the final version of the paper; an earlier draft counted 1,737), scored the emotional tone of the words in a sliding window through each book, and looked for the patterns that recur. They found six:

| Arc | Shape |
|---|---|
| Rags to riches | a steady rise |
| Riches to rags | a steady fall |
| Man in a hole | fall, then rise |
| Icarus | rise, then fall |
| Cinderella | rise, fall, rise |
| Oedipus | fall, rise, fall |

They also checked the shapes against how often each book had been downloaded. The most popular arcs, by that measure, were Icarus, Oedipus, and a double Man in a Hole: fall, rise, fall again, rise again.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Plot your play scene by scene on the two axes. If a long run of scenes sits at the same height, your audience may feel the evening sag. *Hamlet* is the exception that proves the rule: its fortunes are uncertain but its tension never stops climbing.
- **Screen:** Feature films love the double hole. A protagonist climbs out, seems safe, and drops again before the final climb. If your second act is one long climb, look for the second fall.
- **Journalism:** Many features are a Man in a Hole: someone's ordinary life, a crisis, a way out. Plot your subject's fortunes honestly before writing. If the real line ends at the bottom, don't bend it upward for a satisfying finish.

## The pushback

- **Words aren't fortune.** The Vermont study measured how happy or sad the *words* are, not what happens to the hero. A happy funeral speech and a cheerful villain both read as "up". Irony, which says one thing and means another, defeats it.
- **Smoothing makes shapes.** Turning a noisy line into a clean curve can create bumps that aren't there. When Matthew Jockers released a similar tool, Syuzhet, in 2015, the scholar Annie Swafford showed that its smoothing could produce misleading arcs. Critics have raised similar questions about how robust the six shapes are.
- **An old, narrow library.** Project Gutenberg is mostly older, public-domain English-language books. Downloads measure what people click today, not what readers loved when the books came out.
- **Shape isn't story.** Vonnegut himself showed this with *Hamlet*. A graph tells you the rhythm of fortune, not why we care. Compare *Christopher Booker*, who builds his plots on meaning rather than mood, and *Northrop Frye*, whose four mythoi are also shapes of rise and fall.
- **Remember the performance.** Vonnegut was making a serious point in a comic form. The lecture is not a research finding, and the University of Chicago anecdote is a story he enjoyed telling.

## Try it
Take your work-in-progress, or a film you know well. On a sheet of paper, draw the two axes.
1. List the eight to twelve biggest events in order along the bottom.
2. For each, mark how the protagonist's fortune stands afterwards: higher, lower, or level. Join the dots.
3. Name the shape. Is it one of Vonnegut's, or one of the six?
4. Find the longest flat stretch. Either add a rise or fall there, or decide, as Shakespeare did, that the uncertainty itself is the point, and make sure the tension still climbs.

- **Stage:** Plot each scene separately, then the whole play. Do the scenes have shapes too?
- **Screen:** Check whether the line falls a second time before the climax. If not, try the double hole.
- **Journalism:** Plot your subject's real fortunes from your notes, then plot your draft's order. Does your structure tell the truth about the line?

**Check:** Could you draw your story's shape in five seconds on a napkin, and would someone else draw the same one?

## Check yourself

Q: What are Vonnegut's two axes?
A: The beginning–end axis (time, from first page to last) and the good–ill fortune axis (how well or badly things are going for the main character).

Q: Describe the Man in a Hole shape.
A: Someone gets into trouble, gets out of it, and ends up better off than at the start: a fall followed by a rise above the starting point.

Q: Why does Vonnegut draw *Hamlet* as a flat line?
A: Because we can never tell whether its events are good news or bad, and he admired Shakespeare for being honest that we don't know.

Q: What were the six arcs found in the 2016 Vermont study?
A: Rags to riches, riches to rags, man in a hole, Icarus (rise then fall), Cinderella (rise, fall, rise) and Oedipus (fall, rise, fall).

Q: What's one important limit of that study?
A: It measures the emotional tone of words, not what happens to characters, so irony and contrast can mislead it; its smoothing method and its corpus of older Gutenberg books are also open to question.
