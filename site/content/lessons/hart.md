## Who and when

Jack Hart spent decades at *The Oregonian* in Portland, where he was managing editor and the paper's writing coach. He edited reporters whose narrative work won and was shortlisted for Pulitzer Prizes, and he taught narrative writing to newsrooms and at conferences across the United States.

*Storycraft: The Complete Guide to Writing Narrative Nonfiction* (2011; second edition 2021) collects what he learned. It's the most complete practical handbook in the field, with chapters on story, structure, point of view, voice, character, scene, action, dialogue, theme, reporting and ethics. The second edition adds newer forms such as podcasts.

Hart is open about his debt to *Jon Franklin*. Franklin's *Writing for Story* is the foundation; Hart builds a bigger house on it.

## The big ideas

### Story, and narrative more broadly

Hart separates two words that most people use interchangeably.

A **story**, in the full sense, has a protagonist, a complication and a resolution. That's Franklin's definition, and Hart keeps it.

**Narrative** is wider: any writing that moves through time with scenes and characters. Every story is a narrative, but not every narrative is a story. Some of the most useful forms in journalism are narratives *without* a full dramatic arc.

This distinction frees reporters. You don't have to abandon narrative techniques just because reality hasn't supplied a tidy ending.

### The narrative arc

{{diagram:hart}}

For full story narratives, Hart uses a version of the classic arc:

1. **Exposition:** we meet the protagonist and the world, and the complication arrives.
2. **Rising action:** the protagonist struggles with the complication through a series of **plot points**, developments that turn the story in a new direction.
3. **Crisis:** the struggle comes to a head.
4. **Climax:** the complication is resolved, often through what Hart calls a **point of insight**, when the protagonist sees their situation in a new way and can finally act on it.
5. **Falling action:** the loose ends are tied up, the denouement.

This is Franklin's complication–developments–resolution outline, filled in. The emphasis on insight comes from Franklin too: in true stories, change is often a change of understanding.

One of Hart's most practical tools is the **plot-point list**: before writing, list the developments that move the story, in order. It does for a long narrative what Franklin's short outline does for a shorter one.

### Story narratives, explanatory narratives and other forms

Hart sorts nonfiction narrative into families.

- **Story narratives** follow the full arc, like a novel or a feature film.
- **Explanatory narratives** use a narrative line, a person doing something over time, to carry the reader through an explanation. The story stops regularly for digressions into history, science or context, then picks up again. Hart associates this with classic *New Yorker* nonfiction. It doesn't need a dramatic resolution, only a line of action strong enough to carry the ideas.
- **Other forms** include the vignette or slice of life (a scene that shows rather than resolves), the profile, the narrative essay and, in the second edition, podcasts.

The practical point: choose the form the material supports. If there's no resolution, you might have a great explanatory narrative instead of a weak story narrative.

### Point of view and distance

Hart treats **point of view** as a decision you make on purpose, not a default. Whose eyes are we seeing through? First person, third person, or a narrator who moves freely?

He also pays attention to **distance**: how close the narrator stands to the action, from a distant, panoramic view that summarizes, to a close view inside a single scene. Like a film camera, a good narrative moves between them, pulling back to explain, moving in to dramatize.

### Scene and summary

Narratives alternate between **scene**, events shown moment by moment in one place and time, and **summary**, which covers stretches of time quickly. Scenes slow time down and make the reader feel present; summary keeps the story moving. Hart's advice is to save full scenes for the moments that matter most, usually the plot points, and to report them in enough detail to reconstruct them honestly.

### Reporting for story

Hart insists you can't write a scene you didn't report. Narrative reporting means gathering things news reporting skips: what the room looked like, who said what in what order, what someone was thinking (asked directly, not guessed). His chapter on ethics follows from this: the more a piece reads like fiction, the more it depends on the reader's trust that nothing was invented.

## Use it

- **Journalism:** Before committing to a narrative, ask which kind you have. A resolution? Write a story narrative and list your plot points. No resolution, but a strong line of action? Try an explanatory narrative. Neither? Perhaps a vignette, or a conventional feature.
- **Screen:** Documentary editors make the same choice between a character-driven arc and an explanatory structure carried by a central figure. The idea of distance, panorama versus close-up, translates directly into shot choices.
- **Stage:** Plot-point listing is a useful outline tool for any play. The point-of-insight idea fits drama where the turn is a recognition, which Aristotle would recognize (see *Aristotle*).

## The pushback

- **Still built on the arc.** Writers drawn to the *Counterpoints* track will notice that Hart, like Franklin, treats the classical arc as the gold standard, even while making room for other forms.
- **The newspaper narrative style.** Critics of narrative journalism argue that the techniques Hart teaches can turn every subject into a human-interest story, with a protagonist and an epiphany, even where the important story is about systems or data.
- **Reconstruction is risky.** Hart is careful on ethics, but the narrative techniques he teaches, especially interior thought and recreated scenes, are exactly the ones critics of narrative journalism worry about (see *Janet Malcolm & John Hersey*).
- **It's a handbook.** Its strength, breadth, is also its limit. It offers a great many tools and few deep arguments about what story is.

## Check yourself

Q: How does Hart distinguish a story from a narrative?
A: A story has a full arc: protagonist, complication and resolution. Narrative is any writing that moves through time with scenes and characters, so every story is a narrative but not every narrative is a story.

Q: What is an explanatory narrative?
A: A piece that uses a line of action to carry the reader through an explanation, pausing for digressions. It doesn't need a dramatic resolution.

Q: What is the "point of insight"?
A: The moment the protagonist sees their situation in a new way, which lets them resolve the complication. Hart inherits the emphasis on insight from Franklin.

Q: What is a plot-point list for?
A: Planning a narrative: listing, in order, the developments that turn the story in a new direction before you start writing.

Q: Why does Hart want writers to alternate scene and summary?
A: Scenes slow time and make the reader feel present, so they're saved for the moments that matter most; summary covers the stretches in between and keeps the story moving.
