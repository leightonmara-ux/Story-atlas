## Who and when

George Saunders is an American short-story writer, the author of *CivilWarLand in Bad Decline*, *Tenth of December* and the novel *Lincoln in the Bardo*, which won the Booker Prize in 2017. He has taught in the creative writing program at Syracuse University since the late 1990s, and for years ran a class there on the 19th-century Russian short story.

***A Swim in a Pond in the Rain*** (2021) turns that class into a book. It reprints seven stories in translation, three by Chekhov ("In the Cart", "The Darling", "Gooseberries"), two by Tolstoy ("Master and Man", "Alyosha the Pot"), one by Turgenev ("The Singers") and one by Gogol ("The Nose"), each followed by an essay on how it works. The title comes from a scene in "Gooseberries". He has continued the project in a Substack newsletter, *Story Club*.

What makes it unusual among craft books is that it isn't about structure in the abstract. It's about **the reader's mind, moment by moment**.

## The big ideas

### A page at a time

The book opens with Chekhov's "In the Cart", presented one page at a time. After each page Saunders stops and asks: what has just happened to us? What do we know now? What do we expect? What are we curious about?

This is the core of his method. A story isn't a diagram; it's an experience that happens in time, in a particular order, to a reader who is constantly forming expectations. Structure, for Saunders, is simply the sequence of those expectations being raised, met, frustrated and redirected.

### What keeps a reader reading

Saunders keeps returning to a deceptively simple question: why does a reader go on to the next sentence? His answers are practical. Something must be at stake. The reader must be asking a question. And each section should do something to the situation, so that the reader feels it has moved.

He's suspicious of anything that's merely decoration. If a passage isn't changing the reader's understanding or raising the stakes, the reader feels it, even if they can't say why, and the story loses its grip.

### Escalation

One of the book's central ideas is **escalation**. A story, Saunders argues, should be continually moving up a notch: each scene should raise, complicate or intensify what came before. When a story stalls, it's often because a scene is repeating a level the story has already reached.

This is close to McKee's "progressive complications" (see *Robert McKee*), but Saunders arrives at it from the reader's side. He doesn't ask "what does the protagonist want?" He asks "has this page made things *more*?"

### Pattern, and its breaking

Saunders is especially good on **pattern**. Once a story does something twice, the reader learns to expect it a third time. In Chekhov's "The Darling", a woman loves a series of people and each time takes on their opinions wholesale; we quickly learn the pattern and begin to anticipate it.

A pattern does two things. It gives the reader the pleasure of recognition, and it sets up the possibility of **variation**: the moment when the pattern bends or breaks, which is often where the story's meaning lands. The craft is in deciding how long to hold the pattern and how and when to break it.

### Stories that resist their own morals

Saunders loves the way these Russian stories refuse easy lessons. In "Gooseberries", a man delivers an impassioned speech about happiness and complacency, and the story lets his listeners remain unmoved. The story doesn't endorse or refute him; it lets the reader sit with both. For Saunders this openness is a moral achievement: it trusts the reader.

### Revision by instinct

Saunders describes his own revision method as a matter of tiny, intuitive choices. He has written about imagining a meter in his head, running from positive to negative, as he reads his draft: he notices where his attention flags and adjusts, over and over, without imposing a plan. Meaning, he argues, emerges from thousands of these small decisions, not from a theme decided in advance.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Do a page-at-a-time read of your script with a friend. After each page, ask: what do we know, what do we expect, what are we curious about? Any page that changes none of those is a problem.
- **Screen:** The escalation test is a good diagnostic for a sagging second act: is each sequence raising the level, or repeating it? Pattern-and-variation is also how running gags and recurring motifs earn their payoffs.
- **Journalism:** Readers of long features are exactly the "moment by moment" reader Saunders describes. Ask what question is in the reader's mind at each section break, and make sure each section escalates or answers it.

## The pushback

- **Reading in translation.** Saunders doesn't read Russian. His sentence-level observations rest on particular translators' choices, which some reviewers have noted.
- **Light on context.** The book reads the stories as craft objects, with relatively little about their history, politics or the writers' lives. That's a choice, but it leaves things out.
- **A workshop aesthetic.** The emphasis on efficiency, escalation and reader engagement reflects the contemporary American short story. Some stories, including ones in the book, work by lingering.
- **Tone.** Some readers love the warmth and moral earnestness; others find it homiletic.

## Read next

- George Saunders, *A Swim in a Pond in the Rain: In Which Four Russians Give a Master Class on Writing, Reading, and Life* (2021).
- Anton Chekhov, *Selected Stories*, in any good modern translation, to read the stories alongside Saunders's commentary.
- George Saunders, *Tenth of December* (2013), to see the principles at work in his own fiction.

## Try it

Take a story or script you're drafting and read it a page at a time, the way Saunders reads "In the Cart".

1. After each page, write three short lines: what we now know, what we expect, what we're curious about.
2. Mark any page where none of the three changed. That's where the story stalls.
3. Revise one stalled page so it escalates: it should raise, complicate or intensify what the page before reached.
4. Find something your story does twice. Write the third time as a variation that bends or breaks the pattern.

- **Stage:** do the page-by-page read aloud with a friend, stopping after each page to ask the three questions.
- **Screen:** apply the escalation test to each sequence of your second act: is it raising the level, or repeating it?
- **Journalism:** at each section break of a long feature, write down the question in the reader's mind, and check that the next section answers or sharpens it.

**Check:** Does every page make things *more*?

## Check yourself

Q: Why does Saunders present "In the Cart" one page at a time?
A: To make readers notice what each page does to them: what they now know, expect and are curious about. Structure is the sequence of those expectations.

Q: What does he mean by escalation?
A: Each section should raise, complicate or intensify the situation. A story stalls when a scene repeats a level it has already reached.

Q: How does pattern work in a story, according to Saunders?
A: Repetition teaches the reader to expect something; the meaning often lands when the pattern varies or breaks.

Q: How does Saunders's version of escalation differ from McKee's progressive complications?
A: McKee frames it around a protagonist's pursuit of a goal. Saunders frames it from the reader's side: has this page made things more?

Q: What's one limitation critics have noted?
A: He reads the stories in translation and with little historical context, so his close readings rest on translators' choices and a contemporary workshop aesthetic.
