## Where it comes from

The key word, **diegesis**, is ancient Greek for "narration". In the early 1950s the French philosopher Étienne Souriau and his "filmology" group revived it to mean the world of the story: everything that exists for the characters. The narratologist Gérard Genette took it up for literature in the 1970s. Film textbooks, notably David Bordwell and Kristin Thompson's *Film Art* (first edition 1979), made **diegetic** and **non-diegetic** sound standard classroom terms. In the industry, people tend to say **source** sound or music instead.

**Michel Chion** (born 1947), a French composer and critic, is the field's central thinker. *The Voice in Cinema* (1982) introduced his idea of the acousmêtre, and ***Audio-Vision: Sound on Screen*** (1990; English translation by Claudia Gorbman, 1994) gave us added value and synchresis.

**Walter Murch** (born 1943) is the practitioner everyone quotes. He is widely described as the first person to receive a "sound designer" credit, on Francis Ford Coppola's *Apocalypse Now* (1979). He is also a celebrated picture editor (see *Editing*).

## The big ideas

### Inside or outside the story

**Diegetic sound** comes from the story world: dialogue, a slammed door, a car radio, a band in the corner of the bar. The characters could hear it. **Non-diegetic sound** comes from outside: the orchestral score, a narrator's voice-over, a sound effect no one in the scene would notice. Only we hear it.

The difference changes what sound means. Non-diegetic music is commentary; it tells us how to feel from somewhere above the scene. Diegetic sound is evidence; it's part of what's happening, *now*, to these people.

Our ears read the difference from small cues. Diegetic sound has a place: it changes as the camera moves, echoes in a big room, goes tinny from a small speaker. Score usually floats free, full and clean.

### Crossing the line

The most expressive moments happen when sound changes sides. A song plays over a montage like ordinary score; then a character turns it off, and we realize it was on the car radio all along. Suddenly the music isn't the film talking to us. It's something these people were sitting inside, and so are we. That's the jolt into the "now": a montage becomes a scene happening in real time.

It works the other way too. A song starts on a jukebox, thin and in the room, then swells, loses its echo and carries on over the next scenes as score. A private moment has become the film's statement.

The famous joke on the device is in Mel Brooks's *Blazing Saddles* (1974): jazzy big-band music accompanies the sheriff riding across the desert, until he passes Count Basie and his orchestra, playing on the open prairie.

The musicologist Robynn Stilwell called this zone "the fantastical gap" (2007), and some scholars use **transdiegetic** for sound that crosses over.

### Sound anchors us in space and time

Diegetic sound pins us to a place. In Quentin Tarantino's *Reservoir Dogs* (1992), Mr. Blonde's torture scene plays to a song on the warehouse radio. When he walks out to his car, the music falls away and we hear the quiet outside; when he walks back in, the song is still playing. Tied to the room, it holds us in real time. (*Music and silence* looks at the mood.)

George Lucas's *American Graffiti* (1973) plays almost all its music from car radios. Murch re-recorded the songs through speakers in real spaces, which he calls **worldizing**, so they'd sound like part of the world.

### Sound bridges

A **sound bridge** carries sound across a cut. Editors name two shapes after how they look on an editing timeline:

- **J-cut:** the next scene's sound starts *before* its picture. We hear a train before we see it, and our attention is pulled forward.
- **L-cut:** the outgoing scene's sound continues *over* the next picture, so a conversation lingers while we look at its consequences.

In Hitchcock's *The 39 Steps* (1935), a landlady discovers a body and opens her mouth to scream, and the scream becomes the shriek of a train whistle as we cut to the hero's escape. *Apocalypse Now* opens with helicopter blades that turn into the ceiling fan over Willard's hotel bed: the war is inside his head.

### Offscreen sound and the acousmêtre

Offscreen sound widens the world beyond the frame, and frightens, because we hear a threat we can't see. Chion named a special case, the **acousmêtre**, a character who is heard but not seen, borrowing *acousmatic* from the composer Pierre Schaeffer. Such a voice seems to have powers: to be everywhere, to see everything, to know everything. Think of the Wizard in *The Wizard of Oz* (1939), booming until Toto pulls back the curtain. Chion's point is that showing the body attached to the voice, **de-acousmatization**, strips the power away.

### Added value and synchresis

Chion's **added value** is the enrichment sound gives an image, so naturally that we believe the meaning was in the picture all along. The same shot of a face plays as calm with birdsong and as dread with a low hum.

**Synchresis** (synchronism plus synthesis) is the brain's habit of welding any sound to any image that happens at the same moment. It's why foley artists can rebuild a world from objects in a studio: a punched cabbage becomes a punched face.

### Room tone, ambience and subjective sound

Every location has **room tone**, a near-silence of its own, recorded so editors can fill gaps without dead air. **Ambience** (traffic, wind, crowd murmur) tells us where we are and how it feels to be there.

**Point-of-view sound** puts us inside a character's ears. In *Saving Private Ryan* (1998), during the Omaha Beach landing, the battle goes muffled and distant around Captain Miller after an explosion. In *The Godfather* (1972), as Michael sits in the restaurant deciding whether to kill, the screech of an elevated train grows huge: it's out there in the world, but its volume is his pulse. For the image side of this, see *Point of view and voice*.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Theatre has always used offstage sound. *The Seagull* ends with an offstage shot that a doctor passes off as a bottle of ether bursting. *The Cherry Orchard*'s distant sound of a breaking string, which no one can place, hovers right on the line. Decide which side every cue sits on.
- **Screen:** Write sound into the script where it carries story ("the song is still playing on the car radio"). Plan transitions as J- or L-cuts. Where you want the audience pulled into real time, use diegetic sound.
- **Journalism:** In audio and video, ambient sound recorded on location (nat sound) is diegetic evidence; music under narration is non-diegetic commentary, and listeners should be able to tell which is which. See *Mood in journalism and audio*.

## The pushback

- **The binary leaks.** Is a character's imagined voice, or a song they hum along to with an invisible orchestra, inside or outside? Musicals blur the line constantly. Many scholars treat diegetic and non-diegetic as poles, not boxes.
- **Terms borrowed from literature.** Genette's diegesis was built for written narration, and some film theorists think it fits sound awkwardly.
- **Theory lags practice.** Most of these techniques were worked out by editors and mixers, not theorists. Chion's terms name what craftspeople were already doing.

## Try it
Take a scene from your draft, or the opening five minutes of a film you know, and make a sound list: every sound the audience hears, in order.
1. Mark each one D (diegetic) or N (non-diegetic), and note where each D sound comes from in space.
2. Find one moment where you want the audience pulled into the characters' "now". Move a piece of music across the line there: score that turns out to be a radio, or a radio that swells into score.
3. Rewrite the scene's entrance as a J-cut and its exit as an L-cut. What does each bridge connect?

- **Stage:** Add one offstage sound the characters react to but we never see the source of. How long can you keep it offstage?
- **Screen:** Watch your chosen five minutes with eyes closed. Can you tell where you are?
- **Journalism:** For an audio piece, list what was recorded on location and what was added. Is every added sound honest?

**Check:** For every sound, could you say who in the story can hear it?

## Check yourself

Q: What's the difference between diegetic and non-diegetic sound?
A: Diegetic sound comes from the story world and the characters could hear it (dialogue, a car radio); non-diegetic comes from outside it (score, voice-over) and only the audience hears it.

Q: Why does music that turns out to be on a car radio feel so immediate?
A: It stops being the film's commentary and becomes something the characters are living inside, so we're pulled into their moment.

Q: What's the difference between a J-cut and an L-cut?
A: In a J-cut the next scene's sound arrives before its picture; in an L-cut the previous scene's sound carries over the new picture.

Q: What is Chion's acousmêtre, and how does it lose its power?
A: A character who is heard but not seen, whose voice seems all-knowing and all-powerful. It loses that power when we finally see the body the voice belongs to.

Q: What is synchresis?
A: Chion's term for how the mind welds a sound and an image together when they happen at the same moment, even if they have nothing to do with each other.
