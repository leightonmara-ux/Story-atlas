## Where it comes from

**Lev Kuleshov** (1899–1970) was a young Russian filmmaker and teacher who, in the years after the 1917 revolution, ran a workshop in Moscow that studied what cinema could do that theatre couldn't. Film stock was scarce, so his group often worked by re-cutting existing footage. Around 1918–1921 (accounts disagree on the date) Kuleshov is said to have taken a close-up of the matinee idol **Ivan Mosjoukine** wearing a fairly neutral expression, and cut it against different images.

The best-known account comes from Kuleshov's student **Vsevolod Pudovkin**, who later described it in his writing and lectures on film. The face was intercut with a bowl of soup, a dead woman (in some versions, a child) in a coffin, and a little girl playing with a toy bear. Audiences, Pudovkin said, praised Mosjoukine's acting: his hunger over the soup, his grief at the coffin, his tender delight at the child. The face was the same shot every time.

That is the **Kuleshov effect**: the meaning of a shot depends on the shots around it.

Some caution belongs here. The original footage is lost. Kuleshov's and Pudovkin's descriptions of it vary from telling to telling (some versions have a child in the coffin, or an attractive woman in place of the girl), and Pudovkin later claimed a share in the experiment. It may have been closer to a classroom demonstration than a controlled test. Modern replications are mixed: Stephen Prince and Wayne Hensley recreated it in 1992 and found viewers mostly saw a neutral face, while later studies, such as one by Daniel Barratt and colleagues in 2016, found a real but more modest effect that varies by emotion.

The effect's most famous salesman was **Alfred Hitchcock**. In a 1964 interview with Fletcher Markle for the CBC programme *Telescope*, he demonstrated it with his own face: a man squints, we see a woman with a baby, he smiles, and he's a kindly old gentleman. Swap the baby for a woman in a bikini, and the same smile makes him, in Hitchcock's words, a "dirty old man." He gave Truffaut a similar version in their famous interviews.

## The big ideas

### The audience completes the shot

The core of the effect isn't a trick with faces. It's a claim about viewers: they can't help connecting two images. Put shot B after shot A and the audience assumes B is what A is looking at, thinking about, or caused by. They supply the emotion and the link themselves, and because they've done the work, the feeling is theirs.

That's why a neutral or ambiguous face often plays better than an "acted" one. A face that shows too much leaves the viewer nothing to do. *David Mamet*'s idea of uninflected images, plain shots that mean something only when cut together, is a direct descendant. (One correction worth making there: the face-and-soup example belongs to Kuleshov, not Eisenstein, though it's often credited to the wrong Soviet.)

### The reaction is often the scene

If meaning lives in the cut, then the most important shot in a dialogue scene is frequently not the person speaking but the person **listening**. The line tells us what's said; the reaction shot tells us what it *means*, and to whom.

Hitchcock built *Rear Window* (1954) on this. For most of the film, James Stewart's character sits at his window, and the pattern repeats: Stewart looks, we see what he sees across the courtyard, we cut back to his face. His expression is often small. The story of a possible murder is assembled in our heads from those three-shot units. Hitchcock told Truffaut the film was a demonstration of exactly this kind of pure cinema.

A bigger example: in *Jaws* (1975), when the boy is attacked on the crowded beach, the shot everyone remembers is Chief Brody's face as the camera pulls back while zooming in, so the background seems to rush toward him. We barely see the shark. We see what it does to the man who should have closed the beach.

### Close-ups and how much a face can hold

The close-up is what makes the Kuleshov effect possible: it isolates a face so completely that we read it as a mind. Credit for "inventing" the close-up often goes to D. W. Griffith, but that's a myth. Early British filmmakers such as G. A. Smith were cutting in to close views around 1900, and *The Great Train Robbery* (1903) has its famous close shot of a bandit firing straight at the audience. Griffith's contribution was to use close-ups for emotion within the flow of a story.

The extreme case is Carl Theodor Dreyer's *The Passion of Joan of Arc* (1928), told largely in close-ups of Renée Falconetti's face and her judges'. It shows both sides of the lesson: a face in close-up can carry a whole trial, and its meaning keeps shifting depending on whose face comes next.

### Eyelines tell us who's looking at what

The quiet workhorse behind the effect is the **eyeline match**: a character looks off-screen in one direction, and the next shot shows something positioned as the object of that look. Get the eyelines consistent and the audience builds a coherent space and a set of relationships, even from shots filmed on different days in different places. Get them wrong and characters seem to be talking past each other.

The eyeline match is part of the larger system of continuity editing, covered in *Editing*. Here the point is narrower: the direction of a look is a promise, and the next shot keeps it. Who gets to look, and who is only looked at, is a political question as well as a technical one (see *Who gets to be the subject*).

## Use it

- **Stage:** There's no cut, but the audience still reads faces in context. Stage the listener, not just the speaker: decide where the reaction to the key line happens and make sure the audience can see it. A character who hears news with their back to us, then turns, is a stage close-up.
- **Screen:** Write the reaction into the script. After a big line, give a short action line for the listener's response, or let the scene end on their silence. When planning shots, decide what each character looks at and show it, so the look-object-reaction pattern can do its work. Resist directing emotion onto faces in the stage directions; give the image that will make the face mean something.
- **Journalism:** In TV and video, the effect has an ethical edge. "Noddies," reaction shots of an interviewer nodding filmed after the interview and cut in, can make an answer seem to land differently. Cutting a subject's face against unrelated footage can imply a reaction they never had. In print, the equivalent is juxtaposition: placing a quote next to a detail lets readers draw a conclusion you haven't stated, so make sure it's one you could defend.

## The pushback

- **The founding experiment is shaky.** The footage is lost, the accounts disagree, and the cleanest modern replications find a weaker effect than the legend. It's safer to treat it as a well-observed tendency than a law.
- **Faces aren't blank slates.** Actors and audiences know the difference between a truly neutral face and a subtly shaded one. Good screen acting is often about giving the cut something specific but open, not nothing.
- **Context works both ways.** Strong music, a clear story situation and an established character shape how a face is read at least as much as the next image does.
- **The power can mislead.** Because the audience trusts its own inference, the effect is just as good at manipulation as at storytelling, a concern for documentary and news (see *Bill Nichols*).

## Try it
Pick a scene from your draft where a key line lands: a confession, a piece of bad news, an insult.
1. Rewrite it so the listener gets no dialogue for at least three beats after the line. Give them only a look and one small action.
2. Write the scene as shots: speaker, line, listener's face, then the thing the listener looks at, then the face again.
3. Try two versions of what they look at (a photo, the door, their own hands) and notice how each changes what the face means.

- **Stage:** Block the moment so the listener is downstage and visible; the speaker can be upstage or turned away.
- **Screen:** Watch the courtyard scenes in *Rear Window* with the sound off and note each look-object-reaction unit, then apply the pattern to your scene.
- **Journalism:** In a feature or video script, find a quote whose meaning depends on what sits next to it. Write two juxtapositions and ask which one you could defend to the source.

**Check:** Could the scene's meaning change if you swapped only the shot the listener is looking at?

## Check yourself

Q: What is the Kuleshov effect?
A: The tendency of viewers to read a shot's meaning from the shots around it, so the same neutral face seems hungry, grieving or tender depending on what it's cut against.

Q: Why should a writer be cautious about the original experiment?
A: The footage is lost, Kuleshov's and Pudovkin's accounts vary, and modern replications give mixed results, so it's a tendency rather than a proven law.

Q: How did Hitchcock demonstrate the effect in 1964?
A: In a CBC interview with Fletcher Markle, he showed his own smiling face cut against a woman with a baby (kindly) and a woman in a bikini ("dirty old man").

Q: Why does the reaction shot often matter more than the line?
A: The line says what's said; the listener's face shows what it means and what it costs, and the audience completes that meaning themselves.

Q: What is an eyeline match?
A: A cut from a character looking off-screen to a shot of what they're looking at, which builds a coherent space and links the look to its object.
