## Who and when

Jesse Armstrong (born 1970) grew up in Oswestry, Shropshire, and came up through British television comedy. With his writing partner Sam Bain he created *Peep Show* (Channel 4, 2003–2015), the sitcom shot from its two leads' points of view with their inner thoughts in voice-over, and later *Fresh Meat* (2011–2016). He wrote for Armando Iannucci's political satire *The Thick of It*, and shared an Oscar nomination for its film spin-off *In the Loop* (2009). He also co-wrote *Four Lions* (2010) with Chris Morris and Bain.

*Succession* ran on HBO for four seasons, from 2018 to 2023. It follows Logan Roy, the ageing founder of a media and entertainment conglomerate, and his children as they fight over who will take it from him. Armstrong created it and was its showrunner; Adam McKay directed the pilot and Mark Mylod directed many of the key episodes, including the last. Faber has published the complete scripts, with an introduction by Armstrong.

## The big ideas

### An ensemble with no hero

*The cast as a system* shows how *Succession* runs on variations of one want: every Roy child wants the company and their father's approval, and each goes after it with a different weapon. Armstrong's twist is that none of them is the hero. There's no one we're meant to want to win.

That's unusual. Most stories borrow our hopes through a protagonist (see *Whose side are we on?*). *Succession* invites us to care about people we wouldn't trust with a parking space. It manages this in three ways you can see on screen:

- **Shifting focus.** Episodes hand the centre to different characters. Kendall leads in one, Shiv in another, and even the family's hangers-on, Tom and cousin Greg, get their own arcs.
- **Rotating sympathy.** Whoever is losing becomes the one we feel for. Kendall, broken and humiliated after the first season's finale, is pitiable in season 2; once he seems to be winning, he's harder to like.
- **One father, many wounds.** Every child is shaped by the same man, so each is a different answer to the same question: what do you do with a parent who'll never be satisfied?

The ensemble also changes what suspense means. The question is never "will the hero win?" but "who will betray whom, and when?"

### Comedy played as tragedy

Armstrong has said in several interviews that he still thinks of himself as a comedy writer. *Succession* is full of jokes, but the actors don't play them as jokes. In a *Backstage* interview he explained that the cast has to play everything as real; the laughs come from people taking themselves deadly seriously.

That's the register to study. The comedy and the tragedy aren't separate scenes. They're the same scene seen from two distances (compare *Tragedy and comedy*). Up close, the rap Kendall performs in tribute to his father at a celebration in Logan's honour (season 2, episode 8, "Dundee") makes us wince with him; from a step back, it's one of the funniest things in the series.

### Dialogue as combat

*Dialogue reveals character* explains subtext: the real scene is in what people won't say. *Succession* turns that into a style. The Roys almost never say what they feel. They insult, deflect and score points instead, and every exchange is a contest for status.

Watch for three moves:

1. **The insult as affection.** Roman can't express love, fear or grief except as a crude joke. When he's cruel, look for what he's protecting.
2. **The deflection.** Asked a direct question, a Roy answers a different one, or changes the subject to someone else's weakness.
3. **The status test.** In season 2, episode 3 ("Hunting"), Logan suspects a traitor among his executives and, at a hunting lodge, makes them crawl on the floor and fight over sausages for his amusement, a game he calls "boar on the floor". It's a scene entirely about rank: who will humiliate themselves, and who will humiliate others to stay safe.

Tom and Greg are the clearest double act. Tom, insecure about his place in the family, bullies Greg, the one person beneath him; Greg absorbs it and quietly learns from it. Their scenes are comic, and they also map the whole show's pecking order.

### The room, the set and the "alts"

*Succession* is often described as improvised. Armstrong has said that's an exaggeration. The process, as he's described it in interviews over the show's run:

- **The room plans; it doesn't write.** At the BBC Comedy Festival in 2023 he said a writers' room isn't the place to write. The main room, in London, broke the stories and planned the season; scripts were then written and rewritten.
- **Rewriting to the last minute.** He told *The Guardian* (2023) that he kept refining scripts very late, and admitted this caused tension with actors who wanted to learn their lines.
- **Alts.** Shortly before shooting, he and the writers marked moments where a funnier or truer line might fit and wrote alternatives, or "alts": sometimes none for a scene, sometimes a dozen or more. Actors could try them in different takes.
- **The loose take.** He told *The Hollywood Reporter* (2019, after the season 2 finale) that they would shoot scripted takes, then looser ones, and take fragments from both into the edit. Improvisation lived inside a scripted structure, not instead of it.

He also urged writers, at the same 2023 festival, to be part of the conversation on set. For a writer, the lesson is that the script is the skeleton, and the alts are a way to keep testing which line hits hardest.

### Seasons that converge

Each season of *Succession* ends with a family occasion where the scattered schemes come to a head: a wedding at an English castle (season 1), a Mediterranean yacht trip (season 2), Logan's ex-wife's wedding in Tuscany (season 3), a board vote (season 4). The episode before often raises the public pressure: Congressional hearings in season 2, Logan's funeral in season 4.

Every finale turns on a betrayal or a reversal of power. Kendall's attempt to take the company ends with him in his father's grip; then he turns on his father at a press conference; then the siblings are sold out by Tom. The pattern matters because it gives an ensemble show a shape: a season of manoeuvring, released in one room where everyone has to choose a side.

### Logan's death, and the end

In season 4, episode 3 ("Connor's Wedding"), Logan dies on a plane while his children are at their brother's wedding. We never see him die; we hear it through phones, at second hand. Armstrong said in HBO's behind-the-scenes video that leaving out a death scene was deliberate: the episode is about the reactions. Killing the centre of the ensemble early turned the final season into a test of who each child was without him.

The series finale, "With Open Eyes" (2023), ends with the sale going through, Shiv voting against Kendall at the last moment, and Tom, the outsider, named CEO. Before the final season aired, Armstrong told *The New Yorker* (2023) that he'd never thought the show could go on forever, and that the title itself was a promise that needed keeping. After the finale, Jeremy Strong, who played Kendall, said in HBO's post-finale material, as reported by *The Hollywood Reporter*:

> It is a completely tragic ending from my perspective.
— Jeremy Strong, quoted in *The Hollywood Reporter* (2023)

## Use it

- **Stage:** An ensemble play without a hero can still have a spine: one want shared by everyone, pursued with different weapons (compare Chekhov in *The cast as a system*). End each act in one room where everyone has to choose.
- **Screen:** Write insults and deflections that hide a feeling, then check that an attentive viewer can read the feeling. For a series, plan the convergence: where will every scheme meet, and whose betrayal turns it?
- **Journalism:** Corporate and political stories are full of Roys: people who speak in status moves. Report the moves, not just the words. Who was made to wait, who sat where, who was mocked in front of whom? Those details show power more truly than quotes. But never sharpen what people said for comic effect.

## The pushback

- **Why watch awful people?** Some viewers and critics found four seasons of the ultra-rich behaving badly exhausting, and argued the show glamorised the world it mocked. Defenders reply that the show never asks us to admire the Roys, only to understand them, which is exactly what bothers some critics.
- **A real family?** The show is widely read as drawing on media dynasties like the Murdochs. Armstrong has said in interviews that he drew on several real families and scandals rather than one, and the Roys are fictional; treat claims about any single model with care.
- **The improvisation myth.** Much coverage credited *Succession*'s rhythm to improvisation. By Armstrong's own account, it was tightly scripted, with planned alternatives and a loose take at the end.

## Try it

Take a scene from your work-in-progress with three or more characters who want the same thing.

1. For each character, write in one line what they really feel in the scene.
2. Rewrite their dialogue so no one says that feeling. Each line must be an insult, a deflection or a status move.
3. Write two "alts" for the line you're least sure of: one funnier, one truer. Read all three aloud and keep the one that hits hardest.
4. End the scene with one character choosing a side.

- **Stage:** read the scene with actors, letting them try your alts in different runs.
- **Screen:** watch the "boar on the floor" scene first, and map who has power at the start and end of yours.
- **Journalism:** take notes from a meeting you covered and mark every status move you saw.

**Check:** Could a reader name what each character feels, though nobody says it?

## Check yourself

Q: What makes *Succession*'s ensemble unusual?
A: There's no hero. Every child wants the same thing, focus and sympathy rotate, and the question is who betrays whom, not who wins.

Q: How does Armstrong say the actors should play the comedy?
A: As real. The laughs come from characters taking themselves seriously, not from playing for jokes.

Q: What are "alts", and how do they fit the show's process?
A: Alternative lines written shortly before shooting for moments that could be funnier or truer; actors try them within a scripted scene, alongside a looser take at the end.

Q: How does each season end?
A: In a family occasion where the scattered schemes converge and a betrayal or reversal of power resolves them.

Q: Why does Logan die off screen?
A: Armstrong said it was deliberate: the episode is about the reactions, and his early death tests who each child is without him.
