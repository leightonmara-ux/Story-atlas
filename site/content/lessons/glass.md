## Who and when

Ira Glass is the host and creator of *This American Life*, the public radio program he launched in Chicago in 1995. Each episode takes a theme and tells several true stories about it, usually reported, sometimes personal, often funny and sad at once. The show helped shape modern audio storytelling, and much of today's narrative podcasting descends from it.

Glass has never written a book on story structure. His ideas reach most people through a short series of videos, usually titled *Ira Glass on Storytelling*, that circulated online from the mid-2000s and are still widely shared. In four short segments, talking straight to camera, he explains the building blocks of a radio story, how to find good stories, the frustration of being a beginner, and some common mistakes. The third segment, about the gap between taste and ability, has been remixed and quoted so often that it has a life of its own.

What follows sticks to the ideas he set out there and that are widely associated with the show.

## The big ideas

### Two building blocks

Glass says a broadcast story is built from two kinds of material, used in alternation.

1. **The anecdote:** a sequence of actions. Someone did this, and then this happened, and then this.
2. **The moment of reflection:** the point where someone, the narrator or a person in the story, stops and says what it means. Why are we hearing this? What does it tell us about people, or about the world?

Most stories on the show move back and forth: a stretch of anecdote, a moment of reflection, more anecdote, more reflection.

### Why sequence is so powerful

Glass's insight about the anecdote is that a sequence of actions has **momentum in itself**, almost regardless of content. "This happened, and then that happened" makes the listener feel they're on a train going somewhere. Even an ordinary sequence pulls people forward, because they assume it is leading to something.

This is a radio producer's version of an old observation, the one Aristotle made when he insisted that events should follow *from* each other (see *Aristotle*). Glass's twist is practical: you can feel the pull of sequence even before the events carry much meaning, so use it.

### Questions as bait

The second source of momentum is the **question**. A good anecdote constantly raises questions, implicitly or explicitly, and answers some of them while raising new ones. The listener stays to find out. Glass describes this as a kind of bait, laid all the way through the story.

Notice how close this is to the "open question" in *The shared core*. Glass just puts it at the level of the sentence and the minute, not only the whole story.

### Reflection gives the point

A story that's all anecdote is a pleasant ride with no destination. A story that's all reflection is a lecture. The moment of reflection is what makes the listener feel the story was *worth* hearing, and it's often the part they remember. Compare *Vivian Gornick*: the anecdote is close to her "situation", and the reflection is where the "story" lives.

### Find, and cut, ruthlessly

Glass is blunt that much of the work is finding stories good enough to tell. He describes how a lot of material gathered for the show never airs, because the anecdote is weak or there's no real moment of reflection. Killing your own material, he argues, is a large part of making something good. Not every interesting situation is a story.

### The gap

The most quoted part of the series is about beginners. People get into creative work, Glass says, because they have good **taste**. But for the first years, what they make isn't good enough to satisfy that taste, and they know it. That disappointment, the **gap** between what you can recognize as good and what you can produce, makes many people quit.

His advice is that the gap is normal, everyone who does good work went through it, and the only way through is volume: do a great deal of work, on deadlines, until your ability catches up with your taste.

It isn't a structural idea, but it's one of the most useful things any teacher of story has said to beginners, which is why it's here.

### Sound like yourself

Glass also warns against the stilted, formal "broadcaster" voice. On *This American Life*, the narrator talks like a person, admits confusion, and is often present in the story. He urges new producers not to imitate others, including him, but to find their own voice. Like the New Journalists (see *Tom Wolfe & the New Journalism*), he treats an honest personal voice as a tool of truth-telling, not a decoration.

## Use it

- **Journalism:** Glass's model works well beyond radio. When you draft a feature, mark each paragraph A (anecdote) or R (reflection). Long runs of one or the other are where readers drift. If there's no R anywhere, you don't yet know why the story matters.
- **Screen:** Documentaries often rely on exactly this alternation, scenes of action punctuated by interview moments of reflection. The question-as-bait idea is how good editors keep a film moving.
- **Stage:** A play's equivalent of the moment of reflection is the speech where a character steps back and says what something means. Too many and the play sermonizes; none and it may feel pointless. Solo performers and storytellers in live shows use Glass's alternation almost exactly.

## The pushback

- **Too narrow a format?** Glass's model fits a particular sound: intimate, wry, reflective. Critics have argued that the show's influence made a lot of audio storytelling sound alike, with the same rhythms of anecdote, music and epiphany.
- **Reflection can be imposed.** The demand for a moment of meaning can tempt producers to tack on a lesson the material doesn't support, a neat insight in place of a messy truth.
- **Momentum isn't everything.** Not all good stories are driven by "and then". Some reward slow attention or circular structure (see the *Counterpoints* track).
- **Truth under pressure.** In 2012 *This American Life* retracted an episode adapted from Mike Daisey's monologue about Apple's factories in China, after it emerged that parts were invented. The show devoted a full episode to the retraction. It's a sharp reminder of how much narrative journalism depends on trust (see *Janet Malcolm & John Hersey*).

## Try it

Take a draft feature, script or audio piece. (As a warm-up, listen to one *This American Life* story and notice where it switches.)

1. Mark each paragraph or passage A for anecdote (a sequence of actions) or R for reflection (someone saying what it means).
2. Find any long run of A or of R. Break it up: add a moment of reflection to a long anecdote, or turn some reflection back into action.
3. In each anecdote, underline the questions it raises. Where the sequence goes flat, add one.
4. If there's no R anywhere, write one, or ask honestly whether this material is a story at all.

- **Stage:** in a monologue, mark where the character steps back to say what it means. One or two such moments, not a sermon.
- **Screen:** in a documentary cut, alternate scenes of action with interview moments of reflection.
- **Journalism:** read the revised draft aloud as if for radio, and note where your own attention drops.

**Check:** Does the listener always have a question pulling them forward, and do they know why they're hearing this?

## Check yourself

Q: What are Glass's two building blocks of a story?
A: The anecdote, a sequence of actions, and the moment of reflection, where someone says what it means.

Q: Why does a sequence of actions hold attention?
A: It has momentum in itself: "this happened, then this" makes listeners feel they're heading somewhere, and good anecdotes keep raising questions they want answered.

Q: What goes wrong in a story that's all anecdote, or all reflection?
A: All anecdote is a ride with no point; all reflection is a lecture. The alternation gives both momentum and meaning.

Q: What is "the gap"?
A: The difference between a beginner's good taste and their ability to make work that meets it. Glass's advice is to keep producing a lot of work until the ability catches up.

Q: A reporter's draft is vivid but readers say "So what?" What would Glass suggest?
A: It probably lacks a moment of reflection. Add a point where someone steps back and says what the events mean, or cut it if no such meaning can be found.
