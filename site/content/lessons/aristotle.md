## Who and when

Aristotle wrote the *Poetics* in Athens around 335 BCE, roughly a century after the great tragedies of Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides. What survives is probably his lecture notes: short, compressed, sometimes cryptic. A second book, on comedy, is lost.

He isn't telling playwrights what to do so much as describing what worked in the plays he admired most, above all Sophocles' *Oedipus the King*. That distinction matters, because for two thousand years people treated his descriptions as laws.

## The big ideas

### Plot comes first

Aristotle ranks the six parts of tragedy: **plot**, character, thought, diction, song and spectacle. Plot (*mythos*, "the arrangement of the incidents") wins by a mile:

> The Plot, then, is the first principle, and, as it were, the soul of a tragedy: Character holds the second place.
> — Poetics, ch. 6 (trans. S. H. Butcher)

His reasoning: we learn who people are from what they do. A play can work with thin characters but never without action.

### A whole, with a beginning, middle and end

> A whole is that which has a beginning, a middle, and an end.
> — Poetics, ch. 7

It sounds obvious, but his definitions are precise. A **beginning** doesn't need anything before it to make sense. An **end** follows from what came before and needs nothing after it. A **middle** follows something and leads to something. If your opening needs a flashback to make sense, or your ending invites "and then what?", you haven't found the true beginning or end.

### One action, not one hero

A play should imitate a single, unified action, not everything that happened to one person. Aristotle mocks poets who wrote about Heracles' whole life, as if being about one man made it one story. The test: if you can remove or move a scene without the whole being disturbed, it wasn't really part of the whole.

### Because of, not after

The worst plots, he says, are *episodic*: one thing after another with no probability or necessity linking them. Events should follow from each other. The "therefore / but" rule from *The shared core* is a modern version of this.

### Reversal and recognition

Aristotle's two most powerful tools:

- **Reversal** (*peripeteia*): an action produces the opposite of what was intended. In *Oedipus*, a messenger arrives to cheer Oedipus and free him from fear, and by revealing who he is does the opposite.
- **Recognition** (*anagnorisis*): a change from ignorance to knowledge, ideally about who someone really is.

The best plots make them happen at the same moment, as in *Oedipus*. Add a scene of **suffering** (*pathos*) and you have the three parts of a "complex" plot.

### Tying and untying

Every tragedy has a **complication** (*desis*, the tying of the knot), running from the start up to the turn in fortune, and an **unraveling** (*lysis*), from the turn to the end. The unraveling should come out of the plot itself, not from a god lowered onto the stage by a crane, the *deus ex machina*. He singles out Euripides' *Medea* for this fault.

### Hamartia

The tragic hero falls not through wickedness but through *hamartia*, which is better translated as an **error** or **mistake** than the traditional "tragic flaw". Oedipus isn't punished for being a bad man. He acts in ignorance.

### Probable impossibilities

In Butcher's translation, "the poet should prefer probable impossibilities to improbable possibilities." An audience will accept a ghost or a god if the story makes it feel inevitable. It won't accept a coincidence that's merely possible but feels convenient. Every screenwriter who has been told "I know it really happened, but I don't believe it" is learning this lesson.

### Catharsis

Tragedy, through pity and fear, brings about the *catharsis* of those emotions. He never explains this clearly, and scholars still argue whether it means purging, purifying or clarifying.

## A common myth: the three unities

You'll often read that Aristotle demanded unity of action, time and place. He insisted only on unity of **action**. On time he simply observed that tragedy tries to stay "within a single revolution of the sun". He said nothing about place. The strict "three unities" were invented by Italian and French critics in the 1500s and 1600s, and then pinned on him.

## Use it

- **Stage:** The unified action and the pressure of time are why so many great plays happen over one night or one day: *Long Day's Journey into Night*, *Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf?*
- **Screen:** Reversal plus recognition is still the strongest kind of twist. Think of the end of *The Sixth Sense*: one discovery, and the meaning of everything before it reverses.
- **Journalism:** You can't invent a reversal, but you can choose where to reveal one. Structure the piece so the reader learns the turning fact at the moment it will reverse what they assumed.

## The pushback

- **Bertolt Brecht** built a "non-Aristotelian" theatre. He argued that drawing audiences into identification and catharsis makes them passive, when they should leave the theatre thinking critically.
- **Augusto Boal**, in *Theatre of the Oppressed* (1974), called Aristotle's model a "coercive system": tragedy purges the audience of the urge to challenge the social order.
- **Many modern writers reverse his ranking** and build from character, letting plot emerge from who people are.
- **It was built for Greek tragedy:** short plays, a single action, a chorus, an audience that already knew the myths.

## Try it

Take your work-in-progress, or *Oedipus the King*, and list its scenes, one line each.

1. For each scene, apply Aristotle's test: if I cut it or moved it, would the whole be disturbed? Mark every scene that could go without anyone noticing.
2. Find your main turn. Write what the key character intends to happen there, and what their action actually produces. If it isn't the opposite, look for a way it could be.
3. Ask who learns what at that moment. Could the reversal and the recognition land at the same instant?

- **Stage:** check your opening too. Does your first scene need nothing before it to make sense, or are you leaning on a long explanation?
- **Screen:** test your twist against *The Sixth Sense*: does one discovery reverse the meaning of what came before?
- **Journalism:** you can't invent a reversal, but you can find the fact in your notes that overturns the reader's assumption, and place it where it will do that.

**Check:** Does your turning point grow out of earlier events, or arrive like a god lowered on a crane?

## Check yourself

Q: Which of tragedy's six parts does Aristotle rank first, and why?
A: Plot, "the soul of a tragedy". We learn who people are from what they do, so action is the foundation and character comes second.

Q: What is a reversal (peripeteia)? Give Aristotle's example.
A: An action that produces the opposite of what was intended. In *Oedipus*, a messenger comes to relieve Oedipus's fears and, by revealing who he is, confirms the worst.

Q: Did Aristotle demand the three unities of action, time and place?
A: No. He required only unity of action. He noted that tragedy tends to keep within a day and said nothing about place. The three unities were codified by Renaissance and French neoclassical critics.

Q: Why is "tragic flaw" a misleading translation of hamartia?
A: The word means an error or mistake, often made in ignorance. It isn't necessarily a defect of character.

Q: Which does Aristotle prefer: a probable impossibility or an improbable possibility?
A: A probable impossibility. What matters is whether the audience believes it, not whether it could really happen.
