## Who and when

This lesson pairs a historian and an essayist who never worked together but arrived, in the same decade, at the same unsettling question: is the story in the facts, or do we put it there?

**Hayden White** (1928–2018) was an American historian and theorist who spent much of his career at the University of California, Santa Cruz. His ***Metahistory*** (1973) read the great nineteenth-century historians, Michelet, Ranke, Tocqueville and Burckhardt, not as neutral reporters of the past but as writers who had chosen literary forms for it. His essay "**The Value of Narrativity in the Representation of Reality**" (*Critical Inquiry*, 1980) pushed the argument further.

**Joan Didion** (1934–2021) was an American journalist, essayist and novelist, associated with the *New Journalism* of the 1960s and 70s. "**The White Album**" is the title essay of her 1979 collection, a fractured account of Los Angeles in the late 1960s, from the Manson murders to a Doors recording session, and of her own sense that events had stopped making sense.

## The big ideas

### White: history is emplotted

White's key term is **emplotment**: the way a historian turns a sequence of events into a story of a particular kind. The events don't come with a plot attached. The historian supplies one, usually without noticing.

He borrowed four basic plot types from the Canadian critic **Northrop Frye**'s *Anatomy of Criticism* (1957). The types are Frye's; applying them to history-writing is White's move.

| Mode | The shape it gives events |
|---|---|
| **Romance** | A hero triumphs over the world; good prevails. |
| **Tragedy** | A fall that brings understanding of the laws governing the world. |
| **Comedy** | Conflicts resolve into reconciliation and harmony. |
| **Satire** | Nobody masters the world; the story is ironic, without redemption. |

White read Michelet's history of France as romance, Tocqueville's as tragedy, Ranke's as comedy and Burckhardt's as satire. The same French Revolution could be told as the liberation of a people or as a tragic fall, and both could be scrupulously factual.

### The same facts, different stories

In "The Historical Text as Literary Artifact" (1974) White made the point directly: the choice of plot is not dictated by the evidence. The facts limit what you can say, but they don't tell you which kind of story you're in. That choice carries meaning, and often a politics.

### The value of narrativity

The 1980 essay looks at forms of record-keeping that *don't* tell stories. Medieval **annals** list years with an event or two beside some of them, and nothing beside others: a hard winter, a duke's death, a blank year. **Chronicles** go further but often just stop, without a conclusion. Full **narrative history** has a central subject, a beginning, a middle and an end, and above all closure.

White asks why we find the narrative version more real, when life doesn't arrive with endings. His answer: closure gives events a moral meaning. To end a story is to judge it. Narrative, he argues, is bound up with the urge to moralize reality, to make events mean something about how we should live.

### Didion: stories in order to live

"The White Album" opens with one of the most quoted sentences in American nonfiction:

> We tell ourselves stories in order to live.
> — Joan Didion, "The White Album" (1979)

She goes on to describe how we read meaning into what we see: we look for the sermon in a suicide, the lesson in a murder. We impose a narrative line on scattered images in order to bear them.

### The line is a warning

Here's the part often missed. The sentence is usually quoted as a celebration of storytelling, on posters and in commencement speeches. In the essay it's the prelude to a breakdown of that faith. Didion writes that during those years she began to doubt the premises of the stories she had always told herself. She includes her own psychiatric report from 1968. Then she lays out the period in fragments: news items, lists, scenes that refuse to add up.

The form of the essay is the argument. Didion, a reporter whose job was to find the story, shows the material resisting being made into one. She closes by admitting that writing hasn't yet helped her see what it all means.

### Together: testing journalism

Both writers challenge a phrase every journalist uses: *finding the story*. White suggests that the story is chosen, not found, and that the choice of plot carries a judgment. Didion shows what it feels like when the chosen plots stop working. Neither says facts don't matter; both say facts alone don't make a story.

## Use it

- **Journalism:** Before writing, name the mode you've chosen. Is this a romance (the underdog wins), a tragedy (a fall with a lesson), a comedy (the system works itself out) or a satire (nobody is in control)? Then ask whether the facts would support another mode just as well. If so, your choice is an editorial judgment, and you should own it. And Didion's form gives permission: when events don't cohere, a fragmented structure can be more honest than a forced arc.
- **Screen:** Documentary editors face White's problem directly. The same footage can be cut as triumph or as tragedy. Biopics, too, make a choice of mode that usually goes unstated.
- **Stage:** History plays and verbatim theatre emplot the record. Ask which of White's four modes your play adopts, and whether a different one would change its politics.

## The pushback

- **Relativism.** Historians such as Carlo Ginzburg argued that White blurred the line between history and fiction and risked making all accounts equally valid. White denied that facts are invented; his claim was about the stories made from them.
- **The limits of representation.** At a 1990 conference on Nazism and the Holocaust, published as *Probing the Limits of Representation* (ed. Saul Friedländer, 1992), scholars pressed White on whether some events can rightly be told in any mode at all. Could the Holocaust be emplotted as comedy? White's answer there was more qualified than in *Metahistory*.
- **Four boxes are few.** Frye's four types are tidy, and real histories often mix them.
- **Didion's detachment.** Some readers find "The White Album" more mood than argument, and her cool distance from the politics of the 1960s has been criticized. Her doubt about narrative didn't stop her writing narratives; her later books were deeply storied.

## Read next

- Joan Didion, *The White Album* (1979).

## Try it

Choose a real event you know well: a local election, a company collapse, a family move. Write four opening paragraphs for it, one in each of White's modes: romance, tragedy, comedy, satire. Use only true facts in all four. Then write a fifth version in Didion's manner: five short fragments, each a concrete detail, with no connecting explanation.

- **Stage:** Choose the mode for a history or verbatim play, and write the final stage image that mode demands.
- **Screen:** For a documentary or biopic, write the logline in two different modes. Which one is the film you're actually cutting?
- **Journalism:** Show your four openings to an editor or friend and ask which feels "true". Then ask what made it feel that way, since all four are factual.

**Check:** Can you say in one sentence why you chose this plot for these facts, and not another?

## Check yourself

Q: What does Hayden White mean by emplotment?
A: The way a historian gives events the shape of a particular kind of story. The events don't come with a plot; the writer chooses one.

Q: Where do White's four modes come from, and what are they?
A: From Northrop Frye's *Anatomy of Criticism* (1957): romance, tragedy, comedy and satire. White applied them to the writing of history.

Q: Why does White think narrative closure matters?
A: Closure gives events a moral meaning. Ending a story is a way of judging it, which is why narrative is tied to the urge to moralize reality.

Q: How is "We tell ourselves stories in order to live" often misread?
A: As a simple celebration of storytelling. In the essay it introduces Didion's doubt about the stories she'd told herself and a period that refused to make narrative sense.

Q: What do White and Didion both challenge in journalism?
A: The idea that a reporter simply finds the story in the facts. The plot is chosen, and that choice carries a judgment.
