## Who and when

Patrick Radden Keefe (born 1976) has been a staff writer at *The New Yorker* since 2006. His books grew out of that magazine work: *Say Nothing: A True Story of Murder and Memory in Northern Ireland* (2018), which won the National Book Critics Circle Award for nonfiction; *Empire of Pain: The Secret History of the Sackler Dynasty* (2021), about the family behind OxyContin; *Rogues* (2022), a collection of his magazine pieces; and *London Falling* (2026), about the death of a London teenager. He also made the podcast *Wind of Change* (2020).

He's here because his books are true stories that read like thrillers, and because he has been open about how he builds them.

## The big ideas

### Build the book around a question

*Say Nothing* is a history of the Troubles, thirty years of political violence in Northern Ireland. But it doesn't open with history. It opens in Belfast in December 1972, when a gang takes Jean McConville, a widowed mother of ten, from her flat in the Divis Flats while her children watch. She doesn't come back.

That disappearance is the book's engine. The reader wants to know what happened to her and who was responsible, and that want carries them through hundreds of pages of politics, prisons and peace talks they might never have chosen to read about. The history isn't decoration around the mystery. The mystery is the way into the history.

This is the open question of *The shared core*, and the bait *Ira Glass* describes, used at the scale of a whole book. Keefe did it again in *London Falling*, which turns on how and why a nineteen-year-old ended up in the Thames, and in *Wind of Change*, which chases a rumor that the CIA had a hand in the Scorpions' 1990 power ballad.

### Withhold, and pay it off

A thriller controls what the reader knows and when. Keefe does the same with true material. In *Say Nothing*, he holds back his own most explosive finding, his conclusion about who was among those who killed Jean McConville, until the final chapters. By then the reader has spent the book with the people involved, and the answer lands as a reckoning rather than a news item. A *PBS NewsHour* segment on the book described it as containing a plot twist that surprised its own author: the reporting produced the ending, and the structure was built to deliver it.

Withholding in nonfiction has a rule fiction doesn't: you can delay information, but you can't distort it. Keefe doesn't pretend the question is unanswerable and then spring a surprise. He tells the reader there's a question, keeps them company with it, and answers it as soon as the story has earned the answer.

*Wind of Change* shows the honest version of the opposite outcome. After eight episodes, the rumor is neither proved nor disproved, and the podcast says so. A mystery structure promises an investigation, not a solution.

### Several protagonists, decades apart

*Say Nothing* has no single hero. It follows Dolours Price, a young IRA volunteer; Brendan Hughes, an IRA commander; Gerry Adams, the Sinn Féin leader, who has always denied being a member of the IRA; and Jean McConville's children, who spent decades trying to find out what happened to their mother. The story runs from the late 1960s into the 2010s.

Keefe has described the book's structure as fairly lean, in an interview with *Rolling Stone*: a few lives, followed closely, carry the whole history. Each protagonist shows a different side of the same events: the believer who later doubts, the commander who feels betrayed, the politician who moves on, the family left behind. *Empire of Pain* uses the same principle across three generations of one family, from Arthur Sackler's career in medical advertising to the OxyContin era and the reckoning that followed. For how to weave several lives together, see *The braided narrative*.

### Find the archive that lets people speak

Many of Keefe's central figures were dead or unwilling to talk. Hughes died in 2008, Price in 2013; Keefe has said he started the book after reading her obituary. What let him write them from the inside was an oral history archive.

Between 2001 and 2006, the **Belfast Project**, run for Boston College by the journalist Ed Moloney, with former IRA prisoner Anthony McIntyre as interviewer, recorded former paramilitaries talking about the past. They were promised the tapes would stay sealed until their deaths. After Hughes died, his account was published in Moloney's *Voices from the Grave* (2010). Then, from 2011, police in Northern Ireland pursued the tapes through the American courts as evidence in the McConville case, and the promise of secrecy fell apart. The archive became both a source for the book and part of its story.

The lesson for any writer: look for the record people made when they thought no one would hear it for years, such as oral histories, court files, letters and depositions. They're often more candid than anything said to a reporter.

### Writing around the people who won't talk

The Sacklers declined to be interviewed for *Empire of Pain*. Gerry Adams didn't cooperate with *Say Nothing*. Keefe builds those figures from court records, documents, other people's memories and their own public words. In the preface to *Rogues* he writes:

> Some journalists hate writearounds, but I've always enjoyed the challenge they pose.
— Patrick Radden Keefe, *Rogues* (2022)

The craft lies in attribution. When accounts conflict, he says whose account is whose, notes denials where they matter (Adams's denials recur throughout *Say Nothing*), and keeps his sources in the endnotes rather than cluttering the narrative. The reader can follow the story like a novel and still check where each fact came from.

### Openings

His magazine pieces, like *Say Nothing*, usually start with a person in a specific moment, rather than a statement of the subject. The larger issue, whether drug cartels, wine fraud or the opioid crisis, arrives once you already care about someone. It's a long-form cousin of *The feature formula*: scene first, then the reason it matters.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Plays about real events, such as verbatim and documentary theatre, gain drive from a question the audience wants answered. Give the audience the mystery early, and let several characters each hold a piece of it. When a real person won't speak, put the conflicting accounts on stage side by side and let the audience see who says what.
- **Screen:** *Say Nothing* became an FX limited series in 2024, and its shape translates naturally: a cold open on the disappearance, episodes that shift among protagonists across decades, and a final reveal. For any fact-based script, ask what the audience is waiting to learn and when you'll tell them.
- **Journalism:** Find the open question inside your subject, and open on the person it happened to. Hunt for archives made in confidence: oral histories, court files, letters. When subjects won't talk, write around them, attribute every claim to its source, and print their denials.

## The pushback

- **Entertainment from tragedy.** A thriller structure makes real deaths into plot. Some readers find suspense about a real killing uncomfortable, especially when the victim's children are alive and reading.
- **An unproven allegation.** The reveal in *Say Nothing*, naming someone Keefe believes took part in the killing, rests on his reporting, not on a conviction, and the person named didn't respond to him. Critics have asked whether a narrative payoff justifies naming a living person.
- **The archive's broken promise.** The Belfast Project's interviewees spoke because they were promised secrecy. Using material that came out after that promise failed raises questions Keefe himself examines in the book, but they don't go away.
- **People versus systems.** Building *Empire of Pain* around a family gives it villains and momentum, but some reviewers noted that regulators, distributors and doctors share the blame. A story needs characters; a system doesn't always have a face.

## Try it

Take a true story you're working on, or one you'd like to tell.

1. Write the one question a reader will want answered. If you can't find one, you have a subject, not yet a story.
2. List what the reader needs to know, and mark the single most important fact. Decide the latest point you can honestly hold it back, and what the reader must learn first for it to land.
3. Pick two or three people who each see the events from a different side.
4. For anyone who won't talk to you, list the records that could speak for them: court files, letters, interviews they gave others.

- **Stage:** do it for a play about real events. Which character holds the answer, and in which scene does the audience get it?
- **Screen:** outline a limited series: which episode ends on the question, and which delivers the answer?
- **Journalism:** rewrite your opening as a scene with one person at the moment the question begins.

**Check:** Is your withholding honest, a delay rather than a distortion?

## Check yourself

Q: What role does Jean McConville's disappearance play in *Say Nothing*?
A: It's the book's engine: a question readers want answered, which carries them through a broad political history.

Q: What's the rule for withholding information in nonfiction?
A: You can delay a fact until the story has earned it, but you can't distort or hide what's true; the reader should know there's a question.

Q: Why use several protagonists across decades?
A: Each sees the same events from a different side, so a few closely followed lives can carry a whole history.

Q: What was the Belfast Project, and why did it matter to the book?
A: A Boston College oral history of former paramilitaries, recorded on a promise of secrecy until death. It let Keefe write people who had died, and its legal fight became part of the story.

Q: What's a writearound, and how does Keefe handle one?
A: A piece about someone who won't cooperate. He builds them from documents, others' memories and their public words, attributes each claim and prints their denials.
