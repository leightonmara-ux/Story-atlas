## Where it comes from

Two of the oldest ways to tell a story are to send someone down a road. They look alike, but they run on opposite engines.

The **quest** sends a hero after something: a treasure, a person, a cure, a way home. Homer's ***Odyssey*** (probably 8th or 7th century BCE) is the great voyage home: Odysseus spends ten years getting back to Ithaca after the Trojan War. The medieval **Grail romances** made the quest a spiritual search. Chrétien de Troyes left his *Perceval, the Story of the Grail* unfinished around 1190; Wolfram von Eschenbach's *Parzival* followed in the early 13th century; and Thomas Malory gathered the Arthurian quests into *Le Morte d'Arthur*, printed by Caxton in 1485. The mythic pattern beneath many quests is the subject of *Joseph Campbell & Christopher Vogler*, so this lesson won't repeat it.

The **picaresque** sends a rogue down the road with no particular destination. The name comes from the Spanish *pícaro*, a rogue or rascal. The founding book is the anonymous ***Lazarillo de Tormes*** (1554): a poor boy tells, in the first person, how he survived by serving a string of masters, a cruel blind beggar, a stingy priest, a proud but penniless squire and more, and outwitting each. Mateo Alemán's *Guzmán de Alfarache* (1599 and 1604) made it a fashion.

Then Miguel de Cervantes crossed the two. ***Don Quixote*** (Part I, 1605; Part II, 1615) is a parody of the quest: an old gentleman who has read too many chivalric romances rides out as a knight-errant. But the world he rides through is the picaresque one of inns, roads, convicts and con men. In Part I, Quixote frees a gang of galley slaves; one, Ginés de Pasamonte, boasts that he's writing his own life story, which he says will put *Lazarillo de Tormes* in the shade. Cervantes knew exactly which two traditions he was colliding.

## The big ideas

### The quest: a goal that pulls

A quest has a destination, so it has built-in momentum. Every episode is a step toward or away from the goal, and the audience always knows how far there is to go. Its typical shape:

- **A lack or a call:** something is missing, and someone must go.
- **The road of trials:** obstacles, guides, temptations, often in a rising order of difficulty.
- **The goal reached, and changed.** The best quests reveal that the object was not what mattered. Perceval, in Chrétien's version, fails at the Grail castle because he doesn't ask the question that would heal its wounded king. His quest becomes learning what to ask.

*The Odyssey* shows the quest's other great trick: Homer begins near the end, with Odysseus stuck on Calypso's island and his son searching for news. Most of the famous adventures, the Cyclops, Circe, the Sirens, are told later by Odysseus himself, as a story within the story. The destination is fixed; the order of telling is free.

### The picaresque: a hero who doesn't change

The pícaro is the opposite of a questing hero. He has no great goal, only survival. He is low-born, quick, hungry and usually narrates himself. The episodes are a tour of society, each master or town a new target for satire. And he doesn't grow much: the same wits get him into and out of each scrape.

That makes the picaresque a superb vehicle for satire and a weak one for suspense. It is, in *Aristotle*'s terms, **episodic**: events follow one another without probability or necessity. You could swap the order of many chapters and lose little.

### The problem of momentum

Every road story has to answer one question: why should we keep reading after the third episode? The great ones find extra engines:

- **A clock or a destination.** Even a loose road story improves when it's heading somewhere by a deadline.
- **A hidden question.** Henry Fielding, who subtitled his earlier *Joseph Andrews* (1742) as written in imitation of Cervantes, built ***Tom Jones*** (1749) as a road novel of inns and adventures, held together by the secret of Tom's parentage. Samuel Taylor Coleridge ranked it with *Oedipus Tyrannus* and Ben Jonson's *The Alchemist* among the most perfect plots ever planned.
- **A companion and a relationship.** Quixote and Sancho, Huck and Jim. The road changes, but the bond keeps developing.
- **A moral journey inside the physical one.** In Mark Twain's ***Adventures of Huckleberry Finn*** (1884–85), Huck floats down the Mississippi on a raft with Jim, an enslaved man escaping. The episodes are picaresque: a feud, a pair of con men called the King and the Duke. But underneath, Huck is deciding whether to turn Jim in. When he tears up the letter that would betray him, saying "All right, then, I'll go to hell," the book reaches a true climax. Many readers, Hemingway among them, felt the last chapters, where Tom Sawyer turns Jim's rescue into a game, lose that momentum.

### The road movie

Film made the picaresque modern, with cars. Frank Capra's ***It Happened One Night*** (1934) puts a runaway heiress (Claudette Colbert) and a reporter (Clark Gable) on a night bus from Miami to New York. The goal is simple, the episodes are comic set pieces (the hitchhiking scene, the blanket they hang between their beds and call "the walls of Jericho"), and the engine is a romance. ***Easy Rider*** (1969) sends two bikers across America to the New Orleans Mardi Gras and ends with both shot dead from a passing pickup truck: the road as a portrait of a country. In ***Thelma & Louise*** (1991), written by Callie Khouri, a weekend trip turns into flight after a killing, and the film ends with their car sailing over the edge of a canyon, frozen in mid-air. ***Little Miss Sunshine*** (2006) crams a family into a yellow Volkswagen bus with a broken clutch that they must push to start, racing to get a seven-year-old to a beauty pageant in time: destination, clock and companions all at once.

## Use it

- **Stage:** The road is hard to stage, so theatre often turns it into a chain of stations, rooms or encounters, as in Brecht's *Mother Courage*, where the wagon crosses years of war. See *Bertolt Brecht* on episodic structure used on purpose. Give each station a clear new pressure.
- **Screen:** Before writing a road movie, name the destination, the deadline and the relationship that will change. If you can't, you have a string of sketches.
- **Journalism:** The journey narrative follows a person, or an object, along a route. Sonia Nazario's *Enrique's Journey* (*Los Angeles Times*, 2002; Pulitzer Prize for Feature Writing, 2003) follows a Honduran boy riding freight trains north to find his mother in the United States; Nazario retraced much of his route herself. The destination supplies the momentum, the stops supply the reporting.

## The pushback

- **Episodic isn't a failure.** Aristotle disliked it, but many readers love the picaresque precisely for its freedom, variety and satire. Not every story needs a climax.
- **The quest's colonial baggage.** Many quests are stories of going somewhere and taking something. Critics have long noted how the "strange lands" of romance and adventure are other people's homes.
- **Whose road?** The road movie's freedom has mostly been a white man's freedom. *Thelma & Louise* was celebrated, and attacked, partly for showing what happens when two women claim it.
- **Fact on the road.** Travel journalism invites shaping. John Steinbeck's *Travels with Charley* (1962) was later shown by the journalist Bill Steigerwald to contain invented or embellished episodes. A journey's neat shape is a temptation.

## Try it
Take a road, journey or quest story in your work, or a road movie you know.
1. List the episodes in order, one line each.
2. Shuffle two of them in your head. If nothing breaks, you've found the episodic problem.
3. Add or sharpen one engine that runs underneath every episode: a destination with a deadline, a hidden question, a relationship that changes, or a moral choice that builds. Write one line per episode showing how it moves that engine.
4. Decide whether your protagonist is a quester (changed by the goal) or a pícaro (unchanged, sharpening our view of the world). Commit to one.

- **Stage:** Turn the road into stations, and give each a ticking pressure.
- **Screen:** Write the logline with the destination and the clock in it.
- **Journalism:** Map your subject's real route and choose the stops by what each reveals, not by how vivid it is.

**Check:** If you cut any middle episode, would the audience notice something missing from the engine, not just the scenery?

## Check yourself

Q: What's the key difference between a quest and a picaresque?
A: A quest has a goal that pulls the hero forward and usually changes them; a picaresque follows a rogue with no great goal, through episodes that satirize society, and he changes little.

Q: What is *Lazarillo de Tormes*, and why does it matter?
A: An anonymous Spanish novel of 1554, told by a poor boy who survives by serving a series of masters. It founded the picaresque.

Q: How does *Don Quixote* combine the two traditions?
A: It parodies the quest of chivalric romance, but sends its would-be knight through the low, episodic world of the picaresque.

Q: What's the main structural risk of a road story, and name two fixes.
A: Episodic drift: one thing after another, with no necessity. Fixes include a destination with a deadline, a hidden question (like Tom Jones's parentage), a developing relationship, or a moral choice that builds.

Q: What makes *Little Miss Sunshine* a well-engineered road movie?
A: It has a destination, a deadline and a family whose relationships change, so the episodes push toward a climax rather than just following one another.
