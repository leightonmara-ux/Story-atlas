## Where it comes from

The earliest films were single shots: a train arrives, workers leave a factory. Within a few years filmmakers discovered that joining shots could tell a story across time and space, and editing became the one tool that belongs to cinema alone. Three traditions grew up, and writers still use all three.

**Continuity editing**, worked out by American and European filmmakers in the 1900s and 1910s and standardized in Hollywood's studio era, aims to make cuts invisible so the audience follows the story without noticing the joins.

**Soviet montage**, from the 1920s, did the opposite. Building on *The face and the cut* (Lev Kuleshov's workshop), directors such as **Sergei Eisenstein** argued that meaning comes from the collision between shots, and that the cut should be felt.

**The modern editor's craft** has been put into words most memorably by **Walter Murch**, editor and sound designer of *The Conversation* (1974) and *Apocalypse Now* (1979), who won Oscars for both the editing and the sound of *The English Patient* (1996). His short book ***In the Blink of an Eye*** (1995; revised 2001) is the most widely read book by a working editor.

## The big ideas

### Continuity: the invisible cut

Continuity editing is a set of conventions for keeping the audience oriented. A wide **establishing shot** shows the space; closer shots follow; a **shot/reverse shot** pattern alternates between two people talking; the eyeline match (see *The face and the cut*) links a look to its object; and a **match on action** cuts in the middle of a movement, a door opening or a glass raised, so the motion carries us across the cut.

The backbone is the **180-degree rule**. Imagine a line running between two characters. If the camera stays on one side of it, one character always faces right and the other left, and the audience never loses track of who is where. Cross the line without preparation and they appear to swap places. Filmmakers break the rule on purpose to disorient, but it's a deliberate choice.

### Cross-cutting

**Cross-cutting** (or **parallel editing**) alternates between two or more lines of action, usually happening at the same time in different places. It's the engine of the last-minute rescue: the victim, the threat, the rescuers racing to arrive.

It's usually credited to **D. W. Griffith**, but he didn't invent it. Earlier films, such as Pathé's *A Narrow Escape* (1908), already cut between a threatened family and a rescuer, and claims for even earlier examples are disputed. Griffith developed and popularized it, from *The Lonely Villa* (1909) to *Intolerance* (1916), which cuts between four stories set centuries apart. Eisenstein, in his essay "Dickens, Griffith, and the Film Today" (1944), traced the technique back to Dickens's novels.

Two famous uses show its range. Near the end of *The Godfather* (1972), Michael stands at his nephew's christening, renouncing Satan, while the film cuts to his rivals being murdered on his orders: cross-cutting as irony. In *The Silence of the Lambs* (1991), the film cuts between an FBI team surrounding a house and a doorbell ringing inside the killer's house, letting us assume they're the same house until the door opens on Clarice, alone: cross-cutting as misdirection.

### Collision: Soviet montage

Eisenstein's famous disagreement with his colleague **Vsevolod Pudovkin** is worth knowing. Pudovkin saw shots as bricks *linked* in a chain to build a scene. Eisenstein, in essays written around 1929, insisted that shots should *collide*, and that the clash of two images produces an idea neither contains, much as, he argued, Japanese and Chinese characters combine two signs (eye and water, say) into a new meaning (weep).

*Battleship Potemkin* (1925) is the textbook. In the Odessa Steps sequence, in the fourth of the film's five parts, soldiers march down a long flight of steps firing on civilians; Eisenstein cuts between boots, faces, a mother carrying her dead son, and a baby carriage rolling down the steps, stretching a short descent into minutes. Straight after, three shots of stone lions, one sleeping, one waking, one rising, are cut together so that a statue seems to roar. (The massacre on the steps is Eisenstein's staging; there was real violence in Odessa in 1905, but not this scene.)

### Match cuts and jump cuts

A **match cut** joins two shots through a shared shape, movement or sound, so the cut itself makes a connection. The most famous comes at the end of the prehistoric opening of Stanley Kubrick's *2001: A Space Odyssey* (1968): an ape-man throws a bone into the air, and as it tumbles, the film cuts to a spacecraft orbiting Earth. Millions of years vanish in a single cut, and the tool becomes technology.

A **jump cut** removes a piece from the middle of a continuous shot, so the image skips forward. Jean-Luc Godard made it a signature in *Breathless* (*À bout de souffle*, 1960). In the best-known stretch, Michel drives and talks while the camera, behind Jean Seberg's Patricia in the passenger seat, keeps jumping forward as the streets change behind her. It's often said Godard cut this way to shorten a long film; whatever the reason, it announced that a cut could be visible, restless and modern. Today jump cuts are a native language of video essays and vlogs.

### The montage sequence

Hollywood uses "montage" in a different sense: a **montage sequence** compresses time into a series of short shots, often under music. The training sequence in *Rocky* (1976), ending with Rocky running up the steps of the Philadelphia Museum of Art, covers weeks in a few minutes. *Citizen Kane* (1941) does a marriage in a series of breakfasts, Kane and his wife growing more distant with each cut until they're reading rival newspapers in silence. A montage summarizes; use it for change you need the audience to accept, not to feel moment by moment (see *Story vs. telling* on duration).

### Murch's rule of six

Murch asks what makes the ideal cut, and answers with six criteria, ranked and given weights:

{{diagram:editing}}

1. **Emotion** (51%): is the cut true to what the audience should feel at this moment?
2. **Story** (23%): does it move the story forward?
3. **Rhythm** (10%): does it happen at the right moment, rhythmically?
4. **Eye-trace** (7%): does it respect where the viewer's eye is on the screen?
5. **Two-dimensional plane of the screen** (5%): screen direction, the 180-degree rule.
6. **Three-dimensional space** (4%): physical continuity of where things are.

The percentages are deliberately rough. The point is the ranking: if you must give something up, sacrifice from the bottom. A cut that breaks spatial continuity but gets the emotion right will work; a perfectly matched cut that feels wrong won't. Murch also noticed that people tend to blink at the moment they finish a thought, and suggested that a good cut falls where an attentive viewer would blink.

### Rhythm and pace

Editing is also music. Short shots and fast cutting raise tension; long shots let a moment breathe. Pace isn't speed but variation: a film that's fast all the time feels monotonous. Editors often talk about cutting to the moment an image has given everything it has, and not one frame later.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Theatre "edits" with blackouts, scene changes and simultaneous staging. Two scenes played side by side on a split stage, each catching fragments of the other, is cross-cutting without a camera.
- **Screen:** Write in cuts. Use INTERCUT for parallel action, end a scene on an image that rhymes with the next scene's first (a written match cut), and enter late and leave early. Rank your choices as Murch would: emotion first.
- **Journalism:** A feature is edited like a film: alternate scene and summary, cross-cut between two characters converging on the same event, and vary paragraph length to control pace. The podcast edit (see *Ira Glass*) works the same way: tape, then reflection, then tape; trim each quote to the moment it lands, and use music or silence as a cut.

## The pushback

- **Rules are habits.** The 180-degree rule and continuity system are conventions, not laws of perception; audiences adapt quickly to films that break them, from Godard to Wong Kar-wai.
- **Montage as manipulation.** Eisenstein's theory was explicitly about shaping an audience's ideas, and he made films for the Soviet state. The same power drives propaganda and misleading news edits.
- **Murch's numbers aren't science.** He offered the weights as a way of thinking, not measurements, and some editors argue eye-trace matters far more in fast-cut modern video.
- **The writer doesn't make the final cut.** Much of the rhythm is found in the editing room. Write for the cut, but expect it to be rewritten there.

## Try it
Take a scene of yours that feels slack, or one scene of a film you know well.
1. Break it into shots or beats, one line each, and mark where each cut falls.
2. For each cut, write one word for what the audience should feel at that moment (Murch's emotion). Cut any shot or beat that doesn't change that word or move the story.
3. Find one place to cross-cut to a second line of action that raises the stakes, and one scene ending that could match-cut into the next scene's opening image.

- **Stage:** Restage two consecutive scenes as one split scene, alternating lines between them.
- **Screen:** Rewrite the scene with an INTERCUT and end it on an image that rhymes with the next scene's first.
- **Journalism:** Re-sequence a feature draft so two threads alternate and converge; in audio, trim every clip to end on its strongest word.

**Check:** At each cut, can you name what the audience feels and what changed?

## Check yourself

Q: What is the 180-degree rule, and why does it matter?
A: The camera stays on one side of an imaginary line between characters, so their screen positions stay consistent and the audience never loses track of who is where.

Q: Did D. W. Griffith invent cross-cutting?
A: No. Earlier films, such as Pathé's *A Narrow Escape* (1908), already used it; Griffith developed and popularized it.

Q: How did Eisenstein's view of editing differ from Pudovkin's?
A: Pudovkin saw shots as linked building blocks; Eisenstein wanted shots to collide, producing an idea that neither shot contains alone.

Q: What's the difference between a match cut and a jump cut?
A: A match cut joins two shots through a shared shape or movement, as with the bone and spacecraft in *2001*; a jump cut removes part of a continuous shot, so time visibly skips, as in *Breathless*.

Q: What does Murch's rule of six say to sacrifice first?
A: Spatial continuity, from the bottom of the list. Emotion, at 51%, matters most, then story and rhythm.
