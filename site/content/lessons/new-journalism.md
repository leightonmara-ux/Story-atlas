## Who and when

In the 1960s a group of American magazine and newspaper writers began reporting real events with techniques that had belonged to novelists: scenes, dialogue, interior points of view, rich social detail. Their work appeared in places like *Esquire*, *New York* magazine (first as the Sunday supplement of the *New York Herald Tribune*) and *Rolling Stone*.

Tom Wolfe gave the movement its manifesto. In 1973 he and E. W. Johnson edited an anthology called *The New Journalism*, and Wolfe's long introduction explained what these writers were doing and why he thought it mattered. He argued, provocatively, that journalism had taken over the job the American novel had abandoned: describing how society actually lived.

Writers usually grouped under the label include **Gay Talese**, whose "Frank Sinatra Has a Cold" (*Esquire*, 1966) is a landmark; **Jimmy Breslin**, whose columns told big news through small people, most famously the man who dug President Kennedy's grave; **Joan Didion**, whose essays in *Slouching Towards Bethlehem* (1968) set a cool, personal standard; **Hunter S. Thompson**, whose "gonzo" pieces put the reporter at the center of the chaos; and Wolfe himself, with his exclamation marks and pop-art prose. Truman Capote's *In Cold Blood* (1966) and Norman Mailer's *The Armies of the Night* (1968) are its book-length cousins.

They were never a school with shared rules. Didion and Thompson, for example, write in the first person in ways Wolfe's model doesn't really describe. "New Journalism" is best read as a label for a moment.

## The big ideas

### Saturation reporting

Wolfe insisted the techniques depended on reporting of a new depth. To write a scene, you have to have been there, or have reconstructed it in great detail. Writers spent days, weeks or months with their subjects, waiting for things to happen in front of them. Wolfe called this **saturation reporting**. The style was the visible part; the reporting underneath was the real work.

### The four devices

Wolfe's central claim was that the power of realist fiction came mainly from four devices, and that a reporter could use all of them.

1. **Scene-by-scene construction.** Tell the story by moving from scene to scene, as a novelist or filmmaker would, rather than summarizing it in historical narrative. The reader watches events happen.
2. **Full dialogue.** Record conversation as fully as possible. Wolfe thought realistic dialogue involves the reader more completely than any other device, and it establishes character faster.
3. **Third-person point of view.** Present each scene through the eyes of a particular character, so the reader feels they are inside that person's head, experiencing events as they did. In journalism, Wolfe argued, this was possible if you interviewed people about their thoughts and feelings.
4. **Status details.** Record the everyday details that show a person's place in society, or the place they hope to have: clothes, furniture, manners, habits, how someone treats a waiter or a boss. To Wolfe, these details were the heart of realism, because people are always signaling and judging status.

Notice that only one of these, the scene, is mainly about *structure*. The others are about texture. But scene-by-scene construction alone changes everything: it turns a report into a sequence of dramatic units, and that brings with it all the questions about turning points and arcs explored elsewhere in this course.

### Fact with the force of fiction

The excitement Wolfe described was that readers would feel the absorption of a novel while knowing that *it all really happened*. Fiction's tools plus fact's authority. That double appeal is still the basic promise of narrative nonfiction, from *Jon Franklin* to long-form podcasts.

### The writer's voice

New Journalism also loosened the flat, anonymous voice of newspaper prose. Wolfe's own style was loud, playful and full of punctuation; Thompson's was manic; Didion's was precise and personal. The idea that a reporter could have a recognizable voice, and even appear in the story, became normal partly through these writers.

## Use it

- **Journalism:** Wolfe's four devices are a checklist for a narrative draft. Is this a scene or a summary? Have I got the actual words? Whose eyes are we seeing through? What details show status? Then check the reporting: can I stand behind every one?
- **Screen:** Documentaries and true-story films use the same devices. The status detail in particular is a screenwriter's tool: what a character wears, drives, and how they treat a waiter, tells an audience who they are faster than exposition.
- **Stage:** Verbatim and documentary theater are a close relation: real speech, arranged scene by scene. Wolfe's argument for full dialogue is the argument for that form.

## The pushback

- **It wasn't so new.** Critics pointed out that writers from Daniel Defoe and Charles Dickens to Mark Twain, and twentieth-century reporters such as John Hersey and Lillian Ross, had already used scene and dialogue in nonfiction. Wolfe's claim to a revolution was partly salesmanship.
- **Whose thoughts?** The third-person point of view lets a writer describe what someone was thinking. Even when based on interviews, reconstructed interior monologue is hard to verify, and readers often can't tell where reporting ends and imagining begins.
- **Composite characters.** Some writers of the period combined several real people into one figure without telling readers. A well-known case is Gail Sheehy's 1971 *New York* magazine piece about a prostitute, "Redpants", who turned out to be a composite. Such cases helped fix today's mainstream rule against undisclosed composites.
- **Accuracy and fairness.** Wolfe's own 1965 two-part piece mocking *The New Yorker* drew sharp criticism, including from Dwight Macdonald, who called this kind of writing "parajournalism", a hybrid that borrowed journalism's authority without its discipline.
- **Status obsession.** Some readers find Wolfe's eye for status details condescending: a way of sneering at people's taste rather than understanding them.

The debate these criticisms started is the subject of *Janet Malcolm & John Hersey*.

## Try it

Take a narrative draft of your own. (Reading the opening of Gay Talese's "Frank Sinatra Has a Cold" first is a good warm-up.)

1. Go through the draft with four colored pens, one for each of Wolfe's devices: scene, dialogue, point of view (whose eyes are we seeing through?) and status details.
2. Find a stretch of summary that covers an important moment, and rewrite it as a scene, using the words people actually said.
3. Add two status details: what someone wears or owns, or how they treat a waiter or a boss.
4. For every device you used, ask how you know. Cut anything you didn't witness, reconstruct from reporting, or hear from the person.

- **Stage:** take an interview transcript and arrange the real speech scene by scene, as verbatim theater does.
- **Screen:** introduce a character using only status details, with no exposition.
- **Journalism:** wherever you describe a character's thoughts, note which interview they told you in.

**Check:** Could you show your notes for every scene, line of dialogue and thought in the piece?

## Check yourself

Q: What are Wolfe's four devices?
A: Scene-by-scene construction, full dialogue, third-person point of view (seeing each scene through a particular character's eyes), and status details.

Q: What is a status detail?
A: An everyday detail, such as clothing, furniture, manners or how someone treats others, that reveals a person's social position or the position they aspire to.

Q: What did Wolfe mean by saturation reporting?
A: Staying with subjects long and closely enough to witness or fully reconstruct scenes, dialogue and thoughts. The devices depend on it.

Q: Why is the third-person point of view controversial in journalism?
A: It means describing what a real person was thinking. Even when based on interviews, it is hard to verify, and readers can't easily tell reporting from imagination.

Q: Was New Journalism really new?
A: Partly. Critics noted that earlier writers had used fiction's techniques for fact. What was new was the scale, the style and Wolfe's argument for it.
