## Where it comes from

People say they "identify with" a character as if that explained something. Film theorists in the 1970s and 1980s used **identification** as a big, loose word, often borrowed from psychoanalysis, for how films bind us to their characters.

**Murray Smith**, a British philosopher of film at the University of Kent, set out to replace it with something writers can actually use. His book ***Engaging Characters: Fiction, Emotion, and the Cinema*** (1995) breaks "identification" into three separate mechanisms that he calls the **structure of sympathy**: recognition, alignment and allegiance. A later essay, "Gangsters, Cannibals, Aesthetes, or Apparently Perverse Allegiances" (1999), tackled the puzzle of why we root for gangsters and killers.

Smith's tools sit alongside two older ideas you'll meet elsewhere in this course: Hitchcock's account of suspense (in *The shared core*) and Blake Snyder's "save the cat" (in *Blake Snyder* and *Making us care*).

## The big ideas

### Recognition

The first level is so basic we rarely notice it. **Recognition** is our construction of a character as a continuous, individual person: this face, this body, this voice belong to someone who persists from scene to scene. Films make it easy with faces, costume and actors we know. They can also disrupt it on purpose, for instance when two actresses play one character in Luis Buñuel's *That Obscure Object of Desire* (1977).

### Alignment

**Alignment** is about access: whom the story attaches us to, and how much of their inner life we get. Smith splits it in two:

- **Spatio-temporal attachment:** whom we follow. If the camera goes where a character goes, we share their journey and their timing.
- **Subjective access:** how much we know of what they think and feel, through point-of-view shots, voice-over, close-ups, music or a soliloquy.

Alignment is a matter of narration, not morality. A film can align us tightly with someone we despise. It's close to what literary critics call focalization (see *Point of view and voice*).

### Allegiance

**Allegiance** is moral evaluation. Based on what we've learned of a character's values and actions, we take a side: we want things to go well or badly for them. Sympathy and antipathy come in degrees, and they can shift.

The power of Smith's model is that these levels are separate dials. You can be aligned with someone without allegiance, or hold allegiance to someone you barely follow. Most of the interesting effects come from setting the dials against each other.

### Why we side with bad people

Many great stories ally us with criminals. Smith argued that most such allegiances aren't as perverse as they look, and filmmakers use a few reliable tools.

**Make the world around them worse.** Francis Ford Coppola's *The Godfather* (1972) aligns us with the Corleones from the opening wedding and gives Michael, a decorated war veteran who wants no part of the family business, the most access of all. Their enemies are uglier: a drug dealer who has Michael's father shot, a corrupt police captain who breaks Michael's jaw. By the time Michael kills both of them in the restaurant, we're with him. The film then tests that allegiance, cutting between a baptism where Michael stands as godfather and the murders he has ordered, and ending with a door closed on his wife.

**Plan a gradual slide.** Vince Gilligan has often described *Breaking Bad* (2008–2013) as turning Mr. Chips into Scarface. Walter White begins as an underpaid chemistry teacher with cancer and a family to provide for, so our allegiance is secure. Each season he does something worse, and the show keeps asking whether we'll stay with him. Many viewers did, long after they should have.

**Use alignment and suspense.** Hitchcock liked to point out that an audience will fear for almost anyone in danger of being caught. Show someone searching a room while the owner comes up the stairs, and we want to warn them. In *Psycho* (1960), after the shower murder, we're aligned with Norman Bates as he cleans the bathroom and pushes Marion's car into the swamp. The car stops sinking, half under; Norman freezes; and the audience, as Hitchcock himself remarked, pulls for it to go under. That's alignment and suspense producing a flicker of allegiance we'd rather not admit. (For suspense as information, see *The shared core*.)

**Fascination isn't sympathy.** Some characters hold us through charisma, wit or skill rather than moral approval: Hannibal Lecter, Richard III. Smith argues that what feels like perverse allegiance is often this kind of fascination.

### Not the same as saving the cat

Blake Snyder's "save the cat" (see *Blake Snyder*, and the wider toolkit in *Making us care*) is a recipe for allegiance: early on, let the hero do something that makes us like them. Smith's model shows why that's only one dial. You can hold an audience through alignment alone, by giving them steady access to a character's mind, or through fascination, with no cat in sight. And a cat saved in scene one can't guarantee allegiance in scene forty: allegiance is reassessed all the time.

## Use it

- **Stage:** The soliloquy is theatre's great alignment tool. Richard III opens his play by confiding his plans directly to us; Iago does the same in *Othello*. We know their minds better than anyone onstage does, which makes us complicit even as we judge them. Decide whose thoughts the audience will hear, and whose they won't.
- **Screen:** Chart your protagonist's three dials scene by scene. Where do you want allegiance to wobble? Plant the information there that makes it wobble. If you want us to stay with someone dark, either give us access to their reasons or surround them with worse people, and know which you're doing.
- **Journalism:** Every feature chooses whom to align the reader with: whose movements the story follows and whose thoughts it reports. Truman Capote's *In Cold Blood* (1966) gave readers deep access to the killers, especially Perry Smith, and changed how many saw them. Gene Weingarten's "Fatal Distraction" (*The Washington Post*, 2009) aligns readers with parents whose children died after being forgotten in hot cars, people readers are quick to condemn. With real people, alignment is an ethical choice (see *Janet Malcolm & John Hersey*).

## The pushback

- **Too cognitive?** Smith's model treats allegiance as judgment. Critics argue that much of our attachment is faster and bodily: we respond to faces, music and stars before we evaluate anything.
- **Truly perverse allegiances exist.** Some theorists think Smith explains away too much, and that audiences sometimes really do enjoy siding with cruelty.
- **The levels blur.** In practice, access often creates sympathy almost automatically, so alignment and allegiance can be hard to separate.
- **Stars complicate it.** Casting a beloved actor buys allegiance before a word is spoken, whatever the script does.

## Try it
Take your protagonist, or a morally difficult character from a film you know well.
1. List the first five scenes they appear in. For each, rate three dials from 0 to 5: alignment (how closely we follow them), access (how much of their mind we get), and allegiance (how much we want them to succeed).
2. Find the scene where allegiance is highest. What earned it: an action, information, or worse people nearby?
3. Write or plan one new scene that lowers allegiance while keeping alignment high: we stay with them as they do something we can't approve of.

- **Stage:** Give a difficult character a short soliloquy that reveals their reasons without excusing them.
- **Screen:** Restage a scene so the camera follows the antagonist instead. Whose side are we on now?
- **Journalism:** For a feature draft, note whose thoughts you report. Would aligning with a different person change the reader's verdict?

**Check:** Can you say, for each key scene, whether the audience is following, understanding or siding with your character, and which you intended?

## Check yourself

Q: What are the three levels of Murray Smith's structure of sympathy?
A: Recognition (seeing a character as a continuous individual), alignment (whom we follow and how much of their mind we access), and allegiance (our moral evaluation and taking of sides).

Q: What are the two parts of alignment?
A: Spatio-temporal attachment, which character the narration follows, and subjective access, how much we know of their thoughts and feelings.

Q: Why do audiences want Norman Bates's car to sink in *Psycho*?
A: We're aligned with him and in suspense about whether he'll be caught; alignment plus suspense produces a flash of allegiance we wouldn't endorse.

Q: Name two ways films get us to side with criminals.
A: Surround them with worse characters, as *The Godfather* does, and give us access to their reasons while their crimes escalate gradually, as *Breaking Bad* does.

Q: How does Smith's model differ from "save the cat"?
A: Save the cat is a single recipe for allegiance. Smith shows alignment and allegiance are separate, so you can hold an audience through access or fascination without likability.
