## Who and when

Anton Chekhov (1860–1904) trained and worked as a doctor while becoming one of the great short-story writers in any language. His reputation as a playwright rests on four late plays: *The Seagull* (1896), *Uncle Vanya* (published 1897, staged by the Moscow Art Theatre in 1899), *Three Sisters* (1901) and *The Cherry Orchard* (1904).

*The Seagull* famously failed at its St Petersburg premiere. Two years later the new Moscow Art Theatre, led by Konstantin Stanislavski and Vladimir Nemirovich-Danchenko, revived it with great success. The partnership made both names, though Chekhov and Stanislavski didn't always agree about how the plays should be done.

Chekhov never wrote a theory of drama. What we know of his ideas comes from the plays themselves, from his letters, and from friends' memories of his conversation.

## The big ideas

### Indirect action

In a well-made play (see *The well-made play*), the big events happen in front of you. In Chekhov, they mostly happen to the side. The critic David Magarshack called this **indirect action**: the decisive events take place offstage or between the acts, and what we watch is people living around them.

- In *The Seagull*, Konstantin's suicide is a shot heard offstage. The doctor tells the others it was a bottle of ether bursting, and then quietly asks someone to get the young man's mother away.
- In *Three Sisters*, Tuzenbach is killed in a duel we never see; a distant shot is heard, and then the news arrives.
- In *The Cherry Orchard*, the estate is auctioned in town during Act 3 while the family throws a party it can't afford. The news arrives when Lopakhin, the son of a former serf, announces he has bought it.

Things do happen in Chekhov: shootings, fires, duels, bankruptcy. The myth that "nothing happens" confuses where the events are placed with whether they exist.

### Subtext

Chekhov's people rarely say what they mean. They talk about the weather, the samovar, Balzac's wedding, and underneath, lives are breaking. Friends remembered him saying that on stage, as in life, people simply have dinner, and meanwhile their happiness is made or their lives are falling apart.

The word **subtext** came into wide use through the Moscow Art Theatre's work on his plays. For a writer, the lesson is that the scene's real action lies in what a character is trying to do with the words, not in the words themselves. In *Three Sisters*, Masha and Vershinin's love is carried partly by a wordless exchange of sounds, "tram-tam-tam", across the room.

### The ensemble

There's often no single protagonist. *Three Sisters* has three; *The Cherry Orchard* gives weight to a dozen people, from the landowner Ranevskaya to the old servant Firs. Each wants something; few get it. Structure comes from the patterns among them: who loves whom, who is waiting, who will leave.

### Arrivals and departures

Chekhov's four-act plays often follow a simple frame: someone arrives, disturbs a settled household, and leaves. *Uncle Vanya* begins with the professor and his young wife staying on the estate and ends with them driving away, leaving Vanya and Sonya to go back to the accounts. The change is real but small, and life resumes. That shape, rather than a climax and a new order, is Chekhov's most influential structural idea.

### The gun on the wall

"Chekhov's gun" is quoted everywhere, but in several versions. In a letter of 1889 to the writer Alexander Lazarev (who wrote as Gruzinsky), Chekhov says you shouldn't put a loaded rifle on stage if nobody means to fire it. A reminiscence by Ilia Gurlyand, published in 1904, gives the better-known version: a gun hung on the wall in the first act should go off in a later one. Other friends reported similar remarks. The point in each is economy: don't plant what you won't use.

Two cautions. First, he was talking about not wasting the audience's attention, not about twists. Second, his own practice is looser: critics such as Donald Rayfield point out that *The Cherry Orchard* contains firearms that are never fired.

### The impartial witness

In an 1888 letter to his publisher Alexei Suvorin, Chekhov argued that a writer should be an impartial witness to his characters, not their judge. That stance shapes the structure. No one is the villain; even Lopakhin, who cuts down the orchard, is generous and fond of the family. The audience is left to weigh things for itself.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Try putting your loudest event offstage and writing the scene around it: the waiting before, the news arriving, the people who have to keep pouring tea. The audience's imagination does the rest, and the actors get subtext to play.
- **Screen:** Ensemble dramas and slow-burn television owe a lot to Chekhov. Keep the gun principle for genre plotting, but notice how much power a scene gains when characters talk around the thing that matters.
- **Journalism:** Indirect action is often all a reporter can get. You weren't in the room when the decision was made, but you can write the people afterward. A family at the kitchen table after a verdict can carry more than the verdict.

## The pushback

- **Comedy or tragedy?** Chekhov subtitled *The Seagull* and *The Cherry Orchard* "a comedy". He complained that Stanislavski's productions made the plays slow and tearful. Directors still argue about the balance, and getting it wrong can make the plays seem static.
- **Hard to imitate.** Without a strong underlying situation, "indirect" writing can become plotless mood. Chekhov's plays are tightly built: money, property and love are always at stake.
- **The gun myth.** The popular version, "every detail must pay off", is a simplification of remarks he made several ways and didn't always follow himself.

## Try it

Choose the biggest event in your draft: a death, a sale, a confession.

1. Write a new scene set just beside it: the room where people wait, or the moment the news arrives. Keep the event itself offstage.
2. Let the characters talk about something else: the tea, the weather, a book. In the margin, write what each one is actually trying to do with their words.
3. Make sure no one is simply the villain. Give the person who brings the bad news one generous moment.

- **Stage:** frame the scene with an arrival or a departure, and let life resume, a little changed, when they go.
- **Screen:** keep the camera on the people in the next room. Cut away from the event, not to it.
- **Journalism:** write the aftermath you were able to report, using only what your sources saw and said, and let it carry the weight of the event you didn't witness.

**Check:** Could an actor play what your characters want without their ever saying it?

## Check yourself

Q: What is "indirect action"?
A: A structure in which the decisive events (a duel, a suicide, an auction) happen offstage or between acts, and the play shows the people living around them.

Q: Is it true that "nothing happens" in Chekhov?
A: No. Shootings, duels, fires and the sale of an estate all happen. They are placed offstage or reported, so the drama is in people's responses.

Q: What did Chekhov actually say about the gun?
A: Several versions exist: in an 1889 letter he said not to put a loaded rifle on stage if no one intends to fire it, and a friend later recalled him saying a gun hung in the first act should go off later. The point is economy of attention.

Q: Why did Chekhov object to some Moscow Art Theatre productions?
A: He thought of plays like *The Cherry Orchard* as comedies and felt the productions made them too slow and mournful.

Q: What simple shape frames several of his plays?
A: An arrival that disturbs a household and a departure that leaves it changed only a little, with life resuming.
