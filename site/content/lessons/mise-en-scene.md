## Where it comes from

**Mise-en-scène** is French for "putting on stage." It began as a theatre word in nineteenth-century France, where the *metteur en scène* arranged a production: the set, the lights, where the actors stood.

The word became a term of film criticism in the 1950s, through the young critics of the Paris magazine ***Cahiers du cinéma***, among them François Truffaut, Jean-Luc Godard and Éric Rohmer. They argued that a director could be an *auteur*, an artist, even on a studio script, and that you could see it in the mise-en-scène: how a scene was staged, lit and arranged.

The standard modern definition comes from David Bordwell and Kristin Thompson's textbook ***Film Art: An Introduction*** (first edition 1979). They group four things under mise-en-scène: **setting**, **costume and makeup**, **lighting**, and **staging** (how the performers move and act within the space). Everything in front of the camera. Some critics stretch the term to include framing; this lesson leaves the camera to *The camera's eye*.

For a writer the point is simple: before a line is spoken, the audience has taken in a room, a light, a colour and a coat, and started to feel something. You can write that in.

## The big ideas

### Light tells you how to feel

Film lighting usually balances a **key light** (the main source) against a **fill light** that softens its shadows. **High-key lighting** fills the frame, with soft, few shadows: open and safe, the look of musicals and sitcoms. **Low-key lighting** lets the key dominate, with deep shadows and strong contrast: hidden, dangerous or intimate.

Strong light-and-dark contrast is called **chiaroscuro**, Italian for "light-dark," a term from Renaissance and Baroque painting; Caravaggio is the painter most associated with it. Film inherited it through German Expressionism (*The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari*, 1920, painted shadows onto its sets) and then **film noir**, the name French critics gave the dark Hollywood crime films of the 1940s. Two moments to watch:

- **The Third Man** (1949). About an hour in, a cat curls around a man's shoes in a dark Vienna doorway. A light snaps on in a window across the street, and the face of Harry Lime, a man we believed dead, appears out of the shadow, smiling. The whole reveal is done with one light.
- **The Godfather** (1972). In the opening scene, Don Corleone listens to petitioners in a dim office while the wedding outside blazes in sunshine. The cinematographer Gordon Willis lit Marlon Brando from above, so his eyes often sink into shadow. We can't quite read him, and that's the point.

### Colour as a code

A **palette**, the limited set of colours a film keeps to, sets tone. A **colour motif** makes one colour carry a meaning that builds each time it returns.

- **Traffic** (2000) colour-codes its storylines: Mexico in hot yellow, Washington and Ohio in cold blue.
- In **Schindler's List** (1993), filmed almost entirely in black and white, a small girl in a red coat walks through the clearing of the Kraków ghetto. Later Schindler glimpses the red coat again, on a cart of bodies. One colour turns a crowd into a person.
- **The Sixth Sense** (1999) keeps red for things touched by the other world, which M. Night Shyamalan has described as its purpose: the red tent where Cole hides, the red doorknob on the basement door Malcolm can't seem to open. The code helps the twist play fair.

A colour motif is a setup and payoff in light (see *Chekhov*): plant it, repeat it, then cash it in.

### Setting as character

**Production design** is everything built or chosen for the frame: locations, sets, props. A good setting doesn't just locate a scene; it embodies the conflict.

In **Parasite** (2019), Bong Joon-ho builds class into architecture: the rich Parks live in a glass-walled house on a hill, the Kims in a semi-basement whose window looks up at the street. In the rainstorm, roughly two-thirds through, the Kims go down flight after flight of steps to find their home flooding with sewage. The geography is the argument.

### Costume says who, and how they've changed

Costume shows status, taste and inner state, and a change of costume can mark a change of self. In **The Devil Wears Prada** (2006), the editor Miranda Priestly traces her assistant's lumpy blue sweater back through the fashion industry's decisions, turning a costume into an argument about who chooses what we wear. (For costume as social signal in prose, compare Wolfe's status details in *Tom Wolfe & the New Journalism*.)

### Weather and the pathetic fallacy

Weather that matches feeling is the **pathetic fallacy**, coined by the critic John Ruskin in ***Modern Painters***, volume 3 (1856). "Pathetic" here means "of feeling." Ruskin's example was a novel that called sea foam "cruel": the foam isn't cruel, but grief makes the speaker see it so.

A common myth is that Ruskin was naming a technique to admire. He meant "fallacy" seriously: strong emotion produces a false picture of the world, and he ranked writers who saw truly *despite* their feelings above those who didn't. Today the phrase is used neutrally.

Often stronger is weather that means different things to different people: *Parasite*'s storm ruins the Kims' home, and the next day the Parks hold a garden party under a clear sky.

### Blocking within the frame

**Blocking**, a stage term, is where people stand and move. In film it includes depth: near and far, inside and outside, can say what dialogue doesn't.

About twenty minutes into **Citizen Kane** (1941), young Charles plays in the snow, seen through a window at the back of the frame, while in the foreground his mother signs the papers that send him away, out of his hearing. And at the end of *The Godfather*, Kay watches from the outer room as men pay homage to Michael in his office; then a door closes in her face.

## Use it

- **Screen:** You're not the director, but your scene description sets the first image. Name the light (one lamp, a TV's glow, noon glare), one colour, one telling object, and where people stand. Plan a colour or object motif across the script and pay it off.
- **Stage:** Mise-en-scène was a stage word first, and every idea here travels back; see *Mood on stage*.
- **Journalism:** Video and photo journalists make these choices daily, and a scene-setting lede chooses its light and weather too. Only use what was there; see *Mood in journalism and audio*.

## The pushback

- **The term is slippery.** Critics disagree about whether framing and camera belong inside mise-en-scène. Say what you mean when you use it.
- **Symbol-hunting.** Not every red object is a motif. Decoding every colour is the habit *Susan Sontag* warned against; often the mood is the meaning.
- **The auteur question.** Crediting the director with every choice ignores the designers and cinematographers who make them.
- **Sameness.** Digital colour grading pushes many films toward the same teal-and-orange look. Choose a palette for this story.
- **Cliché weather.** "It was a dark and stormy night," which opens Edward Bulwer-Lytton's *Paul Clifford* (1830), became a byword for overwriting. Matching weather to mood is the easiest choice.

## Try it
Take one scene from your draft, or the opening of a film you love, and fill in five lines.
1. **Light:** what's the source, and is it high-key or low-key?
2. **Colour:** what one colour dominates, and does it return later?
3. **Setting:** what in the place embodies the scene's conflict?
4. **Costume:** what does one garment say about who this person is now?
5. **Blocking:** who is near, who is far, who is in the doorway?

Rewrite the scene's opening description in three or four sentences so at least three of those choices carry the conflict before anyone speaks. Cut details that only decorate.

- **Stage:** Write it as the opening stage direction, as if for a lighting and set designer.
- **Screen:** Plant one colour or object here that you will pay off in a later scene.
- **Journalism:** For a scene-setting lede, list only details you observed, and check whether the weather is doing honest work or just mood.

**Check:** If the dialogue were deleted, could a viewer guess what the scene is about?

## Check yourself

Q: What four things do Bordwell and Thompson put under mise-en-scène?
A: Setting, costume and makeup, lighting, and staging: everything placed in front of the camera.

Q: What's the difference between high-key and low-key lighting?
A: High-key fills the frame with soft, even light and few shadows; low-key lets one main light dominate, with deep shadows and strong contrast, as in film noir.

Q: How is a colour motif different from a palette?
A: A palette is the film's overall set of colours, which sets tone; a motif is one colour given a meaning that builds each time it returns, like the red coat in *Schindler's List*.

Q: Who coined "pathetic fallacy," and did he mean it as praise?
A: John Ruskin, in *Modern Painters* volume 3 (1856). He meant it critically, as the false picture of nature that strong feeling produces; today it's used neutrally.

Q: In *Citizen Kane*, how does blocking tell the story when the mother signs the papers?
A: The boy plays in the snow far in the background while his future is signed away in the foreground, in the same frame and out of his hearing.
