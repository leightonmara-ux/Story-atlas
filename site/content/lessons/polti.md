## Who and when

**Georges Polti** (1867–1946) was a French writer and critic. In 1895 he published ***Les trente-six situations dramatiques*** (*The Thirty-Six Dramatic Situations*). An English translation by Lucile Ray followed in 1916 and has stayed in print ever since, mostly as a handbook for working writers.

Polti built the book on a claim he took from Goethe. In his conversations with his secretary Johann Peter Eckermann, Goethe remarked that the Venetian playwright **Carlo Gozzi** (1720–1806), author of fairy-tale plays such as *Turandot*, had maintained that there were only thirty-six tragic situations, and that Schiller had tried to find more but couldn't find even as many. Polti quotes the remark at the head of his book, with "tragic" changed to "dramatic".

So the famous number is thirdhand: Polti citing Goethe citing Gozzi. No list by Gozzi has ever turned up. Polti set out to rebuild one from the drama he knew, from the Greeks through Shakespeare to the French stage of his own day.

## The big ideas

### A situation, not a plot

Polti's unit is the **situation**: a configuration of people and forces that produces dramatic conflict. It isn't a whole plot with a beginning, middle and end. It's the charged arrangement at the heart of one: who is pitted against whom, and what's at risk.

Each of his thirty-six situations comes with a short list of **elements**, the roles it needs, and then a run of variants labelled A, B, C, with examples from plays, novels and myths. The roles are what make it practical. To write a Supplication you need three parties, not two.

### A representative sample

The full list is long and repetitive; these give its flavour. The glosses are plain-English summaries.

| No. | Situation | Elements | In plain terms |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Supplication** | A persecutor, a suppliant, and a power whose decision is in doubt | Someone begs a powerful figure for help against a threat |
| 2 | **Deliverance** | An unfortunate, a threatener, a rescuer | A rescuer arrives to save someone in danger |
| 3 | **Crime pursued by vengeance** | An avenger, a criminal | A wrong is answered by revenge |
| 5 | **Pursuit** | Punishment and a fugitive | Someone flees justice, or what they believe is injustice |
| 6 | **Disaster** | A vanquished power, a victorious enemy or a messenger | A defeat or catastrophe lands, often reported |
| 9 | **Daring enterprise** | A bold leader, an object, an adversary | A risky attempt to seize a prize or reach a goal |
| 11 | **The enigma** | A problem, an interrogator, a seeker | A riddle or mystery must be solved, often with life at stake |
| 16 | **Madness** | A madman, a victim | Someone loses their reason and harms another |
| 24 | **Rivalry of superior and inferior** | A superior rival, an inferior rival, an object | Unequal rivals compete for the same prize |
| 34 | **Remorse** | A culprit, a victim or the sin, an interrogator | A person is tormented by a past wrong |
| 35 | **Recovery of a lost one** | A seeker, the one found | A lost person, often a child or sibling, is found again |

Read down the whole list and you notice its centre of gravity: kinship, adultery, crimes of love and sacrifice. A large share of the thirty-six are about families tearing themselves apart (Enmity of kinsmen, Rivalry of kinsmen, Slaying of a kinsman unrecognized), which tells you what the stage of Greek tragedy and nineteenth-century melodrama was most interested in.

### A situation is a set of roles

The working insight is that a situation is defined by its roles, not by its subject. Supplication is the same engine whether the suppliant is a refugee before a king, a defendant before a jury, or a teenager asking a parent for money to escape a bully. Change who fills each role and the situation stays; change the number of roles and it becomes another situation.

That's why the list travels across media and centuries. In Hitchcock's *North by Northwest* (1959), Roger Thornhill, mistaken for a spy, is Pursuit: chased by the police for a murder he didn't commit and by the spies who framed him. Change the fugitive into a pursuer and you are in Vengeance.

### A prompt generator

Writers have used the list as a deck of cards ever since the English edition appeared. Some common uses:

- **Combine two.** Pick two situations and make them collide: Deliverance plus Remorse gives you a rescuer saving the person they once betrayed.
- **Recast the roles.** Keep the situation and swap who fills each role. A Supplication where the powerful figure is a child.
- **Assign one per character.** Give each major character their own situation and see where the lines cross.
- **Diagnose a stall.** If a scene has no tension, ask which situation it's meant to dramatize, and whether all the roles are actually present.

The impulse to systematize plot ran on. William Wallace Cook's *Plotto* (1928) went much further, offering a vast cross-referenced system of plot fragments that could be chained together. Polti's list remains the simpler and more widely used.

## Use it

- **Stage:** Plays are compact; one or two situations usually drive the whole evening. Name yours, check every role is onstage or felt offstage, and build the scene where the roles finally meet.
- **Screen:** For loglines and pitches, a clear situation is a clear hook. Genre films often run on one situation for the whole length: a Pursuit film, a Daring Enterprise heist. Know which, and give each element a face.
- **Journalism:** Real stories fall into these patterns too, and that's both useful and risky. A court story is often a Supplication; a disaster story a Disaster with a messenger. Use the roles to check whom you've interviewed: if your story is Supplication, have you heard from the power that will decide? Don't force real people into roles the facts don't support.

## The pushback

- **The number is borrowed, not proven.** Thirty-six comes from a passing remark by Goethe about Gozzi. Polti filled the count; he didn't discover it.
- **The categories overlap.** Many situations are variants of each other (several are adultery or crimes of love under different names), and some real plots fit three at once.
- **Situations, not structure.** Polti tells you what the conflict is, not how it develops or ends. For shape, you need another tool: *Aristotle*, *Christopher Booker*, or the arc in *The shared core*.
- **A period piece.** The examples and the emphasis come from classical and nineteenth-century European drama. Many stories that matter now, about work, systems, ordinary life or community, fit the list awkwardly or not at all.
- **Prompts make formula.** Used mechanically, the list produces mechanical stories. It's a starting point, not a substitute for knowing your characters.

## Read next

- Georges Polti, *The Thirty-Six Dramatic Situations*, trans. Lucile Ray (1916). Short and widely reprinted; the English text is in the public domain.
- Compare *Christopher Booker* for plots as shapes, and *Vladimir Propp* for an earlier catalogue of story functions.

## Try it
Take your work-in-progress, or a play or film you know well.
1. Name the situation at its core from Polti's list. Write out the roles it requires and who fills each.
2. Find any role that's empty or offstage too long, and write a scene that puts it in the room.
3. Now draw a second situation at random (number 1 to 36) and collide it with the first in one new scene. Keep it if it raises the stakes.

- **Stage:** Choose the moment all the roles of your central situation first share the stage. Is it the play's strongest scene?
- **Screen:** Rewrite your logline so each element of the situation is a named character.
- **Journalism:** List the roles in your story's situation and mark which you've interviewed. Who is missing?

**Check:** Can you name every role in your central situation, and does each one have a face?

## Check yourself

Q: Where does Polti's number thirty-six come from?
A: From a remark Goethe made to Eckermann, that Carlo Gozzi had held there were only thirty-six tragic situations. No list by Gozzi survives; Polti built his own.

Q: What is a "situation", in Polti's sense?
A: A configuration of roles and forces that creates dramatic conflict, such as a persecutor, a suppliant and a power whose decision is in doubt. It's the core of a plot, not the whole shape.

Q: What three roles does Supplication need?
A: A persecutor, a suppliant, and a power in authority whose decision is uncertain.

Q: How do writers use the list as a prompt generator?
A: By combining two situations, recasting who fills each role, assigning a situation to each character, or checking which situation a stalled scene is meant to dramatize.

Q: What's the biggest limit of Polti's list?
A: It describes conflicts, not structure: it says nothing about how a story develops or ends, and its categories overlap and reflect the drama of its time.
