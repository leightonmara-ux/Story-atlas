## Who and when

David Mamet made his name as a playwright in Chicago and New York in the 1970s, with *Sexual Perversity in Chicago* and *American Buffalo*, and won the Pulitzer Prize for Drama for *Glengarry Glen Ross* in 1984. He moved into film as a screenwriter (*The Verdict*, *The Untouchables*) and then as a director, beginning with *House of Games* (1987).

*On Directing Film* (1991) grew out of classes he taught at Columbia University's film school. It's a short, combative book, mostly a transcript of Mamet working through scenes with students. Its subject is directing, but nearly everything in it is about structure.

Mamet's second key text isn't a book. In 2005, while running the CBS military drama *The Unit*, he wrote a long memo to the show's writers, dated October 2005 and typed largely in capital letters. It leaked and circulated widely online around 2010, and it's now one of the most passed-around documents in screenwriting.

## The big ideas

### Tell the story in the cut

Mamet's theory of film begins with Sergei Eisenstein, the Soviet director who argued that meaning in cinema comes from **montage**: putting one shot next to another. Show a face, then a bowl of soup, and the audience sees hunger. Neither shot says "hunger" on its own.

So Mamet tells students to build scenes from **uninflected images**: simple shots that each do one plain thing, and that tell the story only when cut together. A man looks at his watch. A train pulls out. A door closes. The audience makes the connection, and making it is what holds them.

The alternative he attacks is what he calls following the actor around with a camera: shooting a scene as coverage and hoping the meaning emerges in the edit. For Mamet, the director should know what each shot is *for* before the camera rolls.

### Every scene has an objective

Mamet studied acting at New York's Neighborhood Playhouse, and he brings the actor's central question to film: **what does the protagonist want?** Not in general, but in this scene. The scene is over when they get it, or definitively fail.

Then he applies the same question at smaller scales. What is this sequence for? This shot? If you can't say, in a few plain words, what each unit accomplishes, it probably doesn't belong in the film. Like *Aristotle*, he wants every part to serve one action.

### Cut the exposition

Mamet is merciless about information. Audiences don't want to be told things; they want to find out what happens next. Backstory, explanations, characters describing their feelings to each other: all of it, for Mamet, is the writer doing the audience's work for them and boring them in the process.

The *Unit* memo puts it in one line:

> The audience will not tune in to watch information.
> — David Mamet, memo to the writers of *The Unit* (2005)

If the audience needs to know something, find a way for it to come out under pressure, as part of a character's attempt to get what they want.

### Three questions for every scene

The memo's best-known passage gives writers three questions to ask of any scene:

1. **Who wants what?**
2. **What happens if they don't get it?**
3. **Why now?**

The first is the objective. The second is the stakes. The third is the one most writers forget: why does this scene happen today, rather than last week or never? A scene that could happen at any time has no urgency.

The memo also insists that every scene must both advance the plot and work dramatically on its own. If it does only one, it's superfluous or written wrong. And it offers a test: imagine the characters couldn't speak. Could the scene still play as a silent film? If not, it may be relying on talk to do the work action should do.

### Character is action

Throughout his writing on drama and acting, Mamet is skeptical of "character" as a set of traits or a backstory. What we call character, he argues, is just what people habitually *do*. An audience knows someone only by their actions toward a goal. This puts him close to Aristotle's ranking of plot over character, and in a long-running argument with acting teachers who build performances from emotional memory.

## Use it

- **Screen:** Run the three questions over every scene in your outline. Then try the silent-film test on your talkiest scene. Directors can use Mamet's shot discipline in planning: write the shot list as a series of plain actions, each with a purpose.
- **Stage:** Mamet's own plays are almost all talk, but the talk is always *action*: characters selling, bullying, lying, pleading. "Who wants what?" is the oldest rehearsal-room question there is. The "why now?" question is especially useful for plays set in one room on one night.
- **Journalism:** "Why now?" is also every editor's question about a story. The exposition rule translates too: let background arrive when the reader needs it to understand a moment, not as a block up front.

## The pushback

- **Do as I say?** Mamet's films and plays are famous for their dialogue, the clipped, overlapping rhythm critics call "Mamet-speak". Some find it odd that the great talker preaches silent-film storytelling. Defenders answer that his dialogue is always a form of action, which is exactly his point.
- **Images are never uninflected.** Film theorists note that every choice of framing, lens and light carries meaning. The "uninflected image" is better read as a discipline than a literal possibility.
- **One school among many.** Directors who build meaning through the long take, from Max Ophüls to Alfonso Cuarón, show that cutting isn't the only way to tell a film story.
- **The tone.** Mamet is dogmatic and often contemptuous of other approaches. Many readers find his rules bracing; others find them narrow.

## Read next

- David Mamet, *On Directing Film* (1991). Short enough to read in an evening.
- David Mamet, *Three Uses of the Knife: On the Nature and Purpose of Drama* (1998), his broader theory of drama.
- The *Unit* memo is easy to find online. Read it whole, and note how little of it is about television specifically.

## Try it

Take your talkiest scene.

1. Answer Mamet's three questions in a line each: who wants what? What happens if they don't get it? Why now? If "why now?" has no answer, add a deadline or an event that makes today the day.
2. Rewrite the scene as a silent film: a numbered list of plain, uninflected images, each doing one thing. Does the story survive without words?
3. Find every line of exposition. Move each one to a moment where a character uses it to get what they want, or cut it.

- **Stage:** keep the words but check that each line is an action. Note in the margin what each speech does to the other person: sells, bullies, pleads, lies.
- **Screen:** turn your step 2 list into a shot list, and write beside each shot what it's for.
- **Journalism:** answer "why now?" in your top few paragraphs, and break up any block of background so it arrives when the reader needs it.

**Check:** Could someone watching with the sound off tell who wants what?

## Check yourself

Q: What does Mamet mean by telling the story through "uninflected images"?
A: Using simple shots that each show one plain thing, so the meaning arises from the juxtaposition of shots in the cut, as in Eisenstein's montage.

Q: What are the three questions from the *Unit* memo?
A: Who wants what? What happens if they don't get it? Why now?

Q: Why does Mamet object to exposition?
A: Audiences want to find out what happens next, not receive information. Background should emerge under pressure, as part of a character's pursuit of a goal.

Q: What test does the memo propose for a talky scene?
A: Imagine the characters couldn't speak. If the scene couldn't play as a silent film, it may be leaning on talk instead of action.

Q: How does Mamet's view of character echo Aristotle?
A: Both rank action above character. For Mamet, character is simply what people habitually do in pursuit of what they want.
